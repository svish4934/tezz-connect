Latency, ping and jitter explained

A connection can have high download speed and still feel unresponsive. Latency and jitter help explain why.

What is latency?

Latency is the time it takes for information to travel between two points and for a response to come back. It is usually shown in milliseconds (ms). You can think of it as the waiting time between asking a question online and getting a reply.

Lower latency usually makes online activities feel more responsive. It matters when you play a game, make a video call, use a remote desktop or click through a website. The distance to the server, network congestion, Wi-Fi quality and the route your data takes can all affect latency.

What does ping mean?

Ping is a common way to check how quickly a destination responds. A traditional ping tool often sends a small ICMP message and measures the round trip. Some websites and browser tools instead measure how long an HTTP request takes to get a response from a web server.

Those are not the same thing. They use different methods and may travel through different parts of the network. So, when you compare two results, make sure you know what each tool is measuring.

What is jitter?

Jitter means that the delay keeps changing instead of staying fairly steady. Imagine talking to someone on a call: one sentence arrives quickly, the next is delayed, and the next comes in a burst. That uneven timing can make a call or game feel choppy.

Different tools calculate jitter in different ways. A browser timing estimate is not the same as a full measurement of packet delay variation across your network. Compare results from the same tool and method when looking for changes.

Why do these numbers matter?

  • Online games: your action may take longer to reach the game server and for the update to come back.
  • Video and voice calls: changing delays can make conversations feel uneven or interrupted.
  • Remote work: a slow response can make remote desktops and shared tools feel frustrating.
  • Browsing: pages can feel sluggish if many small requests take a long time to get a response.

What can you do if response times are high?

  1. Try an Ethernet cable if you can. It helps you check whether Wi-Fi is part of the problem.
  2. If you use Wi-Fi, move closer to the router and avoid thick walls or large obstacles where possible.
  3. Pause large downloads, uploads and cloud backups while testing.
  4. Run tests at different times. If delays rise only during busy hours, congestion may be involved.
  5. Try more than one destination. A slow response from one server does not prove that every internet route is slow.
  6. Restart your router only if needed, and check the manufacturer's guidance for firmware updates.
