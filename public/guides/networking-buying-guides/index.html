INTERNET KNOWLEDGE HUB · BUYING GUIDES

Choose networking equipment with confidence.

Understand what routers, mesh Wi-Fi systems, range extenders, Ethernet cables and network switches do—and which specifications matter before spending money.

An educational buying guide · Reviewed for clarity, not a product test

  • Before you buy

Start with the problem you need to solve. A faster router will not automatically fix a slow internet plan, a congested provider network or a weak signal caused by poor placement. First identify whether the issue is coverage, wireless capacity, wired connectivity or the internet service itself.

1. Wi-Fi routers

A router connects your home network to your internet connection and commonly provides Wi-Fi. When comparing routers, check the Wi-Fi generation, supported frequency bands, Ethernet port speeds, coverage guidance, security update policy and compatibility with your internet provider's modem or fibre ONT.

  • Choose a model that supports the devices you already own; a newer Wi-Fi standard does not make older devices support it.
  • Check whether the WAN port can handle your subscribed internet speed.
  • Look for current security support and a clear firmware update policy.
  • Do not treat a manufacturer's maximum advertised wireless rate as the speed a single device will achieve.

2. Mesh Wi-Fi systems

Mesh systems use multiple coordinated nodes to extend wireless coverage across a home. They can help in larger or multi-room layouts where one router cannot provide a reliable signal everywhere.

  • Check how many nodes are included and whether additional nodes are compatible.
  • Look for Ethernet backhaul support if you can wire the nodes together.
  • Consider where each node can be placed; a mesh node still needs a usable connection to the rest of the system.
  • Compare the total system cost, not only the price of the main unit.

3. Wi-Fi range extenders

A range extender repeats or relays a Wi-Fi connection to reach an area with weak coverage. It may be a lower-cost option for a small dead zone, but performance depends on the signal it receives from the router and the design of the extender.

  • Place it where the router signal is still reasonably strong—not inside the dead zone.
  • Check whether it uses a dedicated backhaul radio or shares airtime with client devices.
  • Confirm compatibility with your router's Wi-Fi bands and security settings.
  • For several dead zones or a larger home, compare the total cost with a mesh system.

4. Ethernet cables and adapters

Ethernet provides a wired connection that can reduce wireless interference and improve consistency. For most home users, a correctly installed Cat 5e or Cat 6 cable is sufficient for common gigabit networking distances. The actual link speed is limited by the slowest port or device in the path.

  • Match cable length to the route and avoid unnecessary joins.
  • Check that the computer, router and any adapter support the speed you want.
  • For longer or permanent installations, consider cable quality, installation environment and local electrical/building guidance.

5. Network switches

A network switch adds wired Ethernet ports so several devices can connect to the same local network. Check the number and speed of ports, whether the switch is managed or unmanaged, and whether Power over Ethernet (PoE) is required for devices such as compatible access points or cameras.

A practical checklist before you buy

  1. Identify the actual problem: weak coverage, slow wired speed, too few ports or an outdated device.
  2. Check your internet plan and the speed capability of your modem or fibre ONT.
  3. List the devices you need to connect and the Wi-Fi standards or Ethernet speeds they support.
  4. Consider your floor plan, wall materials, node placement and whether Ethernet cabling is possible.
  5. Review security update commitments, warranty, return policy and total cost.
  6. Compare independent tests where available; separate measured throughput from manufacturer link-rate claims.
