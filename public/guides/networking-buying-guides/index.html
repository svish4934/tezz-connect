Choose networking equipment with confidence.

Understand what routers, mesh Wi-Fi systems, range extenders, Ethernet cables and network switches do—and which specifications matter before spending money.

Before buying anything, work out what problem you are trying to fix. Is Wi-Fi weak in one room? Do you need more wired ports? Is an old router struggling with newer devices? Or is the internet slow even when you connect directly to the router?

A new router cannot fix every internet problem. If your provider's network is congested or your plan is limited, new Wi-Fi equipment may make little difference. Start with the problem, then choose the equipment.

INTERACTIVE BUYING ASSISTANT

Which networking equipment do you actually need?

Answer three quick questions. The guide will suggest a sensible starting point—not a product brand or guaranteed performance result.

YOUR STARTING POINT

    1. Wi-Fi routers

    A router connects your home devices to your internet connection and usually creates your Wi-Fi network. When comparing routers, look at the Wi-Fi generation, supported bands, Ethernet port speeds, security updates and whether it works with your provider's modem or fibre ONT.

    • Check that your phone, laptop and other devices can use the Wi-Fi features you are paying for.
    • Make sure the router's internet (WAN) port can handle your subscribed plan speed.
    • Look for a manufacturer that provides security and software updates.
    • Be careful with big speed numbers on the box. They are not a promise of the speed one device will get in your room.

    2. Mesh Wi-Fi systems

    A mesh system uses two or more units to spread Wi-Fi around a home. One unit connects to your internet service, while the others help cover rooms farther away. It can be useful in a larger home or where one router cannot reach every room well.

    • Check how many units are included and how much extra units cost.
    • Place each unit where it can still get a good connection from the rest of the system.
    • If you can connect the units with Ethernet, check whether the system supports wired backhaul.
    • Compare the cost of the full system, not just the main unit.

    3. Wi-Fi range extenders

    An extender picks up your existing Wi-Fi signal and sends it farther. It can help with a small weak area, but it needs a decent signal from your router to start with. Putting it in the room where Wi-Fi barely works may not help.

    • Place it somewhere between the router and the weak-signal area, where the original signal is still usable.
    • Check which Wi-Fi bands and security settings it supports.
    • Some extenders share wireless capacity between the router and your devices, which can affect performance.
    • If you need coverage in many rooms, compare the cost and performance with a mesh system.

    4. Ethernet cables and adapters

    An Ethernet cable connects a device directly to your router or network switch. It is often a good choice for desktop computers, TVs and gaming consoles because it avoids many Wi-Fi signal problems.

    For many home networks, a good-quality Cat 5e or Cat 6 cable is enough for gigabit connections over typical home cable runs. Your actual speed still depends on every part of the connection: the router port, cable, adapter and device.

    • Check that your device has an Ethernet port or that your adapter supports the speed you need.
    • Choose a cable long enough for the route without creating a trip hazard.
    • For permanent or outdoor cable runs, use suitable cable and follow local installation guidance.

    5. Network switches

    A network switch gives you more Ethernet ports. For example, if your router has only a few ports but you want to connect a TV, computer, console and other wired devices, a switch can add more connections.

    Check how many ports you need and whether they support the speed of your devices. Some switches are simple plug-and-play models. Managed switches offer extra settings for people who need them. Power over Ethernet (PoE) is useful only if you have compatible devices that receive power through the network cable.

    A simple checklist before you spend money

    1. Describe the problem: weak Wi-Fi, slow wired speed, not enough ports or old equipment.
    2. Check the speed and type of internet service you pay for.
    3. Check what your devices support before paying extra for newer features.
    4. Think about your home layout, walls and where equipment can be placed.
    5. Check software update support, warranty, return policy and the full cost.
    6. Look for independent tests where available, and do not confuse advertised link rates with real-world speed.

    Choose equipment by the problem you need to solve

    Buying a new router is not the first step for every slow-connection complaint. Identify the weak point first: internet service entering the home, Wi-Fi coverage, wired ports, or the number of devices that need a connection. The right product depends on the layout of your home and the equipment your provider supplies.

    Compatibility checks that prevent costly mistakes

    • Provider requirements: Ask whether your ISP allows third-party routers and whether the existing ONT or modem must remain in place.
    • WAN and LAN ports: A router with a fast Wi-Fi label may still have a slower internet-facing port. Match port speeds to the service you actually have.
    • Wi-Fi client support: Phones, laptops and smart-home devices must support the features you expect to use. A new router cannot make an older client support a newer Wi-Fi standard.
    • Coverage and construction: Floor plan, walls and interference affect coverage. One centrally placed router may outperform a more expensive unit hidden in a cabinet.
    • Security and updates: Check the vendor’s firmware-support policy, automatic updates, WPA2/WPA3 support and guest-network controls.

    Set a budget around the actual bottleneck

    Write down the problem, the rooms and devices affected, and the features you genuinely need. Compare total cost—including extra mesh nodes, adapters or installation—with a simpler wired or placement fix. Avoid choosing solely by the largest advertised Mbps number: those figures are laboratory or combined-band maximums, not a promise for one device in your home.
