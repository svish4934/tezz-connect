Internet speed explained: Mbps, download and upload

An internet speed test gives you numbers. This guide explains what those numbers mean, what can affect them, and how to compare results fairly.

What does Mbps mean?

Mbps means megabits per second. It describes how much data a connection can transfer in one second. Internet providers usually advertise plans in Mbps. Do not confuse megabits (Mb) with megabytes (MB): one byte contains eight bits, so a 100 Mbps connection has a theoretical maximum of about 12.5 megabytes per second before protocol overhead and other real-world limits.

A speed test measures the connection between your device and the test server at that moment. It is not a permanent rating of every website or service you use. Results can change with Wi-Fi signal, network congestion, device load, server distance and ISP routing.

Download speed vs upload speed

Download speed measures how quickly data reaches your device. It matters when loading websites, streaming video, downloading files and receiving content in a video call.

Upload speed measures how quickly your device sends data out. It matters for sending large files, cloud backups, live streaming and transmitting your camera or screen during calls.

How much internet speed do you need?

There is no single ideal speed for every household. Requirements depend on the number of people and devices, the type and quality of content, and whether several demanding activities happen at once. The ranges below are rough planning examples, not guarantees or official minimum requirements.

Use caseWhat to consider
Web browsing and emailUsually modest bandwidth; stable Wi-Fi and low congestion often matter more than a very high plan.
HD video streamingAllow enough sustained download capacity for each active stream, plus headroom for other devices.
4K video streamingNeeds more sustained bandwidth than HD; check the streaming provider's current recommendation.
Video meetingsBoth upload and download matter. Consistency, latency and avoiding congestion are important.
Large uploads and cloud backupPrioritise upload capacity and consider when backups run.
Online gamingLatency, jitter and route quality can matter more than headline download speed.

How to run a useful internet speed test

  1. Pause large downloads, cloud backups and high-bandwidth streams where practical.
  2. Stand near your Wi-Fi router, or use Ethernet if available.
  3. Close unnecessary browser tabs and let other household users know you are testing.
  4. Run the test more than once, at different times of day, and compare the pattern.
  5. Record whether you used Wi-Fi or Ethernet and which device you tested.

For a fair comparison, test under similar conditions. A single result is only a snapshot. If results are consistently below what you expect, compare another device and connection method before concluding that the ISP is at fault.
