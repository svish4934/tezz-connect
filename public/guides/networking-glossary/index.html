Internet and networking terms, explained simply

Networking can sound technical. This searchable glossary explains common terms in everyday language, with examples that help you understand what they mean at home.

Bandwidth

The maximum data-carrying capacity of a connection. Think of it as the width of a road; more bandwidth can carry more data at once.

Mbps

Megabits per second, a unit used to describe data transfer rate. Internet plans commonly advertise download and upload speeds in Mbps.

MB/s

Megabytes per second, often shown by file downloads. One byte is eight bits, so 8 Mbps is theoretically 1 MB/s before overhead.

Download speed

How quickly data arrives at your device. It matters when loading websites, streaming video and downloading files.

Upload speed

How quickly your device sends data out. It matters for video calls, cloud backups, sending files and live streaming.

Throughput

The data rate actually achieved over a period. It can be lower than the connection's theoretical capacity because of overhead, congestion and other conditions.

Latency

The time taken for data to travel between two points and for a response to return, often measured in milliseconds. Lower latency usually feels more responsive.

Ping

A tool and measurement commonly used to check response time to another device or server. A ping result often reports round-trip time in milliseconds.

Jitter

Variation in packet delay. High or uneven jitter can make voice and video calls sound choppy even when download speed looks good.

Packet loss

When data packets fail to reach their destination. It can cause missing audio, lag, retransmissions or interrupted connections.

Congestion

When more traffic competes for network capacity than is readily available. It can increase delay and reduce the throughput users experience.

Buffering

A player pauses to collect more data before continuing playback. It can happen when the incoming stream cannot keep up with playback demand.

Wi-Fi

A family of wireless networking technologies that lets devices connect to a local network and, through a router, usually to the internet.

2.4 GHz band

A Wi-Fi frequency band that often reaches farther and passes through some obstacles better, but can be more crowded and offer lower peak capacity.

5 GHz band

A Wi-Fi band that can offer higher capacity and more channels than 2.4 GHz in many places, but may have shorter practical range through walls.

6 GHz band

An additional Wi-Fi band available with compatible Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 equipment and permitted regional rules. Device support and local regulations matter.

SSID

The name of a Wi-Fi network shown when you choose a network to join. A router may broadcast separate names for different bands or guest access.

Interference

Unwanted radio energy or competing transmissions that can make wireless communication less reliable. Neighbouring networks and some household devices may contribute.

Router

A device that forwards traffic between networks. In homes it commonly connects the local network to the ISP and may also provide Wi-Fi and Ethernet ports.

Modem

A device that connects to an internet service over a particular access technology. Fibre services often use an optical network terminal (ONT) instead.

Access point

A device that provides Wi-Fi access to a wired local network. A wired access point can add wireless coverage in another part of a building.

Mesh Wi-Fi

A system using multiple coordinated Wi-Fi units to cover a larger area. The units need a suitable wireless or wired connection between them.

Ethernet

A family of wired networking standards commonly used with network cables and ports. A wired link can be more consistent than Wi-Fi for stationary devices.

DNS

The Domain Name System looks up domain names, such as example.com, and returns information such as the IP address a device needs to contact.

Put these terms into practice

Now that you know the vocabulary, explore how the concepts work together. A speed test measures throughput under test conditions; ping helps you understand response time; and Wi-Fi placement can influence the quality of the wireless link between your device and router.

Remember

A single number rarely explains the whole connection. Download speed, upload speed, latency, jitter, packet loss, Wi-Fi quality and the remote service can all affect the experience.
