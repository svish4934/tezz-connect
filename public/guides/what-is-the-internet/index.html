What is the internet and how does it work?

The internet can feel invisible: you tap a link and a page appears. Behind that simple action is a worldwide system of connected networks, routers, cables, wireless links and computers that exchange data.

So, what exactly is the internet?

Think of the internet as a huge system of roads connecting computers and devices all over the world. Your phone, your internet provider, a website's computer and millions of other networks are all connected in some way. These connected networks let information travel from one place to another.

The internet is not one giant computer in one building. It is made up of many networks run by different organisations. They work together so you can send a message, watch a video, make a payment or visit a website.

One useful difference: the internet is the network itself. The web is the collection of websites and pages you open in a browser. Email, online games and video calls also use the internet, but they are not websites.

What happens when you open a website?

Imagine you type a website name into your browser and press Enter. A few things happen very quickly:

  1. Your device connects. Your phone or laptop uses Wi-Fi, an Ethernet cable or mobile data to reach a network.
  2. The website name is looked up. DNS (the Domain Name System) works a bit like your phone's contacts list. It helps find the network address linked to a name such as example.com.
  3. Your request travels across networks. Your router and internet provider help send the request towards the computer that hosts the website. The route can vary.
  4. The website sends information back. The server replies with the files needed for the page.
  5. Your browser puts the page together. It uses those files—text, pictures, styles and other parts—to show the website on your screen.

This is the simple version. Large websites may use several servers, saved copies of files (caches) and content delivery networks to load pages faster.

Modem, router and Wi-Fi: what do they do?

Modem or fibre ONT

This is the equipment that connects your home to the provider's network. Cable or DSL services often use a modem. Fibre services commonly use a device called an ONT. Sometimes these parts are built into one box with the router.

Router

Your router connects the devices in your home to each other and to the internet. It directs information to the right device. Many home routers also provide Wi-Fi and have Ethernet ports for wired connections.

Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi is simply a wireless way for your phone, laptop or TV to connect to your home network. It is not the internet itself. For example, your phone may still connect to your printer over Wi-Fi even if your internet provider has an outage.

What does your internet provider do?

Your internet service provider (ISP) connects your home or office to its network and helps your data reach other networks. It may use fibre, cable, DSL, fixed wireless or a mobile network to provide the connection.

The speed written on your plan is the service level you have subscribed to. It does not mean every app or website will always run at exactly that speed. Your Wi-Fi, device, network traffic, website server and the route your data takes can all affect what you experience.

How does information travel?

When you send a photo or open a website, the information is usually broken into smaller pieces called packets. Think of sending a large parcel as several smaller packages. Network equipment reads the address information on each packet and forwards it towards the right destination.

The receiving device puts the pieces back together. Some types of internet traffic can resend missing pieces; other types use different methods because speed or timing matters more. You do not need to manage any of this yourself—your devices and network handle it.

Why can your internet feel slow?

  • Wi-Fi signal: distance, thick walls, floors and interference can weaken the wireless connection.
  • Too many active devices: several people streaming, gaming or downloading at once can share the available capacity.
  • Your device: an older phone, laptop or network adapter may not be able to use the full connection speed.
  • Your provider's network: busy networks can sometimes slow things down.
  • The website or service: a slow server or a long route can make one website feel slow even when other sites work well.
  • Response delays: a connection can download files quickly but still take longer to respond when you click or play a game.

The simple version to remember

  • The internet is a worldwide network made up of many connected networks.
  • Websites, email, video calls and games are services that use the internet.
  • Your router connects your home devices; Wi-Fi is just one way they connect to it.
  • DNS helps your device find the right website address.
  • Real-world speed depends on more than the plan you pay for.

Try it on your own connection

Run a test near your router, then try again from the room where you usually use the internet. Note whether you used Wi-Fi or a cable. That small comparison can help you spot a home Wi-Fi issue.
