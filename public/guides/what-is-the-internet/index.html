What is the internet and how does it work?

The internet can feel invisible: you tap a link and a page appears. Behind that simple action is a worldwide system of connected networks, routers, cables, wireless links and computers that exchange data.

What is the internet?

The internet is a global network of interconnected computer networks. It allows devices such as phones, laptops, servers and smart televisions to exchange information using agreed communication rules called protocols. The internet is the underlying network; the web, email, video calls, online games and file transfers are services that use it.

There is no single machine that contains the whole internet. It is made up of many independently operated networks connected through physical infrastructure and network equipment. These networks cooperate to move data from one destination to another.

What happens when you open a website?

Imagine entering a website address in your browser. A simplified version of the journey looks like this:

  1. Your device connects to a local network. Your phone or computer uses Wi-Fi or an Ethernet cable to reach your router, or it connects through a mobile network.
  2. The domain name is looked up. The Domain Name System (DNS) helps find the network address associated with a name such as example.com. Your device or network may already have a cached answer.
  3. Your request is routed onward. Your router and your internet provider pass traffic through networks toward the server or service that hosts the website. The exact route can vary.
  4. The server responds. The destination processes the request and sends the required page data back across the network.
  5. Your browser builds the page. It receives resources such as HTML, stylesheets, scripts, images and fonts, then renders the page on your screen.

This is a simplified explanation. Modern websites may use content delivery networks, encrypted connections, caches and several servers to deliver different parts of a page.

Modem, router and Wi-Fi: what is the difference?

Modem or access terminal

The equipment that terminates your internet service depends on the connection type. A cable or DSL modem connects to the provider's access network. A fibre connection commonly uses an optical network terminal (ONT) to convert the fibre signal into a connection your local equipment can use. Some devices combine this function with a router.

Router

A router connects your home or office network to another network, usually your provider's network. At home, it commonly directs traffic between your devices and the internet. Many home routers also provide Wi-Fi, a network switch and basic firewall functions.

Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi is a wireless local-area networking technology. It lets nearby devices communicate with your router using radio signals instead of a physical Ethernet cable. Wi-Fi is not the internet itself: a Wi-Fi network can work locally even when the provider's internet connection is down.

What does an internet service provider do?

An internet service provider (ISP) connects customers to its network and to other networks. Depending on the service, the provider may operate fibre, cable, DSL, fixed wireless or mobile infrastructure. Providers also manage routing, address allocation, network capacity and connections to other operators.

Your advertised plan describes a service tier, not a guarantee that every website will deliver data at that exact rate. Actual performance depends on the access link, network load, Wi-Fi conditions, device capability, destination server and the route between them.

How does data travel in packets?

When information moves across the internet, it is generally divided into smaller units called packets. Network protocols add addressing and control information so devices can forward the packets toward their destination. The receiving device or application reassembles the data as needed.

Packets belonging to one communication may take different paths, arrive at different times or need retransmission if some are lost. Protocols such as TCP provide reliable, ordered delivery for many applications; other traffic uses different transport methods depending on its needs.

What affects internet speed?

  • Your access technology and plan: fibre, cable, DSL, fixed wireless and mobile connections have different performance characteristics.
  • Wi-Fi signal quality: distance, walls, interference, router placement and supported Wi-Fi standards can affect the wireless link.
  • Network congestion: busy home networks or provider networks can reduce available capacity.
  • Device capability: older phones, computers, network adapters or software may limit performance.
  • Destination and routing: the server, its capacity and the path to it influence the experience.
  • Latency and packet loss: response delay and lost data can make interactive applications feel slow even when download bandwidth is high.

Key points to remember

  • The internet is a network of networks, not one central computer.
  • The web is one service that runs on the internet.
  • Wi-Fi connects devices to a local network; it does not by itself provide an internet subscription.
  • DNS helps translate human-readable domain names into network addresses.
  • Routers and other network devices forward data packets between networks.
  • Real-world performance depends on the full path, not just the speed shown on an internet plan.
