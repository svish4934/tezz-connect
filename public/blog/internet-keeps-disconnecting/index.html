Internet Problems & Fixes

Why Does My Internet Keep Disconnecting?

If your internet keeps dropping, first check whether one device, Wi-Fi only or every device loses connection. That clue helps narrow the cause to your device, router, modem or ISP.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

An internet connection that works for ten minutes and then disappears can interrupt video calls, online classes, payments, streaming, gaming and work. The frustrating part is that “internet disconnecting” can describe several different failures. Your phone may leave the Wi-Fi network, the router may lose its connection to the modem or fibre ONT, the ISP may have a local outage, or a device may simply fail to resolve website names. These problems can look similar on screen, but they require different fixes.

The most useful first step is not to reset everything. It is to identify exactly what stops working, which devices are affected and what the router or ONT reports at that moment. This guide explains a safe way to isolate the fault, test Wi-Fi separately from the broadband line, investigate common causes and prepare useful evidence before contacting your provider. The instructions apply broadly to home broadband connections, including fibre, DSL and cable services; menu names and indicator lights vary by equipment model.

1. First identify what is actually disconnecting

Start by separating three layers of a home connection:

  • Device-to-Wi-Fi connection: Your phone or laptop communicates wirelessly with the router. If this link fails, the device may show “Disconnected”, “Saved” or repeatedly ask for the Wi-Fi password.
  • Router-to-internet connection: Your device remains connected to the router, but the router cannot reach the ISP network through its modem, ONT or WAN connection. Apps may say “No internet”.
  • Application or name-resolution connection: The device and router may still be connected, but a particular app, website, DNS resolver or remote service is unavailable. One failed website does not prove the whole broadband line has disconnected.

When the next interruption happens, look at the Wi-Fi symbol and the network name. Does the device remain connected to the correct Wi-Fi network? Can you open the router’s local management page, if you know its address? Do several unrelated websites fail, or only one app? These observations are more useful than repeatedly switching Wi-Fi off and on.

Important distinction: A Wi-Fi icon can remain visible even when the router has lost its internet connection. Wi-Fi describes the local wireless link; it does not guarantee that the ISP connection is working.

2. Use a second device to find the scope of the problem

At the moment of a drop, test another device connected to the same home network. For example, if your phone stops loading pages, check a laptop or another phone. If possible, test a device connected by Ethernet as well. Do not rely on a speed test alone: a speed test cannot tell you whether a device has left Wi-Fi, whether the router has rebooted or whether the ISP link has failed.

This table is a diagnostic guide, not a guarantee. A router can have more than one fault, and an intermittent ISP issue may happen at the same time as weak Wi-Fi. Repeat the comparison during at least two separate interruptions before deciding that one component is responsible.

3. Watch the router, modem or fibre ONT lights

When the connection drops, look at the equipment before restarting it. Note whether the power light remains steady, whether the Wi-Fi indicator stays on, and whether the internet, WAN, DSL, PON or LOS indicators change. The exact labels and normal states depend on the manufacturer and service type. A red LOS light on some fibre ONTs, for example, can indicate loss of optical signal, but indicator meanings must be confirmed in the manual for the exact model.

Take a photo or short video of the lights if the change is brief. Record the time. If the router appears to reboot, note whether all lights go off and return through a startup sequence. If only the internet/WAN indicator changes while power and Wi-Fi remain steady, that points toward the upstream connection rather than a complete power failure.

Do not unplug, clean, bend sharply or reseat a fibre connector as an experiment. Fibre equipment and connectors can be delicate, and provider-installed optical connections may require technician handling. If the optical indicator reports a fault, contact the ISP and describe the exact light state.

4. Check whether the router itself is restarting

A router that unexpectedly reboots can make every device appear to lose internet at once. Common possibilities include overheating, an unstable power adapter, a damaged power socket, failing hardware or a firmware problem. These are possibilities to investigate, not conclusions that can be drawn from one interruption.

Look for a repeating startup pattern

Observe whether the power indicator turns off and then the device repeats its normal startup sequence. If this happens at similar intervals, record the times and whether the router feels unusually hot. Place it in an open, ventilated location, away from direct sunlight and enclosed cabinets. Do not cover the vents or place it on a heat-producing appliance.

Check power safely

Confirm that the adapter is firmly connected and that the cable is not visibly damaged. If the router uses a removable adapter, use only the manufacturer-approved replacement with the correct electrical rating and connector. Do not try a random adapter because its plug fits. If the router and ONT have separate power supplies, note which device restarts; do not assume both need replacement.

Consider the electrical supply

Brief power interruptions, loose sockets or an overloaded extension board can interrupt network equipment. If other electrical devices also flicker or restart, the issue may be broader than the router. Use safe, properly rated power equipment and consult a qualified electrician for suspected wiring or socket faults. Never open the power adapter or router enclosure.

5. If only Wi-Fi drops, investigate the wireless environment

When wired internet remains stable but wireless devices disconnect, focus on the Wi-Fi link. Radio signals weaken with distance and obstacles. Concrete walls, metal cabinets, floors, mirrors, appliances and crowded neighbouring networks can reduce reliability. A device may show a reasonable signal indicator while still experiencing interference or repeated retransmissions.

Test close to the router

Take one affected device into the same room as the router and observe whether the connection remains stable. If it works nearby but fails in a distant room, coverage or interference is more likely than an ISP-wide outage. Repeat the test in the original location. Avoid changing router settings during this comparison.

Choose the band based on distance and device support

Many routers offer 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz Wi-Fi. The 2.4 GHz band often reaches farther and can pass through obstacles more effectively, but it is commonly shared with many nearby networks and household devices. The 5 GHz band can offer higher data rates and more channel options, but its coverage is often shorter through walls. Newer equipment may also support 6 GHz. Availability depends on the router, client device and local regulatory rules.

If your router combines bands under one network name, a feature such as Smart Connect may move devices between bands. Some older or low-cost devices may not handle band steering consistently. If the router supports it and the problem appears limited to one device, consult the manufacturer's instructions before temporarily separating band names for a controlled test. Record the original settings so they can be restored.

Reduce interference and improve placement

Place the router in an open, central and elevated location where practical. Keep it away from large metal objects, enclosed cupboards and obvious sources of radio interference. Do not hide it behind a television or inside a closed cabinet. If you live in a dense apartment building, neighbouring Wi-Fi networks may overlap. A router's automatic channel selection may help, but performance varies with local conditions and equipment.

Do not repeatedly change channels, channel width, security mode and band settings together. Change one setting at a time, test, and keep a note of the result. Some channel choices and transmit-power settings are country-specific; use the options permitted by the router's India-region firmware.

6. Check the device that disconnects

If one phone, tablet or computer is affected while other devices remain online, begin with that device. Restarting it once may clear a temporary software problem, but repeated network resets can erase useful saved settings. Check for operating-system and Wi-Fi driver updates from the device manufacturer. On laptops, review whether power-saving settings are turning off the wireless adapter. On phones, check whether the issue occurs on other Wi-Fi networks as well.

Android phones

  • Confirm that the phone is connecting to the correct saved network and not switching to a weak nearby network.
  • Temporarily disable VPN or private DNS only as a controlled test if you use one; restore the setting afterwards and avoid disabling security tools permanently.
  • Check whether the phone disconnects while the screen is off or during battery-saver mode. Device manufacturers use different power-management behaviour.
  • Install available system updates and, if the problem remains isolated to this phone, consult the manufacturer's network troubleshooting instructions before resetting network settings.

iPhone and iPad

  • Check whether other devices stay connected at the same time and whether the Apple device has the same problem on another trusted Wi-Fi network.
  • Install current iOS or iPadOS updates when practical. If a VPN, security profile or filtering app is installed, check its configuration with the provider before removing it.
  • Forget and rejoin the network only after confirming that you know the correct Wi-Fi password.
  • Use Apple's official Wi-Fi troubleshooting guidance for model- and software-specific steps.

Windows laptops

  • Run the built-in network troubleshooter and note any exact error message.
  • Check Windows Update and the laptop or wireless-adapter manufacturer's driver support page.
  • In Device Manager, review the Wi-Fi adapter's power-management options if the connection drops during sleep or battery use. Menu availability varies by driver.
  • Compare behaviour on battery and while connected to power, without changing several adapter settings at once.

If the device disconnects from several unrelated Wi-Fi networks, its wireless adapter, operating system or configuration deserves more attention. If it fails only on your home network, compatibility with the router or the local wireless environment is more likely.

7. Inspect cables and the router's WAN connection

For wired broadband, a loose or damaged Ethernet cable between the modem/ONT and router can cause intermittent WAN loss. Check that the cable is firmly seated in the correct WAN or Internet port. Look for crushed sections, sharp bends, damaged clips or connectors that move easily. If you have a known-good Ethernet cable of the correct type, test it as a replacement and change only that cable at a time.

For DSL connections, a damaged telephone cable, poor wall socket, faulty splitter or line noise may contribute to drops. Follow the ISP's installation guidance for filters and splitters; do not add or remove them randomly. For fibre, do not bend or pull the optical lead, open the ONT or handle provider-sealed connections. Ask the provider to inspect the optical path if the ONT reports loss of signal.

If the router's WAN port has link indicators, note whether the link disappears at the same time as the internet. A WAN Ethernet link dropping can indicate a cable, port or modem/ONT issue. A stable Ethernet link does not prove that the ISP service is healthy, but it helps narrow the investigation.

8. Understand DNS failures and VPN-related symptoms

Sometimes the broadband connection is still active but domain names are not resolving correctly. DNS translates a website name into the network address needed to reach it. If DNS requests fail, a browser may report that a site cannot be found even though the router remains connected. A DNS issue may affect multiple websites, while an individual website outage may affect only one service.

Compare more than one unrelated website and, if you are comfortable, use the device's built-in network diagnostics. A successful connection to the router's local address but failed access to external destinations suggests the fault is beyond the local Wi-Fi link, although it does not by itself distinguish DNS from ISP routing. Avoid changing DNS settings as the first response. If you test another resolver, record the original setting and use a reputable provider; understand that DNS changes do not repair a damaged cable, weak signal or failing optical line.

A VPN, proxy, private DNS service, parental-control filter or security application can also change how traffic is routed. If the issue began immediately after installing or updating one of these tools, review its logs and configuration. Temporarily pause it only when safe and permitted by your organisation, and restore protection after the test. Never disable security software permanently just to improve connectivity.

9. Restart equipment in a controlled order

A normal restart can clear a temporary software or session problem. It is different from a factory reset. A restart powers equipment off and back on; a factory reset erases configuration such as Wi-Fi names, passwords, ISP credentials and custom settings. Start with a normal restart and follow the ISP or equipment manufacturer's sequence.

  1. Record the current indicator states and the time of the interruption.
  2. If your ISP supplied the equipment, check its instructions for the correct restart sequence. Some installations combine the modem and router in one unit.
  3. Use the power control or unplug the power adapter only as directed. Do not disconnect fibre or other service cables unnecessarily.
  4. Allow the modem or ONT to complete its startup and synchronisation process before powering or reconnecting the router if the instructions specify that order.
  5. Wait for the internet indicator to settle, then test one device and, if possible, a second device.
  6. Record whether the restart fixed the problem and how long the connection stayed stable.

Microsoft's Windows guidance describes a typical modem/router restart process, including allowing the modem lights to settle before reconnecting the router. However, provider-supplied fibre equipment may have a different sequence, so the instructions for your model and ISP take priority. A restart that restores service temporarily is useful evidence, but it does not establish whether the underlying cause is firmware, power, hardware or the provider network.

10. Update firmware carefully; avoid premature factory resets

Router manufacturers sometimes release firmware updates that address stability, security or compatibility problems. Check the exact model and hardware revision on the manufacturer's official support page or router app. Install only firmware intended for that model and region, and follow the manufacturer's instructions. Do not interrupt power during an update. If the ISP manages the router remotely, ask the provider whether it controls firmware updates.

Do not install firmware from unofficial websites or use a file for a similar-looking model. A wrong firmware image can make the router unusable. If the device is already unstable, contact the ISP or manufacturer before attempting an update that could be interrupted.

A factory reset should be a later step, not a routine first fix. It can erase the broadband login, VLAN, IPTV, voice, Wi-Fi and other provider-specific settings. Before resetting, confirm that you have the configuration details and know how to restore the service. If the router belongs to the ISP, ask its support team to guide or perform the reset.

11. Look for ISP outages, line faults and congestion patterns

If wired and wireless devices lose internet at the same time, investigate the modem/ONT and provider connection. Check the ISP's official app, website, SMS alerts or support channel for a local outage. Ask neighbours using the same provider whether they are affected, but treat this only as supporting information: nearby homes may be connected to different network equipment.

Record whether the problem occurs at a particular time, after rain, during electrical interruptions, or after a long period of uptime. A drop that occurs only during busy evening hours may be related to congestion or a provider-side fault, but timing alone does not prove congestion. If the router reboots, that is a different clue. If the optical signal indicator changes, report that specifically. If the link remains connected but only certain services fail, mention those services and any error messages.

For a useful comparison, run a wired test when the connection is stable and again during the problem, if a computer and Ethernet cable are available. Keep the same device and test destination where possible. A speed test result is only a snapshot; combine it with the time, connection type, router indicators and whether ordinary browsing works.

12. A practical 15-minute troubleshooting sequence

Use this sequence during the next interruption. It is designed to gather evidence before you make disruptive changes.

  1. Minute 0–2: Capture the symptom. Note the exact time, the device, the Wi-Fi network name and the message shown on screen.
  2. Minute 2–4: Compare devices. Test a second phone or laptop. Check whether it is connected to Wi-Fi and whether websites load.
  3. Minute 4–6: Check wired service if available. Test a computer connected to the router by Ethernet. Do not connect directly to an ONT or modem unless the ISP's instructions say it is safe and you know the required configuration.
  4. Minute 6–8: Observe equipment. Record power, Wi-Fi, WAN, DSL, PON or LOS light behaviour. Do not rely on generic colour descriptions.
  5. Minute 8–10: Check scope and location. If only Wi-Fi devices fail, test close to the router. If only one device fails, compare that device on another network.
  6. Minute 10–12: Check for provider notices. Use mobile data if available to check the ISP's official outage channel.
  7. Minute 12–15: Preserve evidence and choose one next step. Photograph lights, save error messages and write down the results. Restart only if the equipment instructions support it and the evidence has been captured.

If the connection returns before you finish, keep the notes. Intermittent faults are often difficult to diagnose after they disappear; timestamps and observations help the support team look for matching network events.

13. What to tell your ISP so the fault can be investigated

When you contact support, avoid reporting only “Wi-Fi is not working”. Explain whether the Wi-Fi network disappears, devices remain connected but have no internet, or both wired and wireless devices fail. Provide the router and ONT model numbers, the indicator states, the time and duration of each drop, and the troubleshooting steps already completed.

Ask the provider to check the access link, signal or optical levels where applicable, line events, authentication/session drops and any local outage affecting your connection. The provider may use different technical terms depending on whether your service is fibre, DSL, cable or fixed wireless. Ask for a complaint or ticket reference and the expected next update.

India-specific consumer guidance: TRAI's consumer grievance information describes a two-tier process: first raise the service complaint with the provider's complaint centre, then approach the provider's Appellate Authority if the complaint is not resolved satisfactorily. TRAI also publishes information about complaint handling timelines and applicable service-quality rules. Check the current TRAI guidance and your provider's published complaint procedure, because the applicable details depend on the service and complaint type. Keep your complaint reference, dates and responses.

Do not assume that TRAI directly repairs an individual broadband line. The provider's support and appellate mechanism is the route described for individual consumer complaints. If the provider closes the ticket without addressing repeated drops, reopen or appeal through the published process and attach your log.

14. Troubleshooting mistakes that can make the problem harder

  • Factory-resetting immediately: This may erase provider settings and make the connection harder to restore.
  • Changing several Wi-Fi settings together: You will not know which change helped or made things worse.
  • Assuming every interruption is an ISP fault: A single device or wireless-only issue may be inside the home.
  • Assuming a strong Wi-Fi icon means the broadband line is healthy: Signal strength and internet availability are different measurements.
  • Repeatedly restarting without recording the symptoms: This removes useful evidence and may hide a recurring reboot pattern.
  • Handling fibre connectors or opening equipment: Leave provider-side optical work and internal repairs to qualified technicians.
  • Installing unofficial firmware or random power adapters: These can create new faults or damage equipment.
  • Buying a new router before isolating the fault: New Wi-Fi hardware will not repair a provider line fault, damaged ONT or unstable power supply.

15. When equipment replacement or professional help is justified

Consider a router replacement only after you have evidence that the router is the weak point. Examples include a stable connection through the provider's modem/ONT but repeated failures through the router, wireless drops across multiple devices while wired service remains stable, or a manufacturer-confirmed hardware fault. Check whether the router is still supported and whether the ISP requires a particular model or configuration.

Ask the ISP to inspect its equipment and line when the ONT or modem reports a persistent fault, the WAN connection drops across devices, or a direct provider-approved test remains unstable. Seek qualified electrical assistance if the power supply or household socket appears unsafe. Stop using visibly damaged equipment.

Buying a higher-speed broadband plan is not a reliable fix for random disconnections. Speed and connection stability are related to different factors. A faster plan may increase available throughput, but it will not resolve weak Wi-Fi coverage, a failing adapter, damaged cable, overheating router or optical signal loss.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my internet disconnect for a few seconds and reconnect?

Brief drops can come from Wi-Fi interference, a device roaming between access points or bands, a router reboot, a WAN cable or modem issue, or a provider-side session interruption. Record whether the Wi-Fi network itself disappears and whether wired devices are affected. The pattern is more informative than the duration alone.

Why does my phone disconnect from Wi-Fi but my laptop stays connected?

The phone may have a device-specific software, power-management or compatibility issue, or it may be in a location with a weaker signal. Test the phone near the router and on another trusted Wi-Fi network. Check for system updates and review VPN or private DNS settings if relevant.

Why does Wi-Fi show connected but say no internet?

Your phone may still have a working wireless connection to the router while the router has lost its upstream internet connection. Check another device, the router's internet/WAN status and the modem or ONT indicators. If all devices show the same message, contact your ISP with the time and indicator details.

Can a router cause internet disconnections?

Yes. Router firmware, overheating, a failing power adapter, hardware faults, wireless interference or a WAN-port/cable problem can interrupt service. Compare Wi-Fi and Ethernet behaviour before deciding whether the router is responsible.

Can too many devices make Wi-Fi disconnect?

Many active devices can compete for wireless airtime and available broadband capacity, causing slowdowns, lag or unstable experiences. However, device count alone does not prove the cause of a complete disconnection. Test with a small number of devices and compare wired service, router status and the timing of the drops.

Should I restart my router every day?

A restart can clear a temporary fault, but needing one every day is a sign that the underlying issue deserves investigation. Record how often it happens and whether the router is rebooting or only losing its internet connection. Follow the manufacturer or ISP instructions for restarting.

Will changing DNS stop my internet from disconnecting?

Changing DNS may help when name resolution is the specific problem. It will not fix a Wi-Fi radio dropping, a damaged cable, an unstable power supply or an ISP line fault. Confirm that multiple devices and websites are affected before treating DNS as the likely cause.

Does a factory reset fix frequent disconnections?

It can help in some cases where incorrect router settings or corrupted configuration are responsible, but it also erases saved settings and may remove ISP-specific configuration. Try simpler checks first and ask the provider before resetting ISP-managed equipment.

How do I know if my broadband provider is responsible?

If several devices lose internet at the same time, including a wired device, and the router or ONT indicates an upstream fault, the provider connection becomes a stronger possibility. A provider outage or line fault still needs confirmation by the ISP. Share timestamps, equipment indicators and results from any provider-approved wired test.

What should I do if my fibre ONT shows a red LOS light?

Check the manual for your exact ONT model. On many fibre devices, LOS indicates a loss-of-signal condition, but light meanings vary. Do not bend or disconnect the fibre lead. Contact your ISP and report the indicator state and when it began.

Can rain or bad weather cause broadband disconnections?

Weather may coincide with faults in exposed cables, power systems or provider infrastructure, but timing alone does not establish the cause. Record the conditions and ask the provider to check the access line if drops repeat during or after weather events.

When should I replace my router?

Consider replacement when testing isolates the problem to the router, the device is no longer supported, hardware is failing or it cannot meet the needs of your home. First rule out the ISP line, modem/ONT, power supply and cabling. If the ISP supplied the router, confirm compatibility and configuration requirements before buying another unit.

Conclusion: diagnose the layer before changing the equipment

Frequent internet disconnections are easier to solve when you separate the device, Wi-Fi, router, modem/ONT and ISP layers. Start by checking whether one device or the entire home is affected. Compare wireless with wired service, observe the equipment indicators, record timestamps and make one controlled change at a time. Avoid factory resets, unofficial firmware and unnecessary hardware purchases until the evidence points to a specific cause.

If several devices lose service together or the provider equipment reports a line fault, give your ISP a clear record and request a proper investigation. If only one device or the wireless network fails, focus on that part of the connection. A careful diagnosis can save time, avoid unnecessary expense and help support teams resolve recurring faults more effectively.
