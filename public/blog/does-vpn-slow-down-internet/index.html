Does a VPN Make Your Internet Slower?

Learn why VPNs can change download speed, upload speed and ping, and how to compare servers and troubleshoot performance without compromising security.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Short answer: yes, a VPN can slow a connection, but the effect is not fixed

A virtual private network (VPN) changes the path your internet traffic takes. Instead of sending traffic directly from your device through your internet service provider (ISP) to an online service, a VPN creates an encrypted tunnel to a VPN server. That server then forwards traffic towards the destination. The extra tunnel, encryption work, server capacity and network route can reduce throughput or increase latency.

That does not mean every VPN connection will feel slow. A nearby, well-connected VPN server and a modern device may deliver performance that is adequate for browsing, streaming and work. A distant or overloaded server, an inefficient route, weak device hardware or a problematic network can cause a much larger drop. Sometimes a VPN can even improve access to one particular destination if its route is better than the direct route, but it cannot create additional physical broadband capacity.

The useful question is not simply “Does a VPN reduce speed?” It is “What changes when I connect this device to this VPN server, and is that change caused by the VPN, my home network or the destination?” This guide explains how to answer that with repeatable tests, without sacrificing security just to chase a speed-test number.

1. Understand what changes when you connect a VPN

Without a VPN, your device sends packets through your local network and ISP towards the destination. The destination generally sees the public IP address used by your internet connection. With a full-tunnel VPN, your device first encrypts traffic and sends it to a VPN server. The server decrypts the traffic and forwards it to the destination. Replies travel back through the VPN server and encrypted tunnel.

This creates several performance dependencies. Your device must process encryption and decryption. Your connection must reach the VPN server. The VPN server must have enough capacity for its users. The VPN provider must have a suitable onward route to the destination. The destination itself must also be available and responsive. A slowdown can occur at any one of these points.

A VPN does not normally increase the speed tier supplied by your ISP. If your plan provides a certain maximum access capacity, the VPN still uses that connection to reach its server. The tunnel adds processing and routing overhead; it does not turn a 100 Mbps broadband plan into a 200 Mbps plan.

2. The main reasons a VPN can reduce speed

Extra distance and routing detours

Distance matters because data takes time to travel across networks. If you are in Mumbai and choose a VPN endpoint in another continent, your traffic must travel to that endpoint before it is forwarded to the website or service. A website whose server is geographically close to you may therefore be reached through a much longer route. This is sometimes described as a trombone effect: traffic travels away from the destination region to the VPN endpoint and then back towards the destination.

Geography is only a guide. The shortest physical route is not always the fastest network route. Peering arrangements, submarine cable paths, congestion, transit providers and the VPN operator's network design can change the result. A nearby endpoint can perform poorly if its route is congested, while a somewhat more distant endpoint can perform well if it has better connectivity. Test more than one location rather than assuming the closest country is always best.

VPN server load and capacity

A VPN endpoint is shared infrastructure. Many customers may use the same server or gateway. If the server's CPU, network interface, memory or upstream capacity becomes busy, it may queue traffic or deliver less throughput. The provider may display a load percentage, but that figure is not a complete measurement of end-to-end performance. It may describe only one resource and may not include congestion on the route beyond the server.

Free services and heavily oversubscribed endpoints can be especially variable, although price alone does not prove how a service performs. A paid plan is not a guarantee of fast performance either. Test the actual endpoint and time you intend to use.

Encryption and device processing

VPN software encrypts outgoing traffic and decrypts incoming traffic. Modern phones and computers often have hardware acceleration and can process common encryption efficiently. Older devices, low-power processors, busy background applications and some router-based VPN configurations may become bottlenecks, particularly on fast broadband connections.

Encryption is not automatically the dominant cause of every slowdown. The server route and endpoint capacity may matter more than the cryptographic calculation on a modern device. If the same VPN connection performs differently on a newer laptop and an older router, device processing or router capability may be part of the explanation.

Tunnel overhead, MTU and packet handling

A VPN adds headers and encapsulation to packets so that they can travel inside the tunnel. This reduces the space available for the original data within a network packet. The maximum transmission unit (MTU) describes the maximum packet size a link can carry at a particular layer. If tunnel settings and the path MTU do not work well together, packets may be fragmented, retransmitted or dropped.

MTU problems do not always appear as a simple lower speed-test result. They can show up as certain websites failing to load, large transfers stalling, remote desktop sessions behaving poorly or some applications working while others do not. OpenVPN's performance troubleshooting guidance and Microsoft's networking documentation describe how fragmentation, packet loss and MTU mismatches can affect throughput. Most home users should not change MTU values at random. Use the VPN provider's supported settings or ask its support team to investigate if symptoms suggest a tunnel-size problem.

ISP-to-VPN peering and congestion

Your ISP and VPN provider exchange traffic through interconnected networks. The route between them may be congested at particular times or may take an inefficient path. This can cause a VPN connection to perform poorly even when your broadband is otherwise healthy. It is one reason that two VPN providers, or two servers from the same provider, can produce different results on the same home connection.

Protocol and implementation differences

VPN protocols define how a secure tunnel is negotiated and how data is protected and transported. WireGuard, OpenVPN and IPsec-based options are examples, but their performance depends on implementation, device support, configuration, network conditions and server capacity. A newer protocol is not automatically faster in every environment. Some networks restrict or interfere with certain transport methods, while a protocol that works well on one device may not be supported by an older router.

Use a protocol supported and recommended by the VPN provider for your device. If the app offers a choice, compare options using the same server and controlled test conditions. Do not select a protocol solely because an advertisement claims it is the fastest, and do not weaken security settings without understanding the consequences.

3. Download speed, upload speed and ping can change differently

Download throughput measures how quickly data reaches your device. A VPN can reduce it if the tunnel, endpoint, route or device becomes the limiting factor. The impact may become more visible on a very fast broadband plan because the VPN endpoint must handle a larger volume of encrypted traffic.

Upload throughput measures how quickly your device sends data. It may be affected by the same tunnel and server limits. Upload performance is important for cloud backups, video calls, sending large files and remote work. Do not assume that a VPN affects download and upload by the same percentage.

Latency or ping measures round-trip delay to a test destination. A VPN commonly increases ping when it adds a longer route. The amount depends on where the VPN endpoint is located and how traffic is routed beyond it. A small change may be unnoticeable for ordinary browsing, while a large increase can make remote desktop, gaming and interactive calls feel delayed.

Jitter is variation in packet delay. Packet loss occurs when packets fail to reach their destination. A VPN can expose or contribute to these problems if a tunnel endpoint or route is congested or unstable. A basic speed test may not report all these measures, so investigate them separately if calls, games or remote sessions are affected.

Also distinguish idle latency from loaded latency. If a large upload or download causes ping to rise sharply, the problem may involve queueing or bufferbloat on the home router or network path. A VPN can change the route and the measured result, but it is not necessarily the root cause of queueing.

4. Full-tunnel VPN, split tunnelling and browser extensions

Full-tunnel connections

In a full-tunnel configuration, most or all eligible device traffic is routed through the VPN. This gives a consistent network path for applications covered by the VPN, but it also means that a distant or congested VPN endpoint can affect many activities at once. Some providers exclude local network traffic or selected services through their own routing rules; check the app's documentation.

Split tunnelling

Split tunnelling allows selected applications or destinations to use the VPN while other traffic uses the ordinary internet connection. This can reduce unnecessary routing for activities that do not need the tunnel, and it may improve performance for a local service. It also means that not all traffic receives the same VPN protection. A banking app, browser or file-sharing client may follow a different route depending on the rules you configure.

Use split tunnelling only when you understand which applications are included and excluded. It is not a universal speed fix and may be disabled by workplace policy or the VPN provider. For a work-managed device, follow your organisation's instructions rather than changing routing rules yourself.

Browser VPN extensions and proxy services

A browser extension described as a VPN may protect only browser traffic, or it may operate as an encrypted proxy rather than a full-device VPN. Other applications on the device may continue to use the normal internet connection. This distinction matters when comparing speed tests: a browser-based test may travel through the extension while a separate desktop application may not.

Check the provider's description to understand whether the product is a full-device VPN, browser-only proxy or another privacy tool. Do not assume that installing an extension protects all traffic from your phone or computer.

5. How to test VPN speed fairly

A useful comparison changes one variable at a time. If you switch from Wi-Fi to Ethernet, change the test service, choose a different VPN country and update the app all at once, you will not know which change affected the result. Use a repeatable procedure instead.

  1. Choose one capable device. Use the same laptop or phone for every baseline and VPN test. If possible, connect the laptop to the router by Ethernet to remove Wi-Fi variation. If you must use Wi-Fi, stay in the same location and record the band.
  2. Pause competing traffic. Stop cloud backups, downloads, game updates and streaming on other devices. Keep the test device's background activity as consistent as possible.
  3. Choose a nearby test server. Use the same speed-test service and the same server for the direct and VPN comparisons. If the VPN changes your apparent location and the service automatically selects a different server, manually choose the same test endpoint where possible.
  4. Measure without the VPN. Disconnect the VPN using its normal app control. Run several tests and record download, upload, ping, jitter and packet loss if shown. Do not choose only the best reading.
  5. Connect to one VPN endpoint. Select the VPN server you actually intend to use. Wait until the app reports a stable connection. Confirm whether split tunnelling or custom routing is active and record it.
  6. Repeat the same measurements. Use the same device, test service, server, connection type and approximate time. Record the VPN location and protocol as well as the result.
  7. Repeat at another time. Run the comparison again later or on another day. Network congestion and server load change, so one session is only a snapshot.
  8. Compare a second VPN endpoint if needed. Change only the VPN server location, keeping the rest of the method constant. This helps identify whether one endpoint or route is unusually slow.

Do not run dozens of tests back-to-back and assume every result is independent. A test itself uses bandwidth and may temporarily affect other measurements. Allow the connection to settle between runs and keep a short, consistent testing routine.

Compare ping in milliseconds separately; a percentage change in ping is often less intuitive than the absolute difference.

7. Why a VPN speed test can be misleading

Some speed-test services automatically select a server based on your apparent IP address or estimated location. When you connect to a VPN, that location may move to the VPN endpoint, and the test may choose a different server. If the direct test uses a server near your home but the VPN test uses a server near the VPN endpoint, the two readings do not isolate the VPN's effect.

For a controlled comparison, select the same test server manually if the service allows it. If that is not possible, record the server names and treat the results as a broader end-to-end comparison rather than a strict like-for-like test. Repeat with a second independent service to check whether the pattern is consistent.

Other sources of misleading results include browser extensions, different Wi-Fi bands, mobile data switching, battery-saving modes, device updates, background synchronisation and tests run at different times. Make a note of anything that changed between the sessions.

8. Can a VPN ever make internet access faster?

Sometimes a VPN can make one destination appear faster. The direct route from your ISP to a website may be congested or inefficient, while the route from the VPN endpoint to that website may be better. The VPN can therefore change the path and improve the performance of that specific destination at that moment.

This does not mean the VPN increases the capacity of your broadband line or makes every website faster. Another destination may become slower because it is now reached through a longer path. The VPN endpoint itself may also become busy. Treat a faster result as a route-specific observation, not proof that your ISP deliberately throttles all traffic.

Claims that a VPN will always bypass ISP throttling need care. A VPN can hide some application details from the local network, but it does not prevent congestion, capacity limits, data caps or all forms of traffic management. The VPN provider and destination still see traffic characteristics, and the encrypted tunnel can itself be identified as VPN traffic.

9. Troubleshoot a VPN that suddenly becomes slow

Start with a direct connection baseline

Disconnect the VPN temporarily using the app's normal control and test the same device and service without the tunnel. If performance is poor both with and without the VPN, investigate the broadband connection, router, device and test server first. See our guide to checking whether your ISP is delivering the speed you pay for.

Try a nearby server and another endpoint

Choose a nearby VPN location, then compare another server in the same region. If one endpoint is slow and another is consistently better, the issue may be endpoint load or routing. Do not assume the server's country alone explains the difference.

Compare supported protocols

If your VPN application offers multiple supported protocols, test one at a time using the same endpoint and measurement method. A protocol may behave differently across networks, routers and devices. Restore the provider's recommended setting if the alternative causes connection instability or is not suitable for your use.

Update the app and device

Use the official application from the provider or device platform. Install security updates and VPN app updates, then restart the app if it has become unresponsive. An outdated client can have bugs or compatibility problems. Avoid downloading unofficial modified VPN apps or configuration files from unknown websites.

Check router-based VPN processing

If the VPN runs on your home router rather than on the phone or computer, the router must encrypt and decrypt traffic for all devices covered by the tunnel. Some consumer routers have limited processing capacity for VPN workloads even if they can route ordinary broadband traffic at high speeds. Check the router manufacturer's specifications and provider guidance. Do not assume a router's advertised Wi-Fi or WAN rate is also its VPN throughput rating.

Look for MTU or connection stability symptoms

If only some websites load, large downloads stall, or a connection repeatedly hangs, the issue may involve packet size, path MTU, fragmentation or packet loss rather than ordinary server distance. Do not blindly change MTU or registry settings. Save diagnostic logs and contact the VPN provider or your IT administrator with the symptoms and device details.

Check local network and ISP conditions

Wi-Fi interference, weak signal, overloaded router queues and other household traffic can affect both direct and VPN connections. Run a wired baseline if possible. If the VPN works well on a different network but not on your home connection, that comparison may help narrow down the issue, but it does not by itself identify whether the ISP, router or network policy is responsible.

10. VPNs and streaming, gaming, video calls and downloads

Streaming video

Streaming services adapt video quality to available throughput and may buffer when the path cannot sustain the selected bitrate. A VPN endpoint can change the route to the streaming service, and the service may treat the connection differently based on its licensing, security or network policies. If playback quality drops only with the VPN, compare the same programme, device, time and network with the VPN on and off. Respect the streaming service's terms and regional licensing restrictions.

Online gaming

Games are sensitive to latency, jitter and packet loss, not just download speed. A VPN may add a route detour and increase ping. It can occasionally improve a poor route to a particular game server, but it can also make matchmaking, voice chat or anti-cheat systems behave differently. Compare ping to the actual game region where possible; a speed-test server is not a substitute for the game server.

Video calls and remote work

Calls depend on stable upload and download, low delay, manageable jitter and low packet loss. A corporate VPN may route video traffic through an organisation's gateway for security and policy reasons. Do not bypass a work VPN or change its split-tunnel rules without approval. If calls become unreliable, share timestamps and call-quality diagnostics with your IT team. The issue may be the corporate gateway, home Wi-Fi, endpoint device or service route.

Large downloads and cloud backups

Throughput depends on the VPN tunnel and the remote file server. A download can be limited by the source server or its content delivery network even when the VPN is fast. For cloud backups, upload speed and stability matter. Test the same file source or service where possible, and distinguish a one-off server limitation from a repeated VPN-wide slowdown.

11. Security and privacy: do not trade protection for a benchmark

A VPN can protect traffic between your device and the VPN endpoint, particularly on networks you do not control. It can help remote workers reach private company resources and can reduce the visibility of some traffic details to the local network. It does not make you anonymous, guarantee that a website cannot identify you, or protect you from phishing, malware, weak passwords and unsafe downloads.

HTTPS remains important. A VPN tunnel does not replace the encryption and authentication provided by HTTPS between your browser and a website. The VPN provider becomes an important party in your connection path and may be able to observe connection metadata. Choose a provider whose ownership, privacy policy, logging claims, independent audits, security practices and jurisdiction you can evaluate. Marketing slogans are not a substitute for understanding how a service handles data.

Be cautious with unknown free VPN apps. Some services may collect data, display intrusive advertising, impose restrictive limits or have unclear ownership and security practices. Free does not automatically mean unsafe, and paid does not automatically mean trustworthy. Review the provider carefully, install from official sources and avoid giving unnecessary permissions.

Keep the VPN kill switch enabled if you rely on it to prevent traffic from leaving the device when the tunnel disconnects. Understand how it behaves on your operating system and whether local network access is allowed. A kill switch can interrupt connectivity when the VPN fails; that is an intentional privacy control, not necessarily a broadband fault. Check your provider's documentation before changing it.

12. Choosing a VPN: what to evaluate beyond speed claims

Look beyond a single headline speed figure. Consider whether the provider supports your devices, offers nearby endpoints, publishes clear privacy information, maintains security updates, explains its protocols and provides useful troubleshooting support. For work, confirm whether the organisation requires a specific VPN client or configuration. For personal use, consider the actual locations and applications you need rather than paying for a large server list you will never use.

Check how the provider explains its logging policy and whether independent audits are available. Understand what information is collected for account management, diagnostics, abuse prevention and payment. A “no logs” claim can mean different things; read the policy rather than assuming it means no information is ever processed.

Evaluate reliability as well as throughput. A service that briefly reaches a high speed but disconnects frequently may be unsuitable for remote work. Look at connection stability, support responsiveness, app quality, update history and compatibility with your router or operating system. Avoid selecting a provider solely on affiliate rankings or sponsored speed comparisons that do not disclose testing methods.

13. A practical checklist before blaming the VPN

  • Confirm that the direct internet connection is working normally.
  • Use the same device and, where possible, Ethernet for the baseline and VPN test.
  • Pause downloads, backups and other heavy household traffic.
  • Use the same speed-test service and manually select the same test server if possible.
  • Record the VPN endpoint, protocol, app version and connection time.
  • Compare download, upload, ping and quality metrics separately.
  • Repeat the test at another time rather than relying on one session.
  • Try a second nearby VPN endpoint before changing advanced settings.
  • Check whether the VPN runs on the device, router or browser only.
  • Keep privacy and workplace security requirements in place while troubleshooting.

Frequently asked questions

Does a VPN always slow down the internet?

No. A VPN adds a tunnel and changes the route, so it can reduce throughput or increase latency. The size of the effect depends on endpoint distance and load, routing, protocol, device capacity and network conditions. Some routes may perform better through a VPN than through the direct ISP path.

Why does my internet speed drop so much when I turn on a VPN?

Possible causes include a distant or overloaded VPN endpoint, inefficient ISP-to-provider routing, device or router processing limits, protocol compatibility, tunnel MTU problems or background traffic. Compare a direct wired baseline with the VPN using the same test server and device before changing settings.

Which VPN protocol is fastest?

There is no protocol that is fastest on every device and network. WireGuard, OpenVPN and IPsec-based implementations have different designs and support requirements. Test the options your provider officially supports, and choose a secure, stable configuration rather than relying on a universal speed ranking.

Can a VPN increase ping?

Yes. Traffic must travel through the VPN endpoint, which can add distance and routing hops. Ping can also rise because of server load or congestion. Choose an endpoint with a suitable route to the service you use and compare latency to the actual destination where possible.

Can a VPN make my internet faster?

It can sometimes improve access to a particular destination if the VPN route is less congested or more efficient than the direct route. It does not increase the physical capacity of your broadband plan and may make other destinations slower.

Why is my VPN fast on my phone but slow on my router?

The phone and router may have very different processing capabilities and VPN implementations. A router encrypting traffic for several devices may reach its CPU or throughput limit. Compare the same endpoint and protocol, check the router's VPN performance specifications and follow its manufacturer's guidance.

Should I use a free VPN?

Evaluate the provider's ownership, privacy policy, security practices, limits, app permissions and reputation. Some free services are legitimate, but an unknown app with unclear data practices is not a safe bargain. Do not assume a paid service is automatically trustworthy either.

Does a VPN protect all apps on my phone?

A full-device VPN usually routes eligible device traffic through its tunnel, subject to operating-system and app exclusions. A browser extension or proxy may protect only browser traffic. Check the product description and routing settings to understand what is covered.

Should I turn off my VPN to get better speed?

Only if doing so is appropriate for the activity and does not violate workplace policy or your security requirements. For diagnosis, a temporary direct comparison can help identify whether the VPN changes performance. Restore the protection when the test is complete if you rely on it.

Conclusion: test the route, not just the headline number

A VPN can affect internet performance because it adds encryption, tunnel overhead, a VPN endpoint and a different network route. The impact is shaped by server distance and capacity, peering, protocol, device processing, packet size and the destination itself. There is no single percentage slowdown that applies to every VPN, ISP, country or device.

Measure a direct baseline, repeat the same test with a nearby VPN endpoint, keep the test server and device consistent, and record download, upload and latency separately. If the result changes, compare another endpoint and investigate the route before changing security settings. A good VPN decision balances performance with privacy, reliability and the purpose for which the tunnel is needed.
