What Is Li-Fi? How Visible Light Communication Works

Imagine a ceiling light that does more than illuminate a room. With the right optical hardware, it can also become a wireless network access point. Li-Fi uses rapidly modulated light to carry digital information, creating a communication link that can complement Wi-Fi in offices, factories, hospitals and other indoor spaces. The idea is often described as “internet through light”, but the engineering involves much more than switching a bulb on and off.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

What is Li-Fi?

Li-Fi, short for Light Fidelity, is a form of optical wireless communication designed to provide network connectivity using modulated light. A transmitter changes the output of an optical source—often an LED luminaire or an infrared emitter—in a carefully controlled pattern. A compatible receiver detects those variations and converts them into digital data. In a networked Li-Fi system, access points connect to a wired network and serve client devices in a manner that has similarities to Wi-Fi.

The central concept is straightforward: light can carry information as well as illumination. The data modulation happens much faster than the human eye can normally perceive, so a properly designed lighting system does not visibly flicker during ordinary operation. The receiver is not simply a human eye or an ordinary camera. It generally needs a photodetector and signal-processing electronics designed for the optical link.

Li-Fi is not a replacement for fibre backhaul, routers or the internet itself. A Li-Fi access point still needs a connection to a network, usually Ethernet or another wired or wireless backhaul. It creates the last wireless hop between that network and a compatible client. The internet service provider, routing, DNS, servers and applications remain part of the wider connection.

It is also important not to confuse a product demonstration with a complete wireless network. A lamp that sends a one-way signal to a photodiode is an optical communication link, but a practical Li-Fi network must address uplink traffic, multiple users, access control, mobility, interference, handover, management and interoperability. Those system-level requirements distinguish a network technology from a simple light-based transmitter.

Li-Fi, visible light communication and optical wireless: the terms

Optical Wireless Communication (OWC) is the broad umbrella for sending information through light in free space rather than through a physical cable. Depending on the system, the optical carrier may be visible light, infrared or ultraviolet. Free-space optical links between buildings, infrared links and visible-light data systems all fit within this wider family.

Visible Light Communication (VLC) usually describes communication that uses the visible portion of the spectrum. LEDs can be modulated to transmit data, and photodetectors can recover the signal. VLC can be a one-way link or a component of a larger system. The term does not automatically mean a complete multi-user internet access network.

Li-Fi is commonly used for a bidirectional, networked form of optical wireless connectivity. The system can provide access to multiple users, manage connections and support movement between optical access points. The practical boundary between terms can vary across research papers, standards and vendors, so examine what a particular product actually supports instead of relying only on its label.

IEEE 802.15.7 is a standard family for short-range optical wireless communications and visible-light-related systems. IEEE 802.11bb-2023 brought light communications into the IEEE 802.11 wireless LAN family. Its standardised optical PHY uses the near-infrared band from 800 to 1,000 nanometres, so “Li-Fi” should not always be interpreted as visible room lighting carrying every bit. The wider ecosystem includes both visible and infrared optical approaches.

How does Li-Fi work? From LED modulation to decoded data

A typical optical downlink begins at a network-connected access point. Digital data arrives from the Ethernet or network interface. The transmitter maps that data into a signal suitable for the optical channel. Driver electronics vary the current through the LED or infrared emitter so that its optical output changes according to the encoded information.

The intensity changes are designed to be detected electronically. They are not intended to be slow flashes that a person can count. The LED can continue providing a steady-looking level of illumination while its output contains tiny, rapid variations. The exact modulation scheme depends on the PHY design, channel conditions, light source and receiver capabilities.

At the client, a photodetector—often a photodiode—converts incoming optical power into an electrical current. Analogue front-end circuitry amplifies and filters the signal. A receiver then synchronises with the transmission, estimates the data symbols, corrects errors where supported and passes recovered bits to higher network layers. The client can then exchange normal IP traffic with the network.

A photodetector is not the only possible optical receiver. Camera-based optical communication can use an image sensor, although camera frame rates, exposure control, rolling shutter effects and processing requirements create a different design space. Camera communication is useful in some positioning and identification applications, but it should not be assumed to deliver the same performance as a dedicated high-speed photodiode receiver.

Why the light does not visibly flicker

Human vision does not resolve every rapid change in light intensity. A transmitter can encode data using changes far faster than the visual system can follow. However, safe and comfortable lighting still requires careful control of flicker, dimming behaviour and optical output. Poorly designed drivers can create visible or measurable flicker even if the data modulation itself is fast. Lighting quality and communication performance must be engineered together.

How does the uplink work?

A wireless network needs data to travel in both directions. A laptop downloads a webpage, but it also sends requests, acknowledgements, messages and uploads. A phone receives video, but it transmits camera and microphone data during a call. A Li-Fi system therefore needs a return path from the client to the access point.

One approach uses visible light for the downlink and infrared for the uplink. A small infrared transmitter in the client sends data back to an optical receiver at the access point. Infrared is invisible to the human eye, which can make it suitable for a return channel without adding a visible blinking light on a laptop or handset. Other architectures may use a different optical arrangement or combine optical downlink with a radio-frequency uplink.

The uplink is a practical design challenge. A ceiling luminaire can have room for a relatively powerful transmitter and a broad optical footprint, while a phone or laptop has strict limits on size, power, heat, battery consumption and user orientation. The client may be placed in a pocket, tilted away from the ceiling or partly covered. A good system needs to maintain a usable return channel under realistic device positions.

This is why a headline downlink rate does not tell the whole story. Ask for both downlink and uplink performance, whether figures are aggregate or per user, how performance changes with distance and orientation, and what happens when a client blocks the optical path. For video calls, cloud backups and interactive applications, a weak uplink can be just as frustrating as a slow download.

What is IEEE 802.11bb?

IEEE 802.11bb-2023 is an amendment to the IEEE 802.11 wireless LAN family that specifies light communications. It was designed to bring optical wireless links into a familiar WLAN framework, including physical-layer and medium-access provisions. The aim is to make light-based networking fit more naturally alongside established Wi-Fi network concepts rather than requiring every deployment to invent an unrelated networking stack.

The standard's optical operation uses the 800–1,000 nm near-infrared band. IEEE's task group describes uplink and downlink operation in this band and a minimum single-link throughput requirement of 10 Mb/s at the MAC data service access point. The maximum rate often quoted for the amendment is up to 9.6 Gb/s at the MAC service access point under specified conditions. That is a standard capability figure, not a promise that a retail installation or every client will deliver gigabit speeds in a real room.

One important goal is coexistence and integration with conventional 802.11 networking. A user should not have to understand the optical physical layer to use an IP application. The network can manage access and connectivity through standardised mechanisms, while the optical front end provides the communication medium. Actual interoperability still depends on compliant products, certification, implementation and available client hardware.

IEEE 802.11bb is not the only optical wireless standard. IEEE 802.15.7 addresses short-range optical wireless communications, while ITU-T G.9991 specifies high-speed indoor visible-light communication transceivers and systems. IEEE 802.15.13 targets other high-rate optical wireless scenarios. These standards have different scopes and architectures; they should not be treated as interchangeable product labels.

Li-Fi compared with Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi uses radio-frequency signals, commonly in the 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz bands depending on the generation and region. Li-Fi uses optical signals. Both can carry network traffic, but their propagation behaviour is different. Radio waves can travel around obstacles and through many common building materials, while visible light is generally blocked by opaque objects and walls.

Li-Fi can provide an additional communication medium in areas where RF spectrum is constrained or where spatially confined coverage is useful. A business should compare total system cost and measured performance, not assume that the newer medium automatically delivers a better user experience.

Potential advantages of Li-Fi

More capacity in a confined area

Optical access points can create small coverage cells, sometimes described as attocells. Multiple luminaires in different rooms can reuse optical channels because walls and room boundaries limit signal propagation. This spatial reuse may help add capacity in dense indoor environments, provided the network can manage interference between nearby optical cells and support the required client density.

Reduced dependence on crowded RF spectrum

Li-Fi does not use the same RF channels as Wi-Fi. In a location with heavy radio traffic, a light-based link can provide another access medium. It is not immune to interference: sunlight, other lighting, reflections, receiver saturation and overlapping optical cells can affect performance. The advantage is that its interference profile is different, giving network planners another tool.

Physical confinement

Light generally does not pass through opaque walls. A signal serving one room is therefore less likely to be received from a neighbouring room than a radio signal that penetrates the wall. This can help create spatially bounded coverage and may reduce some forms of unintended signal leakage. It is not encryption, authentication or a complete security boundary.

Compatibility with lighting infrastructure

In some deployments, communication equipment can be integrated into LED luminaires that already provide illumination. This creates an opportunity to combine lighting and network infrastructure. The economics depend on the cost of optical access points, client adapters, cabling, installation and maintenance. Existing lamps cannot simply be assumed to support Li-Fi; they need suitable drivers, transmitters, receivers and network components.

Useful where RF is restricted or difficult

Optical communication may be attractive in selected industrial, medical, research or secure environments where RF use is limited or where a separate optical channel is desirable. Suitability must be assessed against local rules, safety requirements and the exact environment. A marketing claim that Li-Fi is “RF-free” does not remove the need for a complete site survey and compliance review.

Limitations: what Li-Fi cannot do

Obstruction can interrupt the path. A person walking between a transmitter and receiver, a closed door or an object placed over a sensor can reduce or block the optical link. Reflected paths may preserve some connectivity, but performance depends on room geometry and surface properties. Network design must account for real movement, furniture and device orientation.

Coverage is local. Light does not normally travel through walls, so a Li-Fi access point generally serves a limited area. This can be an advantage for spatial reuse but a disadvantage for whole-building roaming. Users need overlapping cells, wired backhaul and handover mechanisms to move between rooms without losing their sessions.

Ambient light can matter. Sunlight and other light sources can add background optical power or noise at the receiver. Filters, modulation, receiver design and placement can mitigate some effects, but bright windows and changing daylight should be tested in the actual deployment. Outdoor use can be particularly challenging.

Client devices are not universal. Most mainstream phones and laptops do not contain a Li-Fi optical transceiver. Users may need a dongle, dedicated adapter or purpose-built device. Until client hardware becomes widely available and interoperable, the addressable market and everyday convenience are limited.

Uplink and mobility require engineering. A high-speed downlink alone is not enough. The return path, device orientation, cell handover and coexistence with Wi-Fi all affect user experience. A network must be tested with moving users and typical device positions, not only with a stationary receiver pointed directly at a lamp.

Lighting and network requirements can conflict. The best location for illumination is not always the best location for an optical access point. Dimming, colour temperature, glare, flicker, energy use and ceiling layout need to be considered together. A communication design must not compromise safe, comfortable lighting.

Does Li-Fi work in daylight or when lights are dimmed?

Daylight does not automatically make Li-Fi impossible, but it can create a strong background optical signal. The receiver must distinguish the modulated communication from ambient illumination. Optical filters, narrow fields of view, suitable wavelengths, signal processing and careful receiver design can improve robustness. The actual result depends on the link budget and installation, especially near large windows or outdoors.

Dimming is another system-specific question. A transmitter may continue carrying data while the lamp is dimmed, but the available optical signal level and communication margin can change. Some systems use modulation and coding methods designed to maintain illumination requirements. Other systems may use infrared for communication so that data transmission is not tied directly to visible brightness.

“Works with the lights off” is therefore not a universal property. A visible-light downlink generally needs enough visible optical output for the receiver. An infrared-based link can operate without visible illumination, but it still needs a compatible optical transmitter and receiver. Always check the wavelength and operating mode of the product rather than assuming all Li-Fi systems use the same light source.

Security and privacy: is Li-Fi safer?

Because light is usually confined by walls, optical networks can reduce the area in which a signal is readily received. In a building with opaque walls, an adjacent room may not receive the same optical signal as it would from a Wi-Fi access point. This physical property can be valuable for network segmentation and spatially controlled coverage.

However, a person inside the illuminated area may still receive the signal. A window, open doorway or reflective surface may allow optical energy to escape or be observed. A malicious receiver could be placed in the same room. The presence of a light source does not prove that a user is authorised to connect.

Li-Fi networks still need strong authentication, encryption, secure key management, network segmentation, firmware updates and monitoring. Apply the same security principles used for Wi-Fi and Ethernet. Do not transmit sensitive data in plaintext merely because the physical medium is light. Physical confinement is a useful additional property, not a substitute for cryptography.

Privacy matters in location-aware optical systems as well. Some VLC deployments can use the identity or position of a luminaire to provide indoor positioning. If a device's location is collected, explain the purpose, limit retention and control who can access the data. A network should not silently turn lighting infrastructure into a staff or visitor tracking system.

Where could Li-Fi be used?

Offices and conference rooms: Optical access points integrated with ceiling fixtures could provide additional capacity in meeting spaces with many connected devices. A hybrid system can use Wi-Fi for general coverage and optical cells for selected work areas. The deployment should be tested for laptops on desks, people moving around and devices used under tables.

Hospitals and healthcare facilities: Optical networking may be useful in selected areas where RF planning is complicated or where spatially confined connectivity is desirable. Medical environments have strict safety, reliability and equipment requirements. A Li-Fi installation must be evaluated alongside clinical workflows, infection-control practices, device compatibility and fallback connectivity. It should not be assumed that optical wireless is automatically approved for every medical area.

Factories and industrial facilities: Optical links can provide an additional communication path in environments with challenging RF conditions or high device density. However, moving machinery, dust, vibration, reflective metal surfaces and safety-critical controls require careful engineering. Industrial networks need predictable availability and validated failover; a consumer lighting link is not a substitute for a certified control network.

Education and libraries: Classrooms and lecture halls could use optical access points to add local capacity or provide controlled connectivity zones. A practical system needs compatible student devices or adapters, straightforward support and a Wi-Fi fallback for devices that cannot use the optical link.

Retail, museums and indoor positioning: Light fixtures can potentially provide both communication and location references. A phone or dedicated receiver may identify a luminaire and use that information to support indoor navigation, exhibit information or asset workflows. Positioning accuracy depends on the infrastructure and receiver, and the data should be designed with privacy in mind.

Aircraft and transport environments: Optical communication has been explored for environments where RF spectrum is constrained or where local wireless links need different interference characteristics. Any deployment in aircraft or transport systems must meet stringent electromagnetic compatibility, safety, certification and operational requirements. A research demonstration is not evidence of commercial approval.

Li-Fi and indoor positioning

Lighting infrastructure is already distributed across many buildings, which makes it an interesting platform for indoor positioning. If luminaires transmit identifiers or carefully structured signals, a receiver can estimate which light source it can see. With multiple known reference points, the system may calculate an approximate position or guide a user through a building.

Positioning and data communication are related but different tasks. A system can send an identifier through light without providing high-speed internet access. Conversely, a high-speed Li-Fi link does not automatically provide accurate coordinates. Positioning needs a map of the light sources, a calibration process, receiver information and algorithms that account for reflections, occlusion and movement.

Camera-based optical positioning can use a phone camera to detect coded light sources, while photodiode systems can use dedicated receivers. The choice depends on accuracy, latency, power, device availability and privacy. Camera systems may need permission to access the camera and can be affected by exposure, motion and field of view.

What does a real Li-Fi deployment need?

Begin with a site survey. Record room dimensions, ceiling height, lighting positions, windows, reflective surfaces, doors, furniture and expected user movement. Identify whether the target is one room, a floor or a multi-building campus. Decide where optical coverage is genuinely useful and where Wi-Fi or wired Ethernet remains the better choice.

Specify the whole link, not just the lamp. Document optical access point model, wavelength, downlink and uplink, modulation, supported standard, MAC integration, user capacity, latency, handover, encryption, authentication and network management. Ask vendors for independent measurements and test results in realistic lighting conditions. Confirm whether the quoted data rate is physical-layer, MAC-layer, aggregate or per-client throughput.

Plan the client side early. Determine whether users will carry an adapter, use a purpose-built laptop or phone, or connect fixed equipment. Check operating-system support, drivers, device management and how clients fall back to Wi-Fi. A network that performs well with a demonstration receiver but cannot connect the organisation's actual devices is not a practical deployment.

Design the uplink and backhaul. Ceiling access points need power and a network connection, often through Ethernet and Power over Ethernet where supported. The optical return path needs a compatible transmitter in each client. Evaluate whether the uplink is optical, radio-frequency or hybrid and what happens when the optical path is blocked.

Test resilience. Walk through the area while transferring data, rotate and cover client devices, open and close doors, change lighting levels, introduce daylight and add realistic numbers of users. Test handover between access points and observe whether voice or video sessions continue. Measure latency, jitter, packet loss, throughput and recovery time—not just a peak download speed.

Finally, calculate lifecycle cost. Include access points, client adapters, cabling, installation, lighting integration, management software, support, replacement parts and staff training. Compare the cost with alternatives such as additional Wi-Fi access points, better channel planning, wired connections or private cellular. Li-Fi makes sense when its specific advantages solve a measurable problem.

Is Li-Fi the future of Wi-Fi?

Li-Fi is better understood as another access technology that can join a heterogeneous network. Future indoor networks may combine fibre backhaul, Wi-Fi, cellular, optical wireless and other short-range systems. Each medium can serve the places and applications where its propagation, capacity, mobility and cost profile is strongest.

The IEEE 802.11bb amendment is an important standardisation milestone because it brings light communication into the 802.11 family. Standards can improve interoperability and create a clearer framework for vendors, but they do not guarantee mass-market adoption. Affordable client radios, compact optical receivers, easy installation, robust uplinks, certification and a compelling business case still matter.

For most homes today, a well-placed Wi-Fi router or mesh system remains far more practical. Most consumer devices already support Wi-Fi, and the ecosystem of routers, phones, laptops and support tools is mature. Li-Fi may become useful in selected enterprise or specialised environments before it becomes a routine feature in ordinary homes.

The realistic question is not whether light will eliminate radio. It is whether optical wireless can add value where radio has limitations or where a room-scale optical cell provides a useful extra layer. In those settings, Li-Fi could become part of the broader connectivity toolkit rather than a universal replacement.

Frequently asked questions

1. What does Li-Fi mean?

Li-Fi means Light Fidelity. It is a bidirectional optical wireless networking approach that uses modulated light, commonly from LED or infrared sources, to transmit data between access points and compatible devices.

2. How does Li-Fi transmit data?

A transmitter varies the intensity of an optical source at high speed to encode information. A photodetector receives the changing light and converts it into an electrical signal that a receiver decodes.

3. Is Li-Fi faster than Wi-Fi?

There is no universal answer. Laboratory demonstrations and standards specify high potential rates, but real-world throughput depends on equipment, distance, lighting, uplink, interference, network design and client support. Compare tested products rather than headline laboratory figures.

4. Does Li-Fi work when the lights are off?

A visible-light link needs an optical signal, but data transmission does not necessarily require a room to look brightly lit. Systems may use dimmed light or infrared wavelengths, depending on their design and standard. Complete darkness can interrupt a visible-light downlink.

5. Can Li-Fi pass through walls?

Ordinary walls block or strongly attenuate visible light, so a Li-Fi cell is generally confined to a room or illuminated area. This limits coverage but can also reduce signal leakage into neighbouring spaces.

6. Is Li-Fi the same as visible light communication?

Visible Light Communication (VLC) is a broad optical transmission technique using visible light. Li-Fi usually refers to a bidirectional, networked optical wireless system with multiple users and network access features, rather than every one-way light-based link.

7. Does Li-Fi replace Wi-Fi?

Li-Fi is generally considered complementary to Wi-Fi. A hybrid network can use optical links where they work well and radio-frequency Wi-Fi or cellular connectivity for coverage, mobility, uplink and fallback.

8. What is IEEE 802.11bb?

IEEE 802.11bb-2023 is an amendment to the IEEE 802.11 wireless LAN family that specifies light communications, including physical-layer and MAC provisions for optical wireless networking.

9. Is Li-Fi more secure than Wi-Fi?

Light confinement can make a signal less likely to reach beyond a room, but it does not guarantee security. Someone in the same room or with optical access may still receive a signal. Authentication, encryption, secure network management and physical controls remain necessary.

10. What devices do I need to use Li-Fi?

A Li-Fi network needs compatible optical access points or luminaires and client devices with suitable optical transmitters and receivers. Ordinary phones and laptops do not automatically support Li-Fi just because they have Wi-Fi.
