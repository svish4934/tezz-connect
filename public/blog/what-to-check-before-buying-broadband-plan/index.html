Broadband & ISP Insights

What Should You Check Before Buying a New Broadband Plan?

Compare the full service—not just the headline speed—before choosing a broadband connection.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Choosing a home broadband connection is a decision you live with every day. It affects work calls, online classes, television, gaming, payments, cloud backups and the simple ability to open a page without waiting. Yet plan comparisons often begin and end with one large number: the advertised download speed.

That number matters, but it is only one part of the service. Two plans with the same headline speed can differ in upload performance, fair-usage rules, installation cost, router ownership, outage handling, address-level availability and what you pay after a promotion ends. A plan can look inexpensive on a banner and become less attractive after the full terms are considered.

This guide uses an original TEZZ CONNECT buying method: define the household's actual needs, verify the service at the exact address, compare the complete cost, inspect the technical and contractual details, and keep a written record of the provider's answers. It is designed for Indian households comparing fibre, cable and fixed-wireless broadband offers. It does not recommend a particular operator or assume that one technology is best in every building.

The short answer: compare the service, not the headline

Before ordering, confirm these seven things in writing: service availability at your exact flat or house; typical download and upload speeds; any data cap or fair-usage policy and the speed after the limit; the total amount payable now and in an ordinary billing month; equipment charges and ownership; installation and fault-support arrangements; and cancellation, relocation and refund terms.

Then compare at least two providers using the same checklist. If only one provider can serve your building, compare its available plans and ask current residents about reliability. Do not choose a plan solely because it has the highest download figure or the lowest introductory price.

Step 1: describe how your household actually uses the internet

Start with the people and activities, not the plan catalogue. Write down how many people are likely to be online at the same time during a normal busy period. Then list the activities that matter most. A household where everyone mostly browses and sends messages has a different demand pattern from one where a person uploads large video files, another attends work meetings, and several televisions stream at once.

Separate activities into three groups:

  • Download-heavy: streaming, downloading games, software updates, viewing large files and loading media-rich websites.
  • Upload-sensitive: video meetings, sending large files, cloud photo backups, livestreaming, remote desktop use and publishing content.
  • Delay-sensitive: online gaming, interactive calls, remote control and applications that respond to frequent small requests.

This distinction matters because download speed alone cannot describe every experience. A plan may advertise a high download rate while offering a much lower upload rate. That may be acceptable for one household and frustrating for another that regularly sends large files or works with cloud storage.

Estimate simultaneous demand without pretending there is one perfect number

Do not add every device's maximum advertised requirement and assume all devices will use it continuously. Real usage changes from minute to minute. Instead, create a realistic busy-hour picture. For example, a hypothetical family might have one television streaming, one laptop on a video call, a second laptop downloading a work file and two phones synchronising photos. The important question is not how many devices are connected; it is what they are doing at the same time.

Write down the activities that must remain usable together. If a video meeting must not become unstable while someone else uploads a large file, upload capacity and latency under load deserve attention. If the household often downloads very large games, the time required for large downloads may matter more. If the connection is mainly for browsing and occasional streaming, paying extra for a very high tier may not deliver a noticeable everyday benefit.

Use your existing connection as evidence, if you have one

If you already have broadband, review the last few weeks rather than relying on memory. Note the times when the connection feels slow, the rooms where it happens, whether multiple devices are affected and whether the problem is mainly download, upload or responsiveness. Run several tests at different times, ideally including a wired Ethernet test if you have suitable equipment. A speed test over Wi-Fi measures the combined effect of the plan, router, wireless environment, device and test route; it does not isolate the ISP by itself.

If your existing service is generally fast but Wi-Fi is poor in one bedroom, a more expensive broadband tier may not solve the problem. If wired tests are repeatedly slow across devices, the access service or equipment may deserve investigation before you pay for an upgrade.

Step 2: verify that the provider can serve your exact address

Coverage maps and city-level availability pages are useful starting points, not a confirmed installation promise. A provider may serve one road but not the next, one building but not the neighbouring building, or one wing of a large housing society but not another. Fibre may reach the building while the internal duct, riser or last section to your flat is unavailable or full.

Give the provider the exact installation address and ask for a feasibility check. If you live in an apartment, include the building or society name, wing and flat location where the provider requests it. Ask whether the service is already active in the building or whether new cabling, permissions or civil work are required. Get the feasibility result and expected installation timeline in a message, email or order record.

Ask what technology will reach your home

The word broadband describes the service, not necessarily the physical connection. Common delivery methods include:

  • Fibre to the home (FTTH): an optical fibre connection reaches the premises or home termination point. It can support high capacity and strong performance, but installation quality, provider network design, equipment and power still matter.
  • Fibre to the building (FTTB): fibre reaches the building, while the final connection to an individual flat may use another medium. Ask what is used for that last section rather than assuming the entire path is fibre.
  • Cable broadband: service is delivered over a cable network. Performance and architecture vary by provider and locality, so ask about the actual service offered at your address.
  • Fixed wireless or 5G home broadband: the home router receives service over a radio link rather than a dedicated fibre drop. Indoor placement, signal conditions, network load and local capacity can affect the experience.

Do not assume that the technology label guarantees performance. Ask what is installed, where the termination equipment will sit and whether the provider has checked signal or line feasibility for your specific premises.

Ask neighbours useful questions

Residents in the same building can provide information that a national advertisement cannot. Ask whether installations are currently being completed, how long repairs usually take, whether service drops during power cuts, whether evening performance differs from daytime and whether the provider's support team follows up. Treat these conversations as local observations, not a guarantee that your experience will be identical. Different floors, wiring routes, routers and usage patterns can produce different results.

For a new building or a provider with little local presence, ask the salesperson what happens if installation is not feasible after payment. Confirm the refund process, amount and expected timeline before placing the order.

Step 3: compare download, upload and the meaning of “speed”

Read the detailed plan page or tariff sheet. Record the advertised download speed and upload speed separately. If the provider publishes typical speeds, record those too. Ask whether the stated figures refer to the access connection, a wired test near the router or a particular test arrangement. Do not treat a Wi-Fi result in every room as a guaranteed speed.

TRAI's current broadband FAQ says that it does not prescribe one universal minimum download or upload speed for every broadband plan. It says providers must indicate the typical download and upload speeds generally available in their tariff offerings, and that at least 90% of measured test samples must meet or exceed those declared typical speeds. The same FAQ points consumers to TRAI MySpeed for measuring broadband speeds. Read the current TRAI Broadband FAQ rather than relying on old articles that quote a different rule.

Understand what the numbers mean

Broadband plans are commonly described in megabits per second (Mbps). File sizes are often shown in megabytes (MB). One byte contains eight bits, so a theoretical 100 Mbps connection corresponds to 12.5 megabytes per second before protocol overhead and other real-world limits. A download application may show a lower MB/s figure than the Mbps plan number without necessarily indicating a fault.

For a rough illustration, a 1 GB file contains about 8,000 megabits using decimal units. At a perfectly sustained 100 Mbps, the mathematical minimum would be about 80 seconds. Actual downloads can take longer because of protocol overhead, server limits, Wi-Fi conditions, other household traffic and fluctuations in throughput. This calculation is a simplified estimate, not a speed guarantee.

Do not ignore upload speed

Upload matters whenever your device sends data out. Video calls send camera and microphone data. Cloud backups upload photos and documents. A creator may upload a large video while other people use the connection. If upload capacity is limited or becomes saturated, interactive applications can feel slow even while download speed looks healthy.

Ask whether download and upload are symmetrical or whether the upload rate is lower. If the provider lists only a download figure in the advertisement, request the upload figure in writing before subscribing. Do not infer upload performance from the download number.

Speed is not the same as responsiveness

Latency is the time taken for data to travel to a test endpoint and back. Jitter describes variation in delay. Packet loss means packets do not successfully reach their destination. These factors can affect gaming, calls and remote work even when a download test shows a large number.

Ask whether the provider publishes latency, packet-loss or service-quality information for your area. If it does not, do not accept an unsupported promise that the plan is “best for gaming” or “zero lag”. Those phrases need measurable context: where was latency measured, at what time, to which endpoint and under what conditions?

Step 4: read the fair-usage policy and the real meaning of unlimited

One of the most important parts of a broadband plan is often hidden below the price and speed headline. Look for a fair-usage policy (FUP), monthly data allowance, post-limit speed, traffic-management condition or extra data charge.

TRAI explains that an unlimited plan can have a fair-speed usage limit: up to a specified data consumption level the subscriber receives the promised speed, and after that the speed is reduced. TRAI says the provider must clearly specify the FUP limit and the speed available after the limit is exhausted. This is why the word “unlimited” should never be read without the plan's conditions. See the TRAI broadband guidance.

Find the threshold and the after-limit speed

Ask four direct questions:

  1. How much data is included in one billing cycle before the FUP condition applies?
  2. What happens when that allowance is reached: speed reduction, additional charge, a top-up requirement or another restriction?
  3. What exact speed is available after the threshold, and does it remain available until the next billing cycle?
  4. Does the provider send usage alerts, and where can the subscriber check current consumption?

Do not assume that two plans labelled unlimited have the same FUP. The threshold and reduced speed may differ. Also check whether the provider's definition of data usage includes all connected devices and whether any bundled services have separate limits.

Estimate your household's monthly usage

Use actual device and application usage reports where available. If you do not have records, estimate by activity and then leave a margin for software updates, cloud backups and new devices. Do not use one universal number for every household: a home that streams frequently in high resolution and downloads large games may consume much more data than a home that mainly browses and attends occasional calls.

For a practical check, write down the approximate data use in a typical week, multiply by the number of weeks in the billing period and add a reasonable allowance for unusually busy weeks. This is a planning estimate, not a precise forecast. If your estimate is close to the FUP threshold, ask how usage is measured and whether you can upgrade or purchase extra data without changing the connection.

Check what happens at renewal

Ask when the billing cycle resets and whether the full-speed allowance refreshes automatically. Confirm whether unused data carries forward, expires or is treated another way. Do not assume that a plan's monthly allowance is based on the calendar month; it may follow the account's billing cycle.

Step 5: calculate the full price, not just the advertised monthly rental

A plan's headline price may exclude taxes, installation, activation, equipment rental, security deposit or advance payment. A promotional discount may apply only for a limited period or only when the customer chooses a longer advance-payment term. The correct comparison is the total amount you will pay over the period you realistically expect to keep the service.

Separate recurring and one-time charges

Build two columns. In the first, record recurring charges: monthly plan rental, router or mesh rental, optional add-ons, bundled television or voice services and applicable taxes. In the second, record one-time charges: installation, activation, wiring, technician visit, equipment purchase and refundable deposit. Keep refundable deposits separate from non-refundable charges; they affect cash required upfront but are not necessarily a permanent cost if returned under the terms.

Then calculate the cost for a comparison period. For a 12-month view, add all recurring charges expected over 12 months, include one-time non-refundable charges, and account for any promotional months followed by the standard price. Show refundable deposits separately as money temporarily tied up. If the provider requires a six- or twelve-month advance payment, record the cash required today as well as the average monthly cost.

Illustrative example only: suppose Plan A advertises a monthly rental of ₹599 but has a one-time installation charge of ₹1,000. Plan B advertises ₹699 with installation waived. Before taxes or other charges, Plan A costs ₹8,188 over 12 months (12 × ₹599 + ₹1,000), while Plan B costs ₹8,388 (12 × ₹699). Plan A is ₹200 cheaper over that first year, but the difference is small. If Plan A's router rental, post-promotion price or service conditions are worse, the apparent saving may disappear. These are hypothetical numbers, not current market offers.

Check taxes and bundled services

Ask whether the quoted price includes applicable taxes. If the plan bundles television channels, OTT subscriptions, landline calling or security services, identify which items are compulsory and which can be removed. Check whether a bundle price changes when an included subscription expires or when the provider changes the package. A service you do not use should not be counted as a benefit simply because it appears in a promotional banner.

Compare monthly, quarterly and annual payment options carefully

Advance-payment discounts can reduce the effective monthly price, but they also tie up money and may complicate a move or cancellation. Ask what happens if the provider cannot install the service, if you relocate, or if you need to close the account before the prepaid period ends. Get the refund calculation and processing timeline in writing. A lower effective price is not automatically better if the terms make it difficult to recover unused amounts.

Step 6: understand the router, ONT and home Wi-Fi arrangement

The broadband line and the home Wi-Fi network are related but separate parts of the service. A provider may deliver a fast connection to its termination equipment while the Wi-Fi router, its location or the building layout limits what devices experience in other rooms.

Ask what equipment will be installed. Fibre services commonly use an optical network terminal (ONT) to convert the optical connection for the home network; a separate router may be used, or the functions may be combined in one unit. Ask whether the device is owned by the provider, rented, included in the plan or sold to you. Confirm the replacement charge if it is damaged and whether you must return it when you disconnect.

Check Wi-Fi capabilities against your devices and home

Ask which Wi-Fi bands the router supports, whether it supports 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, and whether it has newer capabilities such as Wi-Fi 6 if those features matter to your devices. A newer standard is not a guarantee that every room will receive the plan's full speed. Device support, distance, walls, interference, channel configuration and router placement all influence the result.

For a large home or a multi-floor building, ask whether the provider offers mesh equipment and what it costs. A mesh system may help distribute coverage, but it does not create additional broadband capacity. Its performance depends on the connection between mesh nodes and the placement of each unit. Do not pay for a mesh add-on until you have considered the size and layout of the home and whether the current router can be positioned more effectively.

Ask about Ethernet ports and wired use

If you use a desktop computer, gaming console, television or work device in a fixed location, check whether the router has suitable Ethernet ports and whether the provider permits a wired connection. Ask about the port speed if you subscribe to a plan above 100 Mbps; an older or limited port can constrain a wired device even when the incoming service is faster. The exact port capability should be confirmed from the equipment model, not guessed from the plan name.

Power cuts and backup

Fibre equipment and routers need electrical power. During a power cut, the home router may switch off even if the provider's wider network remains operational. Some buildings have backup power for network equipment, while others do not. Ask the provider and building management what remains powered during an outage. If you need internet for work continuity, consider whether a suitable UPS or backup supply is required for the router and ONT, and check the manufacturer's power specifications before buying one.

Step 7: check installation, repair support and service reliability

A plan is only useful if the provider can install it correctly and restore it when something goes wrong. Before paying, ask how soon a technician can visit, whether building permissions or drilling are required, how internal wiring is handled and whether extra cable length or relocation creates a charge.

Ask what the normal process is when the connection fails. Is support available by phone, app, website or messaging? Can you log a fault at any time? Does the provider issue a complaint or service-request reference? How can you track the case and escalate it if the first response does not resolve the issue?

Do not rely on a sales representative's verbal assurance that service is available around the clock unless the plan documents or service terms support it. Ask what the provider commits to and what is merely a best-effort target. If a service-level agreement exists, read the definition of downtime, measurement method, exclusions, restoration targets and any service-credit conditions.

Check the complaint route before you need it

TRAI's consumer information describes a two-level grievance process: first approach the service provider's complaint centre, then use the provider's appellate authority if the complaint is not resolved satisfactorily. TRAI also explains that individual consumer complaints are not directly handled by TRAI. Keep the complaint number and correspondence. Read the current TRAI grievance-redressal information and Industry Charter.

The Industry Charter describes consumer rights that include advance disclosure of tariff, coverage and service availability; accurate billing; privacy; complaint redressal; and quality and reliable services. It also says providers should make plan terms and restrictions available transparently. Use this as a checklist of questions to ask, while remembering that the charter itself describes commitments within the applicable regulatory framework.

Step 8: read cancellation, relocation and equipment-return conditions

People often inspect installation terms carefully and skip exit terms. That can be costly if you move house, change jobs, experience repeated service issues or decide that the plan does not suit your household.

Find out whether the plan has a minimum commitment period, whether there is a cancellation fee, how much notice is required and whether any advance rental is refundable. Ask what happens to a security deposit and how long its return takes. If the router or ONT belongs to the provider, ask how and where to return it and whether a pickup is arranged. Keep the equipment serial number and a record of its condition at handover.

For a relocation, ask whether the existing account can be shifted to the new address, whether the provider serves that address and whether shifting creates an installation charge. Do not assume that service can be transferred just because the provider operates in the destination city. A new building still requires a feasibility check.

TRAI's grievance and consumer information pages are useful for understanding the complaint route and the information that should be disclosed. However, exact billing, refund and equipment-return obligations can depend on the service terms and applicable rules. Read the current contract rather than applying a rule from a different provider or service category.

Step 9: compare providers using one consistent scorecard

Once you have the facts, put providers side by side. Avoid giving a high score to a feature simply because it sounds advanced. Score each item against what your household actually needs. If a provider does not disclose a detail, mark it “not confirmed” and ask before ordering; do not silently treat missing information as a positive answer.

For a more deliberate decision, assign each category a priority from 1 to 3: essential, useful or low priority. A household that depends on video calls may mark upload and fault support as essential. A household that streams and downloads large files may place more weight on sustained download capacity and FUP. Someone renting a home for a short period may prioritise installation flexibility and cancellation terms over a small monthly discount.

Do not create a single numerical winner if the underlying information is incomplete. A plan with an unknown FUP or unclear installation charge should remain unconfirmed until the provider answers. The purpose of the scorecard is to make trade-offs visible, not to manufacture certainty.

Step 10: use official tariff and consumer information

TRAI provides an online tariff portal that lets consumers view tariff filings from telecom and internet service providers and compare reported offerings. The portal's purpose is to make filed tariff information available for informed comparison. Check the current portal at TRAI's tariff portal. Provider websites and order pages should still be checked for the latest offer, address-specific feasibility and applicable terms.

TRAI's Plans and Tariff FAQ says there is no single tariff plan that suits every subscriber; consumers should estimate expected usage and preferences before deciding. It also explains that providers have flexibility to offer different tariff combinations while following applicable transparency requirements. See TRAI Plans and Tariff FAQs.

Use official information to verify the regulatory and tariff context, then obtain the actual offer from the provider. Do not rely on an old comparison article, a social-media screenshot or a sales message that does not identify the plan's current terms.

Common broadband buying mistakes to avoid

Choosing the highest download speed without checking upload

This can leave a remote worker, creator or household with frequent cloud backups disappointed. Compare both directions and the typical speed information, not only the largest download number.

Assuming unlimited means no restrictions

Read the FUP threshold and post-limit speed. A high data allowance may be enough for your household, but you should know what happens when it is reached.

Comparing only the first month's price

Promotions, taxes, installation charges and advance-payment requirements can change the real cost. Calculate a 12-month or realistic stay-period total and show refundable deposits separately.

Believing a coverage map confirms installation

Ask for an exact address and building feasibility check. Confirm the final connection method and refund conditions if installation cannot proceed.

Assuming the included router will cover every room

Wi-Fi coverage depends on the home layout, device capability and router placement. Ask for the equipment model and its capabilities. Do not confuse the plan's wired access speed with the speed every wireless device will receive.

Ignoring support and cancellation terms

A small saving may not be worthwhile if repairs are difficult to log, relocation is expensive or equipment-return conditions are unclear. Understand how to get help and how to leave the service before you subscribe.

Trusting verbal promises without a record

Ask for important details in the provider's official email, app, order summary or tariff document. Save the plan page and the terms that applied on the order date. A clear record makes it easier to resolve misunderstandings about price, speed, FUP or installation.

A simple message to send a broadband provider before ordering

If the plan page does not answer your questions, send a concise written request. Ask the provider to confirm exact-address availability, the technology used to reach your premises, typical download and upload speeds, FUP threshold and reduced speed, all recurring and one-time charges, equipment ownership, installation timeline, complaint route, cancellation and relocation conditions. Request the latest tariff sheet and full terms.

Keep the reply with your order documents. If a salesperson gives a different answer by phone, ask them to confirm the change in writing before you pay. Avoid sharing unnecessary identity documents or payment information through unofficial personal messaging accounts; use the provider's official ordering and verification channels.

Frequently asked questions

How much broadband speed does a family need?

There is no single speed that suits every family. Estimate how many people will use the connection at once and whether they stream, attend calls, play games, download large files or upload backups. Choose a plan that supports those simultaneous activities with reasonable headroom, and compare upload as well as download speed.

Is fibre broadband always better than wireless broadband?

Not in every location or situation. Fibre can provide a direct wired access path with high capacity, while fixed wireless depends on radio conditions and local network capacity. Actual performance, availability, reliability and price depend on the provider and address. Compare the specific service that can be installed at your premises.

Does a higher Mbps plan guarantee faster Wi-Fi in every room?

No. The plan's speed and the home's wireless coverage are different parts of the connection. Router placement, walls, interference, device capabilities and network activity affect Wi-Fi performance. A higher broadband tier cannot automatically fix a weak wireless link.

What is FUP in broadband?

Fair-usage policy describes conditions that may apply after a specified amount of data is consumed in a billing cycle. Depending on the plan, speed may be reduced or another condition may apply. Check the exact threshold and the speed or charge after it is reached.

What does unlimited broadband mean in India?

The word alone is not enough to determine the conditions. Read the plan's FUP, data threshold, post-limit speed and any additional usage charges. TRAI says providers must disclose the fair-usage limit and the speed after it is exhausted.

Should I rent or buy the router?

Compare the total rental over the period you expect to keep the service with the purchase price, warranty, compatibility and replacement terms. Also check whether the provider requires its own ONT or router and whether third-party equipment is permitted. The cheapest option depends on ownership and support conditions.

Can I change my broadband plan later?

Ask the provider about available upgrades and downgrades, effective dates, charges and whether a change affects promotional pricing or the billing cycle. Do not assume every plan can be changed instantly or without conditions; confirm the current terms.

What should I do if the provider does not install after taking payment?

Use the provider's official complaint channel and keep the order number, payment receipt and written feasibility response. Ask for the reason, the expected installation date or the refund process. If the complaint is not resolved satisfactorily, consult the provider's appellate route described in TRAI's grievance-redressal information.

Where can I compare broadband tariffs in India?

TRAI's online tariff portal provides filed tariff information for comparison. Also check each provider's current official plan page and confirm address-level availability and complete terms before ordering.

The final decision: buy the plan that fits your address and your usage

A good broadband purchase is not a race to the biggest Mbps number. It is a decision based on evidence: the provider can actually connect your premises; the download and upload performance fit your household's busy-hour activities; the FUP is clear; the full cost is affordable; the equipment suits your home; and support, cancellation and relocation terms are understood.

Use the same scorecard for every provider. Mark missing answers as pending instead of guessing. Save the tariff and terms that were shown when you ordered. If the provider cannot confirm a material detail, pause the purchase until it does.

The most useful question to ask before subscribing is not "Which plan is fastest?" It is: "Which available service will reliably support the things my household needs, at this exact address, for a clearly understood total cost?" That is the comparison that helps avoid paying for capacity you do not need—or discovering restrictions after the installation team has left.
