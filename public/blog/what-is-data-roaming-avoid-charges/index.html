Internet in Everyday Life

What Is Data Roaming and How Can You Avoid Unexpected Charges?

Find out how mobile data roaming works, why charges vary by destination and how to prepare your phone before travelling.

You land in another country, switch your phone on and see a welcome message from a mobile network you have never heard of. Your phone may have connected to a local partner network. That is data roaming: your home mobile operator lets you use another operator’s network, then applies the roaming terms attached to your plan.

Why roaming can cost more than using data at home

Your operator may have agreements with networks in other countries, but those agreements do not make every destination part of your domestic allowance. One plan might include roaming in selected places; another might charge by the amount of data used or sell a daily travel pass. Calls and texts can have separate rules too.

There is no single worldwide roaming price. The country you visit, your home operator and the exact plan all matter. A plan that includes one destination may not include a neighbouring country.

Small background tasks can use data abroad

Maps, email, photo backups, app updates and video can all use mobile data. Some activity happens in the background, so the total may grow even when you are not actively browsing. Near a national border, a phone may also connect to a network in the neighbouring country. Check the network name and your operator’s travel message rather than relying only on the signal bars.

Check the details before you leave

Look up the roaming terms for every country on your itinerary. Confirm whether data, calls and texts are included; how much data is available; what happens when it runs out; and whether a daily pass starts automatically. Also check whether voicemail, call forwarding or receiving calls can cost extra under your plan.

Phone settings that can help

If you do not want to roam, turn off data roaming before departure. You can also turn off mobile data or use airplane mode and then enable Wi-Fi where permitted. Menu names vary across phones. If you use two SIMs, check which line is selected for mobile data and whether automatic data switching is enabled.

Should you use a travel eSIM or local SIM?

It can be a useful alternative, especially for a longer trip or when you need predictable data costs. Before buying, check that your phone supports eSIM or the local SIM size, is not locked to another operator, and works with the provider’s network. Some travel eSIMs are data-only, so they may not provide a local phone number or ordinary voice calls.

When you arrive, confirm which SIM is carrying data and whether your home line is still allowed to roam. Keeping your usual number active can be convenient, but it does not automatically make roaming free.
