Internet Resilience: How Networks Stay Connected During Power Cuts and Disasters

When a cyclone knocks down power lines, a flood cuts a fibre route or a landslide blocks access to a mobile tower, the internet can become more than a convenience. People need to contact family, receive warnings, coordinate medical help and find safe routes. Yet a network is not one tower or one cable. It is a chain of radio sites, power systems, fibre and microwave links, data centres, routing equipment, software, field teams and emergency agencies. If one critical part fails, a service can disappear even while other parts remain healthy. Network resilience is the work of preparing for those failures, keeping essential communication available where possible and restoring service quickly when damage occurs. This guide explains the engineering behind that work, what India is doing, and what households and organisations can realistically prepare for.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

1. What does internet resilience actually mean?

Network resilience is the ability of a communications system to prepare for disruption, continue providing essential services where possible, adapt when parts fail and recover within an acceptable time. It is broader than simply having a backup internet connection. A backup only helps if it is independent of the failure that affected the primary system.

Imagine two fibre cables entering a building. If both cables run through the same underground duct and a single excavator cuts that duct, the building loses both paths. On a network diagram there were two links; in the real world there was one shared point of failure. Resilience requires understanding the physical route, power supply, equipment, people and dependencies behind each connection.

There is also a difference between resilience and uninterrupted service. A severe disaster can damage multiple towers, power substations, roads, fibre corridors and buildings at the same time. No responsible operator can promise that every user will stay connected in every circumstance. The practical goals are to reduce the chance of widespread failure, preserve priority communications, provide alternatives and restore service safely and quickly.

2. A mobile connection depends on more than the nearest tower

When your phone connects to a mobile network, it communicates with a nearby base station. That radio site then needs a working connection to the rest of the operator's network. Traffic may travel over fibre or microwave backhaul to aggregation sites and the mobile core. The core handles functions such as subscriber authentication, mobility, sessions and routing. From there, data reaches the internet, an application platform or another operator.

Every part has dependencies. The radio site needs electricity and working equipment. The backhaul needs an intact cable or microwave path. The core needs power, software, cooling and links to other network systems. The application you are trying to reach may depend on a separate cloud provider or data centre. A mobile signal bar only tells you something about the radio link; it does not prove that the tower has a working route to the service you want.

Fixed broadband follows a similar chain. A home router connects to an optical network terminal or cable modem, then to an access network and the ISP's aggregation and core systems. A local power cut can switch off the home router even when the fibre network outside is healthy. Conversely, your router may remain powered by a UPS while a street cabinet, exchange or upstream fibre route has failed.

3. Backup power: keeping network equipment alive

Telecom equipment needs electricity even when the public grid is down. A typical site may use batteries for short-duration backup and a generator for longer outages, with solar or other power arrangements at selected locations. The exact combination depends on the site, local grid reliability, space, climate, access and service requirements.

Batteries can bridge the gap while grid power is restored or a generator is started. Generators can run longer, but they need fuel, maintenance, safe access and people who can reach the site. A fuel tank is not a guarantee if roads are flooded or a bridge is damaged. Solar can support some sites and reduce fuel dependence, but its output depends on sunlight, panel condition, storage and the size of the load. A hybrid system may be more useful than relying on one source.

There is no universal number of hours that every mobile tower can operate without grid electricity. Backup duration varies with battery capacity, age, temperature, radio load, generator availability, fuel reserves and maintenance. A site's backup may also power only selected equipment or services. An operator's emergency plan needs to account for the full site and its supporting transmission path.

Power resilience extends beyond the tower. Fibre exchanges, aggregation routers, data centres, network operation centres and emergency alert platforms need power too. A mobile site can remain on air but become isolated if the equipment at the other end of its backhaul has lost power. Resilience planning therefore maps power dependencies across the network, not just at individual towers.

4. Diverse fibre routes: why a second cable must take a different path

Fibre carries large amounts of data over long distances. It is fast and reliable, but physical cables can be cut by construction work, damaged by floods, buried by landslides or affected by fire and other hazards. A network with one route into a town or district may be vulnerable even if the cable itself is high quality.

Route diversity means arranging separate paths so that a single event is less likely to damage all of them. A resilient design may use different ducts, roads, bridges, railway crossings, river crossings, entry points or exchanges. It may also use microwave radio links as an alternate path where fibre is unavailable or damaged. The important question is not “How many links are shown?” but “Can one hazard take out all of these links at once?”

Mesh networks and ring topologies can allow traffic to move around a failed segment, but only if the alternate path has enough capacity and the equipment can switch traffic correctly. A route that is technically available but already overloaded may not preserve service quality. Operators need to test failover, maintain route maps and know which circuits share ducts, poles, power or buildings.

International connectivity has the same challenge at a larger scale. Submarine cables carry the majority of international data traffic, and a country or region that relies on a small number of landing points or cable systems can be exposed to a concentrated failure. The ITU's 2026 Submarine Cable Resilience report highlights physical risk, geographic concentration, repair delays and dependency on a limited number of cable systems as major concerns. Diverse cable routes, landing stations, terrestrial backhaul and repair coordination all contribute to resilience.

5. What happens when a mobile tower goes down?

If a base station loses power, suffers physical damage or becomes disconnected from its backhaul, nearby users may lose service or experience a weaker signal from a more distant site. Neighbouring cells may absorb some of the traffic, but they were not necessarily designed to serve the extra load. Congestion can rise, and devices may struggle to attach or maintain a call.

Operators can deploy a Cell on Wheels (COW) or another temporary mobile base station. A COW is a transportable unit that can be positioned where coverage is needed, connected to available backhaul and supplied with power. It can help restore local radio coverage while permanent sites are repaired. A temporary site still needs a working backhaul route, power, suitable positioning, permissions and a safe way for crews to reach it.

Other measures include mobile generators, temporary fibre, microwave links, portable equipment, leased circuits and priority repair teams. In remote areas, access can be the hardest part. A site may be technically repairable but unreachable while roads are blocked or weather conditions remain dangerous. Network restoration is therefore a logistics operation as well as an engineering task.

6. Emergency roaming: using another operator's network

Intra-Circle Roaming (ICR) is an arrangement that can allow a subscriber to use another operator's mobile network within the same telecom circle or region when their own provider's network is unavailable in an affected area. It can preserve access if one operator has a working site and another does not. It cannot help if all usable networks in the area are down, if the phone cannot receive a signal or if the relevant emergency arrangement has not been activated.

India's Department of Telecommunications has described ICR as part of its disaster response framework. In its October 2026 update after Cyclone Arnab, DoT said ICR remained operational across affected districts and advised users to keep automatic network selection enabled. If automatic selection does not connect, the department advised users to open mobile network settings and manually select another available network.

Emergency roaming is not the same as ordinary international roaming or a permanent promise that every phone can freely use every operator at all times. It is an operational arrangement that depends on network configuration, authorisation, the incident and the networks that remain available. The ITU's 2026 guide to developing national emergency roaming frameworks explains how regulators and operators can establish arrangements for users of affected networks to access voice, SMS and data through another national network during outages and disasters.

For users, the practical lesson is simple: leave automatic network selection enabled unless there is a reason not to. During an officially announced emergency, follow the instructions issued by the telecom authority. Manual network selection may help only when another compatible network is actually available.

7. Emergency alerts: SMS, Cell Broadcast and SACHET

Getting a warning to people is a different challenge from keeping the internet working. An alert system needs an authorised source, a message format, geographic targeting and one or more ways to reach the public. India uses the CAP-based SACHET Integrated Alert System, developed by C-DOT and operated with NDMA and other warning agencies. It supports the dissemination of disaster alerts through channels including SMS and Cell Broadcast.

SMS sends a message to individual mobile numbers. It can be delayed or queued when networks are busy, and it depends on the messaging route and delivery process. Cell Broadcast sends an alert from a cell site to compatible phones in a defined geographic area. It is designed for one-to-many warning delivery rather than sending a separate text to every number. A broadcast can reach many devices in a cell at once, but it still relies on the relevant cellular infrastructure being operational and the device being powered, compatible and within coverage.

India launched its indigenous Cell Broadcast System on 2 May 2026. The government said it was integrated with the CAP-based SACHET platform and intended to deliver geo-targeted alerts rapidly to mobile devices. The platform brings together warning agencies such as the India Meteorological Department, Central Water Commission and Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services with state disaster management authorities. The agencies are responsible for issuing and authorising their respective alerts; the system helps distribute them.

Cell Broadcast is not a substitute for radio, television, sirens, official websites or local emergency announcements. A phone may be switched off, out of coverage, damaged or unable to receive the alert. A disaster may also affect the infrastructure used to distribute warnings. Multiple channels and geographically redundant alert platforms are therefore important. Users should treat official alerts as instructions to act, not as proof that every other communication channel will remain available.

8. Data centres, cloud services and the hidden core of resilience

Modern internet services rely on data centres and cloud platforms. These facilities use backup power, uninterruptible power supplies, generators, redundant cooling and network connections to keep services operating during equipment or utility failures. Critical systems may be deployed across more than one facility or region so that an outage at one location does not take down the entire service.

But geographic redundancy must be real. Two servers in the same building do not protect against a building-wide flood or power failure. Two facilities in the same floodplain may share the same hazard. A recovery site may also depend on the same identity provider, DNS service, fibre corridor or cloud control plane as the primary site. Resilience engineers look for common dependencies that can defeat apparent redundancy.

Failover is not automatic magic. Applications need tested backups, replicated data, recovery procedures, access controls and people who know how to operate them. Replication can copy corrupted data or malicious changes if the recovery design is not careful. Some services can tolerate a short interruption; others need stricter recovery objectives. A business should define how much data it can afford to lose and how long a service can remain unavailable, then test whether its design meets those targets.

Even when a data centre stays online, users may be unable to reach it if local access networks, DNS, internet routes or electricity at home have failed. Resilience has to be considered end to end—from the user's device to the service and back again.

9. Satellite and radio: alternatives when terrestrial networks are damaged

Satellite communication can provide an alternative when terrestrial fibre, mobile towers or microwave links are unavailable. Emergency agencies may use satellite phones, portable satellite terminals or satellite backhaul to connect remote response teams. These systems can be valuable when roads, bridges and terrestrial routes are damaged, but they are not a universal replacement for mobile broadband.

Satellite terminals need power, suitable equipment, a clear view of the sky for many systems, service availability and the correct authorisation. Capacity can be limited, and weather, terrain, antenna placement and congestion can affect performance. A satellite phone may support voice or low-rate data but is not equivalent to a high-capacity home broadband connection. Different satellite systems offer different capabilities.

Amateur radio can also support emergency communications when commercial networks are unavailable. The ITU's 2026 Recommendation M.1042-4 encourages administrations to support amateur and amateur-satellite networks that can operate independently of other radio systems, including from temporary locations using local generators or battery power. This depends on trained licensed operators, suitable equipment, coordination with authorities and local rules. It is a complementary emergency capability, not an informal substitute for public telecom services.

Resilient emergency plans use a mix of tools. The right combination depends on the hazard, geography, who needs to communicate, how much data is required and how quickly equipment can be deployed.

10. India's approach: coordination, standards and real incidents

India's Department of Telecommunications uses its Standard Operating Procedure for telecom services during disasters, known as SOP-2020, as a national coordination framework. State Telecom Disaster Coordination Committees bring together DoT field units, telecom service providers, state authorities and disaster-response agencies. Measures described by the government include priority call routing for disaster relief agencies, ICR, temporary mobile base stations, emergency fibre repair teams and disaster control rooms.

India has also addressed the physical durability of equipment. In a Rajya Sabha answer on 21 August 2025 concerning climate resilience in disaster-prone regions, the Ministry of Communications referred to Telecommunication Engineering Centre environmental testing requirements under QM-333 and the BIS wind-load standard IS 875 (Part 3) for telecom towers. The same answer described backup arrangements including diesel generators, battery backups and solar-powered systems, temporary sites, redundant fibre routes and mesh connectivity.

A real incident shows how these measures work together. In its 30 October 2026 update after Cyclone Arnab affected parts of Andhra Pradesh and Odisha, DoT reported that power supply disruptions and localised optical-fibre cuts were key factors affecting network operations. It said overall telecom network availability in Andhra Pradesh had reached 99.18 per cent at the time of the update, while figures varied by affected district. ICR remained active and 11 Cell on Wheels units had been deployed. These are time-specific government-reported figures, not a guarantee of uninterrupted service during future storms.

Another lesson comes from the Konkan region, where floods and landslides can damage roads, power infrastructure and fibre routes together. The government has discussed climate-resilient equipment, tower wind-load standards, redundant connectivity and emergency restoration in its response to questions about disaster-prone coastal districts. Local hazard maps, terrain, access routes and the location of shared infrastructure should guide each operator's engineering plan.

11. How to design a resilient network or business connection

For a telecom operator, enterprise or public agency, resilience starts with a map of critical dependencies. The map should include physical routes, power sources, equipment locations, suppliers, cloud services, staff access and the communication systems used during restoration.

  1. Assess local hazards: Identify flood levels, storm surge, wind, landslide, earthquake, heat, fire and access risks for each critical site.
  2. Remove shared points of failure: Check whether supposedly separate fibre routes, power feeds, buildings or cloud regions depend on the same physical asset.
  3. Provide independent power options: Size batteries, generators, fuel storage and alternative power for the actual critical load and expected restoration window.
  4. Plan alternate connectivity: Use diverse fibre routes, microwave, satellite or other approved links where they make technical and economic sense.
  5. Prioritise essential traffic: Define which services, users and public agencies need priority during an emergency, and test the policy before an incident.
  6. Prepare temporary capacity: Identify where COWs, portable generators, spare radios, fibre repair kits and satellite terminals can be staged safely.
  7. Protect people and access: Plan for field-team safety, fuel delivery, permissions, transport, accommodation and communications when roads are blocked.
  8. Test recovery: Simulate a site outage, fibre cut, grid failure and loss of a data-centre region. Record how long failover and restoration actually take.
  9. Review after every incident: Update asset maps, fuel assumptions, contact lists and procedures using lessons from real outages and drills.

The World Bank's practitioner guide on resilient telecommunications infrastructure recommends risk-informed network design, asset protection and elevation, monitoring, emergency plans, backup energy and communication systems, and incident reporting. Its central message is that resilience measures should be chosen for local risks rather than copied as a one-size-fits-all checklist.

12. What can households do before a power cut or disaster?

Households cannot repair a fibre route or restart a mobile core, but a few preparations can make communication more reliable when networks are under stress.

  • Keep devices charged: Charge phones and power banks before a forecast severe-weather event. Use a reputable power bank and keep it dry.
  • Save essential contacts offline: Keep important phone numbers, medical contacts and local emergency information somewhere accessible if cloud accounts are unavailable.
  • Enable automatic network selection: This may help a phone use an available network when an official emergency roaming arrangement is active. Follow authority instructions if manual selection is advised.
  • Use text when calls are difficult: Short SMS or messaging updates may use less network capacity than repeated voice calls or video calls, although delivery is not guaranteed.
  • Conserve battery: Reduce screen brightness, close unnecessary apps, disable non-essential radios and use battery saver. Keep enough charge for emergency communication.
  • Keep another information channel: A battery-powered radio, official local broadcasts or sirens can provide information when mobile data is unavailable.
  • Download essentials in advance: Save offline maps, tickets, documents and key instructions where possible. Do not assume cloud files will always be reachable.
  • Use official information: Follow alerts from authorised disaster management and government sources. Avoid forwarding unverified messages or rumours.
  • Do not overload networks: Make essential calls, send concise updates and avoid unnecessary high-bandwidth uploads during a local emergency.

For emergencies in India, the national emergency response number is 112. Dial it from a phone wherever a mobile network is reachable; if one network is unavailable, use another available official emergency channel. If a situation is life-threatening, do not delay seeking help because one preferred app or network is unavailable.

13. What resilience cannot guarantee

Redundancy reduces risk; it does not remove it. A wide-area disaster can affect primary and backup routes at the same time. Batteries can be depleted, generators can run out of fuel, and field teams can be prevented from reaching damaged sites. A temporary tower can restore a signal but may not have enough capacity for everyone. Emergency roaming can help only where another operator still has usable coverage and backhaul.

Likewise, an emergency alert system cannot guarantee that every person receives or reads a warning. Cell Broadcast depends on a working cell site and a compatible, powered device. Satellite links need equipment and power. A data-centre recovery region may not contain the latest data if replication was delayed. A network can be available while a particular website or payment system is down.

Resilience should therefore be measured in layers: how many people and critical services remain reachable, what minimum service is available, how long backup resources last, how quickly failures are detected, how long restoration takes and whether the same weaknesses return after each event. A single uptime percentage does not tell the whole story.

14. The future of a more resilient internet

Climate risks, growing dependence on digital services, AI infrastructure and increasingly complex supply chains are changing how networks are planned. Operators are using better monitoring, automated fault detection, digital maps of assets, predictive maintenance and more flexible network architectures. These tools can help identify a fault and coordinate restoration, but they still depend on accurate data, safe automation and people who can act when physical infrastructure is damaged.

Future networks may combine fibre, mobile, satellite, Wi-Fi, private networks and edge systems more seamlessly. Emergency roaming frameworks can help subscribers move between available networks. Cell Broadcast and other public-warning channels can reach people in affected locations. More geographically diverse data centres and submarine cable routes can reduce dependence on a single site or corridor.

The strongest design is not the one with the most complicated technology. It is the one that understands local hazards, removes common failure points, protects people and critical equipment, tests alternatives and restores essential communication in a controlled way. A resilient internet is built before the storm arrives—and improved after every disruption.

Frequently asked questions

1. What is internet resilience?

It is the ability of networks and digital services to prepare for disruption, continue essential communication where possible, adapt to failures and restore service after an incident.

2. Can mobile towers work during a power cut?

Many sites have batteries, generators or other backup arrangements, but runtime depends on equipment, battery condition, traffic, fuel, temperature and site access. Not every site has the same backup duration.

3. Why does my phone show signal during an outage but the internet does not work?

The radio link to a tower may still be active while its fibre or microwave backhaul, power elsewhere in the network, core systems or internet route is unavailable.

4. What is a Cell on Wheels (COW)?

It is a temporary mobile base station that can be deployed to provide or strengthen coverage while permanent sites are damaged or unavailable. It still needs power and a working backhaul connection.

5. What is Intra-Circle Roaming during a disaster?

ICR can let a subscriber use another operator's network within the same telecom circle when their own operator is unavailable in an affected area, if the emergency arrangement is active and another network is working.

6. How can I try another mobile network during an emergency in India?

Keep automatic network selection enabled. If it does not connect and DoT has activated an emergency arrangement, follow official instructions; DoT has advised manual selection of another available operator where necessary.

7. What is the difference between SMS alerts and Cell Broadcast?

SMS sends messages to individual numbers. Cell Broadcast sends a message to compatible devices within a defined cell area simultaneously. Both depend on functioning alert and telecom infrastructure.

8. Does Cell Broadcast work if the mobile network is completely down?

No. The alert needs a functioning cell site and supporting systems in the target area, as well as a powered, compatible device within coverage. Other warning channels remain important.

9. Can satellite internet replace mobile networks during a disaster?

It can provide an alternative link in some situations, especially for response teams, but satellite equipment needs power, suitable placement, service capacity and authorisation. It is not a universal substitute for terrestrial networks.

10. Why are two fibre cables not always enough for resilience?

If both cables share a duct, bridge, exchange or other vulnerable route, one incident can cut both. True route diversity means reducing shared physical points of failure.

11. How do data centres keep websites running during disasters?

They may use UPS systems, generators, redundant network and cooling equipment, backups and geographically separate recovery sites. The design must also account for shared dependencies and tested data recovery.

12. What should a household prepare before a cyclone or flood?

Charge phones and power banks, save essential contacts and offline maps, keep automatic network selection enabled, have a battery-powered information source and follow official alerts.
