Broadband & ISP Insights

Fiber vs Cable vs DSL vs Fixed Wireless vs Satellite Internet: Which Connection Is Best?

Compare fiber, cable, DSL, fixed wireless and satellite internet by speed, upload performance, latency, reliability, availability and cost—and learn how to choose.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Choosing an internet plan can feel like comparing numbers that do not tell the whole story. One provider advertises a very high download speed, another promises symmetrical uploads, and a third offers coverage where no cable or fibre network reaches. The technology behind each service affects more than the headline speed: it influences latency, upload capacity, reliability, installation, equipment and where the connection can be delivered.

The main broadband access types are fibre, cable, DSL, fixed wireless and satellite. They solve the same problem—connecting a home or business to the wider internet—but use very different physical systems. Fibre carries data as light through optical strands. Cable and DSL use copper infrastructure. Fixed wireless sends data over radio between a local access point and customer equipment. Satellite services relay traffic through spacecraft in orbit.

There is no universal winner for every address. Fibre is often the strongest all-round choice where it is available at a sensible price. Cable can provide excellent download performance. DSL may remain useful in places with legacy telephone infrastructure. Fixed wireless can reach locations that are expensive to wire, while satellite can serve remote areas beyond practical terrestrial coverage. The best decision comes from matching measured service quality and contract details to the way a connection will be used.

Quick comparison: the five main broadband technologies

This table describes typical characteristics, not guaranteed speeds. Providers use different equipment, spectrum, network layouts and service profiles. A premium cable plan may outperform an entry-level fibre plan on download speed, while a congested wireless service may perform worse than a well-maintained copper line. Always compare offers available at the exact installation address.

Do not treat the technology label as a speed guarantee. A provider's advertised maximum is not the same as the speed a household will receive during a busy evening. Look for typical or median performance, upload speed, latency information and the terms used to define advertised results.

1. Fibre internet: light signals and high capacity

Fibre broadband carries information as pulses of light through thin strands of glass or plastic. The fibre can run all the way to a residence, to a building, to a street cabinet or to another distribution point. These arrangements are often described using terms such as FTTH (fibre to the home), FTTB (fibre to the building), FTTC (fibre to the cabinet) and FTTdp (fibre to a distribution point). The final section matters: a service marketed as fibre may still use copper or coaxial cable for part of the path.

Full-fibre connections are attractive because optical fibre has very high capacity and low signal loss over long distances compared with traditional copper access lines. Modern passive optical network systems can serve multiple premises through passive splitters. The word passive describes the optical distribution network's splitters; it does not mean that the entire internet route is dedicated to one household. Capacity is engineered and shared according to the provider's design and service tiers.

Fibre plans commonly offer high download speeds, and many networks can also provide strong upload speeds. Symmetrical plans are especially useful for video production, cloud backups, large file transfers, remote work, hosting and households where several people upload at once. The access technology itself does not guarantee that every plan is symmetrical, however. Providers may sell asymmetric tiers or apply service-specific limits.

Latency on fibre access networks is often low and consistent compared with older copper technologies. That can help interactive applications such as video calls, remote desktops and online games. Yet a low-latency fibre access link cannot eliminate delay caused by a distant server, congested interconnection, inefficient routing, overloaded Wi-Fi or a slow device. A speed test to a nearby server may look excellent while a particular application remains slow for reasons beyond the access line.

Fibre also has practical considerations. A home may need an optical network terminal (ONT), sometimes integrated with a router. The provider may need permission to install a cable, drill an entry point or place equipment near a power socket. Fibre itself does not carry power to the ONT, so a power cut can stop the home connection unless backup power is provided. A fibre outage can also occur because of a cut cable, damaged connector, failed optical equipment or a provider-side fault.

When comparing fibre plans, check whether the service is truly fibre to the premises, whether the router and ONT are included, the upload tier, installation charges, public IPv4 or IPv6 options, data policies and the provider's repair process. Ask whether the quoted speed is a line rate, a maximum, or a typical measured speed. A 1 Gbps plan does not mean every device will measure 1 Gbps over Wi-Fi.

2. Cable internet: coaxial networks and high download speeds

Cable broadband commonly uses hybrid fibre-coaxial (HFC) networks. Fibre carries traffic through much of the provider's network, while coaxial cable connects neighbourhood distribution equipment to homes. The coaxial portion was originally built for television distribution and can carry broadband using the Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification (DOCSIS) family of standards.

Cable services can offer very high download rates and are widely used in areas where cable television networks were built. DOCSIS generations and network upgrades influence the capacity available to a provider. The service tier, local plant condition, upstream design and available spectrum all affect what a subscriber can actually receive. It is inaccurate to assume every cable network has the same speed ceiling.

Many cable plans are asymmetric: download capacity is substantially higher than upload capacity. That may suit households that mainly stream, browse, download games and receive files. It can be less convenient for people who regularly send large video files, back up large libraries to cloud storage, host services or work with large remote datasets. Newer network upgrades can improve upstream performance, but availability differs by operator and location.

Cable networks can share capacity among users connected to the same local segment. This does not mean that every neighbour's activity directly subtracts a fixed amount from your plan. It means the provider has to engineer enough capacity for expected demand, and performance can vary when many subscribers are active. If a connection slows mainly during predictable busy periods, record results at several times and ask the provider whether the local segment is congested.

Latency on a modern cable network can be suitable for gaming, calls and real-time work. The important factors are not just average ping but also jitter, packet loss and latency under load. A cable connection may have a good idle ping but experience a large delay increase when someone uploads a large file. Quality-of-service or active queue management in the modem and router can help manage some forms of bufferbloat, but it cannot fix every provider-side capacity issue.

Most cable services use a cable modem, sometimes combined with a Wi-Fi router. Check whether the provider supplies a modem that supports the required DOCSIS version and plan speed. If using your own equipment, confirm the exact model is approved by the operator. Also check equipment rental, activation, installation, data allowance, upload tier and whether the provider uses carrier-grade NAT or offers public IP options.

3. DSL internet: broadband over telephone copper

Digital Subscriber Line (DSL) technologies transmit broadband data over copper telephone pairs. A DSL modem communicates with equipment at an exchange or street cabinet. A family of technologies exists, including ADSL, ADSL2+, VDSL and G.fast. They differ in frequency use, attainable data rates, reach and deployment requirements. The phrase DSL is therefore a broad category rather than one fixed speed.

Traditional DSL performance is strongly influenced by the copper line between the premises and the provider's equipment. Longer lines generally experience greater signal attenuation, which can reduce the data rate that can be maintained reliably. Cable quality, joints, interference, internal wiring and the specific DSL profile also matter. Two homes on the same advertised plan can receive different speeds because their physical lines are not identical.

Some VDSL and G.fast deployments place fibre closer to the customer and use copper only for the final section. Short copper runs can support higher speeds than long loops. This is why a nearby cabinet can make a significant difference compared with an older arrangement where copper runs back to a distant exchange. The actual performance depends on the distance and condition of that final copper segment.

DSL may be the only fixed broadband option in some rural or older service areas. It can still support email, web browsing, standard-definition video and basic work applications when the line is stable and the plan is adequate. It may struggle with several simultaneous high-resolution streams, large cloud backups or high-bandwidth uploads, especially on older or long lines.

When evaluating DSL, ask the provider for the expected speed range at your address rather than relying only on a national or regional plan headline. Check the estimated upload rate, whether the service is ADSL or VDSL, whether fibre has been extended to a cabinet, and whether the line has known faults. If your modem reports synchronisation rates, compare them with the service estimate, but remember that sync rate is not identical to application throughput.

DSL's future depends on local network investment and regulation. Some operators are retiring legacy copper networks as fibre expands; others continue to maintain DSL where replacement is not yet practical. A low-cost DSL contract may be reasonable as a temporary or light-use option, but consider whether a fibre or fixed-wireless upgrade is likely to become available during the contract period.

4. Fixed wireless broadband: a radio link to a nearby network

Fixed wireless access (FWA) connects a home or business to a provider's radio network. A customer terminal—sometimes an indoor gateway, sometimes an outdoor antenna—communicates with a base station. The base station then connects to the provider's wider network, often through fibre or microwave backhaul. FWA can use licensed mobile spectrum, dedicated fixed-wireless spectrum or other authorised frequency bands, depending on the country and operator.

FWA is useful because it can reduce the need to dig trenches or install a new cable to every property. It can serve new developments, rural communities, temporary sites and buildings where wired installation is difficult. Some services use 4G or 5G radio access, while others use proprietary point-to-point or point-to-multipoint systems. The label fixed wireless does not identify one single radio technology.

Performance depends on the signal path and network design. Distance to the base station, terrain, trees, buildings, antenna alignment, frequency band, channel width, interference and the number of active users can all affect throughput. Higher-frequency bands may provide substantial capacity but can be more sensitive to obstruction and require careful line-of-sight planning. Lower-frequency signals may travel farther or penetrate obstacles better but have different capacity characteristics.

Some FWA services are sold with unlimited data, while others apply data caps, fair-use policies, traffic management or speed reductions after a threshold. A provider may also manage capacity by limiting the number of customers in a sector. Ask whether the service is designed for indoor self-installation or requires an outdoor antenna, and whether installation includes a signal survey. A strong signal indicator alone does not guarantee that the provider's backhaul or radio sector has spare capacity.

FWA can be a credible alternative to wired broadband, but evaluate it using real-world tests. Ask for typical busy-hour speeds and latency, not just the maximum theoretical rate. Check whether the quoted speed is shared across a sector, whether the plan has a usage threshold, and whether the provider can move or realign the antenna if performance changes. If the service uses a mobile network, check whether it has a fixed-broadband priority profile or is treated like ordinary mobile data.

Weather effects vary by frequency and system. Rain attenuation can matter more at some higher microwave and millimetre-wave frequencies, while foliage growth, wet leaves, wind movement and new construction can change a radio path. Power is also required at the customer premises and network site. A local power outage can interrupt FWA unless backup batteries or generators are available.

5. Satellite internet: connectivity from orbit

Satellite broadband is designed to reach locations where terrestrial networks are unavailable, unreliable or uneconomical to build. A customer terminal communicates with a satellite, which relays traffic through a gateway or another part of the satellite network. The connection may use a geostationary (GEO) satellite or a non-geostationary satellite in medium-Earth orbit (MEO) or low-Earth orbit (LEO).

Geostationary satellites orbit at an altitude where they appear nearly fixed over one point on Earth. Their wide coverage can serve large regions, but the long distance travelled by signals introduces substantial propagation delay. This can make interactive applications feel slower even when download throughput is high. A video stream may work well after buffering, while a fast-paced game or remote-control session may be frustrating.

LEO satellites operate much closer to Earth and can offer significantly lower latency than traditional GEO broadband. However, a LEO network needs many satellites and sophisticated handovers, gateways and network management to provide continuous service. Actual latency and speed vary with satellite visibility, network load, gateway routing, weather, terminal placement and the provider's architecture. Do not assume every LEO service performs identically.

Satellite is often the only practical broadband option for remote homes, ships, isolated work sites and communities beyond terrestrial coverage. It can provide meaningful access for education, communication, cloud services and emergency connectivity. Yet it may involve a higher upfront equipment cost, a monthly service price, installation requirements and a clear view of the sky. Trees, buildings, roof geometry and other obstructions can interrupt the link.

Rain and other atmospheric conditions can affect satellite links, particularly at higher frequencies. Providers design systems with link margins and adaptive techniques, but severe weather can still reduce performance or cause outages. Snow or debris may also obstruct a terminal if it is not designed or installed to handle local conditions. Check the equipment's environmental rating and the provider's guidance for mounting, power and maintenance.

Satellite capacity is not unlimited. A provider serves many users over a finite set of radio resources and gateways. Network congestion, service prioritisation, data policies and coverage-cell demand can affect performance. Before buying, verify coverage at the exact address, the expected service tier, data terms, equipment return policy, installation costs, support availability and whether the plan is intended for residential or mobile use.

Speed is only one part of connection quality

Download speed measures how quickly data arrives. It matters for downloading files, loading media and receiving content from websites. Upload speed measures how quickly data leaves your connection. It matters for video calls, cloud backups, sending large files, livestreaming and remote work. A household that only checks download speed may miss the bottleneck that affects its daily experience.

Latency is the time taken for a packet to travel to a destination and for a response to return, commonly measured in milliseconds. Lower latency usually makes interactive applications feel more responsive. Jitter describes variation in packet delay. Packet loss means packets fail to reach their destination. Even a fast connection can feel poor if it has unstable latency, high jitter or recurring loss.

Run a test with a wired Ethernet connection where possible, then repeat over Wi-Fi in the rooms where the service is used. Test at more than one time of day. Use the same device and test server when comparing results, and pause large downloads or backups during the baseline test. A single result is a snapshot, not a full diagnosis. TEZZ CONNECT's internet speed test can help record download, upload and latency results, but a complete assessment should also include stability over time and the performance of the applications you actually use.

Latency to a nearby test server does not predict every route on the internet. The destination server, transit providers, peering, geographic distance and application architecture all contribute. A connection can have excellent local ping but still take a longer route to a cloud service hosted on another continent. Compare tests to more than one destination when investigating a specific service.

Shared capacity, dedicated capacity and the meaning of “fibre”

People sometimes assume fibre means a dedicated strand from the provider's core directly to one home. Residential networks are more varied. A passive optical network can share an optical distribution system among multiple customers, with traffic scheduled and capacity managed by the provider. Cable networks also share local capacity. Fixed wireless sectors share radio resources. Satellite beams and capacity pools are shared as well.

Shared access is not automatically bad. Networks are designed around statistical multiplexing: users do not all consume their maximum plan rate at the same moment. A well-engineered shared network can deliver consistent service at a lower cost than building a separate dedicated circuit for every home. The relevant question is whether the provider has provisioned sufficient capacity for the number and usage patterns of subscribers.

Businesses that need guaranteed performance may require a dedicated internet access product with service-level commitments, defined repair targets, fixed public addressing and stronger support. Such services are different from ordinary residential broadband, even if both use fibre. Compare the contract and service-level agreement rather than relying on the physical medium alone.

Which technology is best for different households?

For gaming and real-time applications

Start by comparing latency, jitter and packet loss, not just download speed. A stable fibre connection is often a strong choice. Cable can also perform well, especially when local congestion and upload-related bufferbloat are controlled. Modern LEO satellite may be usable for many games, but variability and obstruction can be a disadvantage. Traditional GEO satellite is generally less suitable for latency-sensitive play. Fixed wireless quality depends heavily on the radio path and network load.

For remote work and video calls

Upload speed and stability matter because your camera, microphone and shared screen send data upstream. Fibre with a good upload tier is often ideal. Cable can be suitable if its upload capacity is adequate. DSL may support one or two modest video calls but can be constrained by a low upstream rate. FWA and satellite can work when their signal and capacity are stable. Test at the times your team holds meetings.

For streaming and large downloads

Download capacity is important, but so is consistency during evening peak periods. Fibre and cable commonly offer high throughput. FWA may deliver strong results where the base station has capacity. Satellite can stream successfully if the plan provides enough data and throughput, though weather and congestion can affect quality. DSL may be adequate for one stream at a suitable resolution but can become limiting when several streams run together.

For rural and remote properties

Compare all available options rather than assuming satellite is the only answer. A local fibre build, fixed wireless provider, community network, upgraded DSL line or mobile broadband service may be available. Satellite becomes valuable where terrain and distance make terrestrial access impractical. Check line-of-sight, installation feasibility, power backup and repair arrangements before choosing.

For creators, cloud backups and large uploads

Look closely at upload speed, data caps and sustained throughput. A symmetrical fibre plan is often the most convenient. Some cable plans now provide stronger upstream tiers, but confirm what is available at your address. FWA and satellite can be constrained by shared radio capacity or data policies. DSL is usually the least attractive option for frequent large uploads.

How to compare broadband offers before you sign

  1. Confirm availability at the exact address. A provider may serve one street but not every building. Ask for a serviceability check, not only a postcode-level coverage map.
  2. Identify the actual access path. Ask whether the service is fibre to the premises, fibre to the building, HFC cable, DSL over copper, fixed wireless or satellite. Marketing language can be broad.
  3. Compare download and upload tiers. Write both numbers down. A plan with a large download headline may still have a modest upload limit.
  4. Ask for typical busy-hour performance. Seek typical speeds or measured performance during evening peak periods. Ask whether results are independently measured and how they are calculated.
  5. Check latency and stability. If gaming, calls or remote access matter, ask about latency, packet loss, jitter and congestion management. Request trial or cancellation terms if available.
  6. Read data and traffic policies. Look for data caps, fair-use limits, speed reductions, prioritisation and restrictions on hotspot or business use.
  7. Calculate the full cost. Include installation, activation, equipment rental, optional mesh systems, shipping, taxes, early termination and price changes after a promotional period.
  8. Check equipment compatibility. Confirm whether the modem, ONT, gateway, dish or antenna is included and whether you can use your own router. Check Ethernet port speeds if you are buying a gigabit or multi-gigabit plan.
  9. Understand support and repair. Ask how to report an outage, what diagnostics are available, whether there is local technician coverage and how long repairs typically take.
  10. Check the contract details. Review minimum term, renewal, cancellation, service address restrictions, public IP options and any service-level commitments.

Do not choose a plan solely because it has the largest advertised number. A lower-speed plan with consistent performance, adequate upload capacity and responsive support can be more useful than a faster plan that suffers from congestion or has restrictive terms.

Home equipment can become the bottleneck

The access technology is only one part of the connection. The modem or ONT, router, Ethernet cabling, Wi-Fi standard, device network adapter and server all influence the final result. A gigabit broadband plan connected to a router with a 100 Mbps WAN port cannot deliver gigabit throughput. An older Wi-Fi device may also measure far below the service tier even when the wired connection is fast.

For a fair baseline, connect a modern computer directly to the router with a suitable Ethernet cable. Check that the router's WAN and LAN ports support the expected speed. If using Wi-Fi, test near the router and on a supported band. Avoid interpreting a weak Wi-Fi result as proof that the ISP access line is slow. The guide to strong Wi-Fi signal but slow internet explains why wireless bars and internet performance are different measurements.

Also consider how the household uses the connection. Large uploads can fill an upstream queue and raise latency for everyone else. Cloud backup, game downloads, security cameras and operating-system updates can run in the background. A capable router with effective queue management can improve responsiveness under load. If Wi-Fi coverage is the problem, a wired access point or suitable mesh system may help; changing broadband technology will not automatically fix a poor in-home wireless layout.

Power, resilience and outage planning

Every broadband technology depends on powered equipment somewhere. Fibre needs powered provider equipment and a powered ONT or router at the premises. Cable uses powered distribution amplifiers and network nodes. DSL needs powered exchange or cabinet equipment and a modem. Fixed wireless depends on base stations and customer radios. Satellite needs a powered terminal, gateway infrastructure and network operations.

During a local power cut, the home router and modem usually stop unless they have backup power. Providers may have batteries or generators at network sites, but this does not keep a customer's equipment running. If connectivity is important for work, healthcare coordination or safety, consider a properly sized uninterruptible power supply and a backup connection on a different network path. A mobile hotspot may help, but it can share the same local tower or backhaul failure as a fixed wireless service.

Resilience also depends on physical routes. Two services that appear to be different brands may use the same underlying fibre duct, tower, wholesale network or upstream provider. Ask about diverse routing if continuity is essential. For ordinary households, a practical backup may simply be a mobile data plan from a different operator, with a tested hotspot device and a charged power bank.

Common broadband myths

“Fibre always means symmetrical gigabit.”

Fibre can support high and symmetrical rates, but the plan and network architecture determine the service sold. Some fibre plans are asymmetric or have lower entry-level tiers.

“Cable is always slow at night.”

Cable networks share capacity, but a properly engineered network can remain fast during peak hours. Measure the service before assuming congestion is present.

“DSL is always unusable.”

DSL performance varies widely. A short, clean VDSL line can be useful, while a long or degraded copper loop may perform poorly. Check the actual address estimate.

“A 5G fixed wireless signal guarantees fibre-like performance.”

Radio signal strength is only one factor. Sector capacity, spectrum, backhaul, obstructions and traffic management all affect throughput and latency.

“All satellite internet has the same latency.”

Orbit altitude and network architecture matter. GEO services generally have much higher propagation delay than LEO services, although actual performance still varies.

“The fastest download plan is always the best plan.”

Upload capacity, latency under load, data policy, reliability, support and total cost may matter more than a headline download figure.

A practical decision framework

If full fibre is available and the price, contract and provider support are reasonable, it is usually the first option worth comparing. Look for a suitable upload tier and confirm that the installation is fibre to the premises rather than a mixed copper arrangement. If fibre is unavailable, compare cable plans by upload capacity and busy-hour performance. Cable can be a strong everyday connection when the local network is well maintained.

Where cable and fibre do not reach, check whether a short-loop VDSL service or a reputable fixed-wireless provider can deliver stable performance. Ask for measured local results and confirm whether installation requires an outdoor antenna. For remote locations beyond practical terrestrial coverage, satellite may be the most realistic solution. Compare GEO and LEO options carefully, and understand the latency and data trade-offs.

Finally, use evidence from your own address. Ask neighbours about outages and peak-time performance, but treat their experience as a clue rather than a guarantee. Run repeated wired tests if you already have a service. Record download, upload, latency and packet loss. Read the contract. The best broadband connection is the one that reliably supports your actual use at a fair total cost—not simply the one with the biggest number in an advertisement.

Frequently asked questions

Is fiber internet always faster than cable?

Fiber services often offer higher upload capacity and strong performance, but a well-provisioned cable plan can deliver very high download speeds. Actual results depend on the provider, plan, network load, equipment and connection to the device.

Is cable internet good for gaming and video calls?

Cable can work very well for gaming and video calls when latency, jitter and packet loss remain low. Congestion on a shared local segment can affect performance at busy times, so test at the hours that matter to you.

Why is DSL slower farther from the exchange or cabinet?

Traditional DSL uses electrical signals over copper telephone pairs. Signal quality and attainable data rates are affected by line length, cable condition, interference and the specific DSL technology deployed.

Can fixed wireless replace fiber?

It can be a practical alternative where fiber is unavailable, especially with a clear radio path and a well-engineered network. Performance depends on distance, terrain, signal quality, spectrum, cell loading and weather-related conditions for some bands.

Is satellite internet suitable for online gaming?

Low-Earth-orbit satellite services can have much lower latency than traditional geostationary systems, but latency variation, obstruction, weather and network conditions still matter. Competitive real-time gaming is usually easier on a stable terrestrial connection.

Does fiber internet use a dedicated line to every home?

Not always. Many residential fiber networks use passive optical network architectures in which optical capacity is shared among subscribers through passive splitters. The service is still delivered over fiber, but the access architecture and contention design matter.

Which internet type has the lowest ping?

Fiber often provides the lowest and most consistent access-network latency among common fixed broadband technologies. However, routing, peering, server distance, congestion and home networking can outweigh the access technology in an individual test.

What should I check before switching broadband providers?

Compare typical busy-hour download and upload performance, latency, data limits, installation and equipment fees, contract terms, outage support, public IP options, coverage at your exact address and the provider's service-level commitments.

Can I use my own router with any broadband type?

Often, but compatibility varies. Fiber may require an optical network terminal, cable may require a compatible DOCSIS modem, DSL needs a supported DSL modem, fixed wireless may use provider-supplied outdoor equipment, and satellite normally needs its own terminal.

How can I tell whether my slow internet is caused by the access technology?

Test with Ethernet where possible, repeat at different times, compare download and upload, record latency and packet loss, and test more than one device. If wired results are consistently poor, ask the provider to investigate the access line, modem or network segment.
