What Is an Internet Exchange Point (IXP)? How Networks Connect and Exchange Traffic

Learn what an Internet Exchange Point does, how peering and transit work, why route servers matter, and how local interconnection can improve internet performance, cost and resilience.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

The internet is a network of networks

When you open a website, join a video call or stream a film, your data may cross several independently operated networks before reaching its destination. The internet is not one giant company network with a single control room. It is a global system of interconnected networks run by internet service providers (ISPs), mobile operators, cloud platforms, content delivery networks, universities, businesses, governments and many other organisations.

Those networks need places and arrangements through which they can exchange traffic. One important piece of this infrastructure is the Internet Exchange Point (IXP). An IXP provides a shared interconnection facility where multiple independent networks can connect and exchange internet traffic directly, according to their own routing and commercial policies.

IXPs are mostly invisible to everyday internet users. You do not subscribe to an IXP in the same way you subscribe to a broadband plan, and an IXP does not normally provide the last-mile connection into your home. Instead, it helps the networks that carry your traffic connect with one another more efficiently. When a local ISP and a content network exchange traffic at a nearby IXP, a request for local content may avoid a longer route through distant networks.

This guide explains what an IXP is, how it works, how peering differs from transit, what a route server does, and why local interconnection can matter for latency, cost and resilience. It also explains what an IXP cannot guarantee: joining an exchange does not automatically make every route shorter, faster or free.

What exactly is an Internet Exchange Point?

An Internet Exchange Point is a technical facility that enables independent networks—commonly identified by their Autonomous System Numbers (ASNs)—to interconnect and exchange internet traffic. In a typical public IXP, participants connect their routers to a shared Ethernet switching fabric. The fabric gives participating networks a common place to establish peering relationships without building a separate physical connection to every other participant.

Think of an IXP as a busy transport interchange. Many transport operators arrive at the same station, but each operator still decides where its vehicles go and which services it will run. The station provides the shared infrastructure; it does not own every route or decide every passenger's journey. Similarly, the IXP provides the switching environment, while each network decides which other networks to peer with and what routes to exchange.

The European Internet Exchange Association (Euro-IX) describes an IXP as a network facility that enables interconnection and traffic exchange between more than two independent Autonomous Systems. The distinction matters: an IXP is not simply any cable between two networks, nor is it an ISP, a transit carrier or a content host. It is a shared interconnection environment for networks that remain independently operated.

IXPs can be operated by non-profit organisations, industry associations, commercial neutral data-centre operators, academic institutions or other entities. Their governance models differ, but successful exchanges generally depend on reliable infrastructure, clear technical policies, neutral access rules and trust among participants.

How does an IXP work in practice?

A network that wants to participate arranges a physical or remote connection to the IXP. In a physical deployment, the network places a router in the exchange facility or connects through a carrier that reaches the facility. The router connects to an IXP switch port, usually over Ethernet or an agreed optical interface. The IXP switch fabric provides layer-two connectivity among member ports.

After connecting, the participant configures Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) sessions with other networks. BGP is the routing protocol used between Autonomous Systems to exchange information about which IP address prefixes they can reach. Participants can establish bilateral sessions with individual peers or use a route server to exchange routing information with many participating networks.

When two networks agree to peer and exchange routes, their routers can select paths that send eligible traffic across the IXP fabric. The actual user packets then travel from one participant's router, through the switching fabric, to the other participant's router. The route server, if used, helps distribute routing information; it is not normally in the data path and does not forward the traffic itself.

That separation between control plane and data plane is important. BGP messages help networks learn routes and make policy decisions. Once a route is selected, ordinary IP packets move through the routers and switches along the chosen path. A failure in a route-server session does not necessarily stop existing traffic from flowing, because participants may have other BGP sessions or routes. The effect depends on each network's configuration and redundancy.

An IXP may also provide additional services such as traffic statistics, looking glasses, route collectors, monitoring, remote peering access, IPv6 support, or shared route servers. These services vary. The essential role remains interconnection: helping networks exchange traffic through a common facility.

Peering, transit and private interconnection: what is the difference?

Peering

Peering is an arrangement in which two networks agree to exchange traffic between their respective networks and customers. It is often settlement-free, meaning neither network pays the other a per-traffic carriage fee, but paid peering arrangements also exist. The agreement may define traffic ratios, capacity, locations, route announcements, operational contacts and other conditions.

Peering does not mean that two networks exchange all internet traffic with each other. A peering relationship generally covers traffic that each network and its customers originate or serve, according to the agreed policy. If a destination is not reachable through that peer, the network may use another peer or a transit provider.

Transit

Transit is a service in which one network pays another network to carry traffic to and from the wider internet. A customer network buys transit so it can reach destinations beyond its direct peers. Transit providers often have broad connectivity to many other networks and can advertise routes that make those destinations reachable.

Transit is essential because it is impractical for every network to establish a direct peering relationship with every other network on the planet. Even a large ISP will typically combine peering, transit and private interconnections. The mix depends on geography, traffic volume, commercial terms, performance requirements and network strategy.

Private Network Interconnects

A Private Network Interconnect (PNI) is a dedicated connection between two networks, often established in a data centre or carrier hotel. Large content platforms and ISPs may use PNIs when traffic volumes justify dedicated capacity, predictable performance or a specific operational arrangement. A PNI can coexist with public peering at an IXP.

Remote peering

Remote peering allows a network to connect to an IXP without placing its own router in the same building. A third-party carrier transports the participant's traffic to the exchange. This can reduce the cost and complexity of joining an exchange, but the remote participant must understand the transport path, service-level commitments, redundancy and any additional delay introduced by the carrier.

These mechanisms are complementary. A network may use public peering for many local routes, private interconnection for high-volume partners, and transit for broad global reach. No single method replaces all the others.

What is a route server—and does it carry internet traffic?

Imagine an IXP with 100 participating networks. If every participant wanted to exchange routes with every other participant through separate bilateral BGP sessions, the number of possible relationships could become difficult to manage. A route server can simplify this by allowing participants to establish a BGP session with the exchange's route-server service and receive routes from many other participants that have opted into the service.

The route server is a control-plane tool. It receives routing announcements from participants, applies the IXP's policies and distributes eligible routes to other participants. It generally does not forward the actual user packets. The data traffic still passes across the IXP switching fabric directly between participant routers.

Route servers lower the operational barrier to multilateral peering, particularly for smaller networks that may not have the staff or time to configure and maintain dozens or hundreds of bilateral sessions. They do not force participants to peer with every other member. A participant can apply filters, choose which routes to accept and decide whether to use the route-server service at all.

Route servers also do not remove the need for good routing policy. Participants must validate route announcements, apply appropriate filters, monitor sessions and maintain accurate contact information. A route server can distribute an incorrect or unwanted route if safeguards and participant configuration are inadequate. Operational discipline remains essential.

Why do IXPs matter for latency and internet performance?

Latency is the time taken for data to travel through a network path and for a response to return. A shorter, more direct route can reduce propagation and forwarding delay, although the result depends on the path and equipment involved. If two networks exchange traffic locally at an IXP, packets may avoid travelling through a distant transit provider or an unnecessary international route.

Consider a user connected to an ISP in one city who accesses a service hosted by a content provider in the same metropolitan area. If the ISP and content provider peer at a local IXP, traffic may be exchanged nearby. If they do not have a suitable interconnection, the traffic could travel through a regional or international transit network before returning to the same city. That longer route may add latency and consume more upstream capacity.

Local peering can improve the delivery of locally hosted content, video, gaming traffic, cloud services and other high-volume applications. Content delivery networks often connect at multiple exchanges so they can place caches closer to users and exchange traffic with access networks efficiently. But the benefit is conditional: the relevant networks must actually peer, advertise the right routes, have sufficient capacity and select the IXP path for the traffic in question.

An IXP does not guarantee that every packet takes the shortest geographic path. BGP routing is policy-driven. Networks may prefer a route for commercial, security, capacity or operational reasons. A path that appears geographically longer may be selected because it offers better reliability or contractual terms. The IXP creates an interconnection option; participants decide how to use it.

Users should also remember that internet performance depends on more than interconnection. Wi-Fi interference, home router queues, access-network congestion, DNS resolution, application server load and the route beyond the IXP can all affect the experience. A good IXP cannot fix a weak wireless signal or an overloaded game server.

How IXPs can reduce network costs

Networks need capacity to carry traffic to other networks. If an ISP sends a large amount of traffic through a paid transit provider, it incurs costs based on its commercial agreement. When the ISP can exchange eligible traffic directly with another network at an IXP, it may reduce the volume of traffic it needs to purchase through transit.

Shared switching infrastructure can also be more economical than building a separate physical link to every potential peer. Instead of arranging individual circuits for every network relationship, participants connect to a common fabric and establish routing sessions over that connection. The exchange operator can spread the cost of the facility, power, cooling, switching and operations across its participants.

Cost savings are not automatic. Networks may pay membership fees, port fees, cross-connect charges, transport costs for remote peering, router costs and staff expenses. Some peering arrangements are paid. A network must compare the total cost of joining and operating at an exchange with the transit capacity it may avoid purchasing. The value depends on traffic volume, peer availability, route quality and geography.

For smaller ISPs, a local exchange can make direct interconnection practical where building private links would be too expensive. For large networks, multiple IXPs and private interconnects can form part of a broader traffic-engineering strategy. In both cases, a healthy exchange ecosystem can create more choices for how traffic moves.

IXPs and internet resilience

Resilience is the ability of a network ecosystem to continue operating when equipment, links, facilities or external routes fail. Local interconnection can contribute to resilience by creating additional paths between networks and reducing dependence on a single upstream route for local traffic.

If international connectivity is disrupted, networks that can still reach one another locally may continue exchanging domestic or regional traffic. This does not mean the entire internet remains available. A local exchange cannot replace a damaged access network, restore a disconnected data centre, or make overseas services reachable when all paths to them are unavailable. It can preserve some local connectivity when the wider network is impaired.

IXP resilience depends on the design of the facility. Important considerations include redundant switches, diverse power feeds, backup generators or batteries, cooling, physical security, multiple fibre entry routes, spare capacity, configuration backups and tested incident procedures. A single building or cable duct can become a point of failure if participants have no alternative route.

Participants also need diverse interconnection. Connecting to an IXP does not automatically provide route diversity if all links enter the same facility through the same carrier or if the network relies on one router. Larger networks may connect at multiple sites, use redundant routers and maintain alternative peering and transit paths.

Good operational coordination is part of resilience. Participants need working network operations contacts, clear maintenance notices, incident communication and a process for handling route leaks or misconfigurations. The technical switch fabric is only one element of a functioning interconnection community.

What equipment and facilities does an IXP need?

At the centre of a traditional IXP is a switching fabric. It may use one or more Ethernet switches designed for high-throughput packet forwarding. Participants connect their routers to the fabric using optical or copper interfaces that match the exchange's technical specifications. Larger exchanges may operate across multiple data centres or metropolitan sites using carefully engineered inter-site links.

The facility also needs reliable power, cooling, physical access controls, fire protection and appropriate cabling infrastructure. Fibre cross-connects allow participants and carriers to reach the exchange. If the IXP offers remote peering, it may connect to transport providers that extend access beyond the building.

Operational systems can include network management, telemetry, traffic graphs, route-server platforms, looking glasses, route collectors, configuration management, alerting and documentation. These tools help the operator and participants detect failures, understand traffic patterns and troubleshoot routing issues.

Not every IXP has the same design or service catalogue. A small regional exchange may operate a simple switching fabric with a compact team. A large metropolitan exchange may have multiple sites, high-capacity ports, route servers, IPv4 and IPv6 peering LANs, remote access services and extensive operational procedures. The right design depends on the local network community and traffic demand.

How does an ISP or content network join an IXP?

  1. Identify the exchange. Review the IXP's location, membership criteria, technical policies, port speeds, fees, available carriers and participant list.
  2. Confirm the business case. Estimate traffic that could be exchanged locally, potential transit savings, performance benefits and the total cost of connectivity.
  3. Arrange physical or remote access. Order a port and cross-connect, or arrange transport through a remote-peering provider. Confirm diverse paths if resilience is important.
  4. Prepare network addressing and routing. Obtain the required peering LAN details, configure router interfaces and establish BGP sessions with selected peers or route servers.
  5. Apply routing policy. Filter prefixes, set import and export policies, validate announcements and document which routes should be exchanged.
  6. Test and monitor. Confirm BGP sessions, route visibility, packet forwarding, IPv4 and IPv6 reachability, traffic levels and failover behaviour.
  7. Maintain the relationship. Keep operational contacts current, monitor capacity, review route policies and coordinate planned maintenance or incident response.

Joining an IXP is a network engineering and commercial decision, not a consumer configuration. A home user cannot normally choose an IXP in a router menu. The ISP and content networks make interconnection decisions on behalf of the networks they operate.

How can an IXP affect ordinary internet users?

Users usually experience the effects indirectly. Better local interconnection may reduce the distance traffic travels to reach a nearby content provider, improve access to locally hosted services, reduce congestion on upstream links and provide alternative paths when part of the wider network fails.

However, the impact differs by ISP, destination and time. Two customers in the same city may use different providers with different peering arrangements. One ISP may exchange traffic directly with a streaming platform at a local IXP, while another may reach it through a regional transit provider. Even within one ISP, some destinations may use local peering while others use international transit.

A speed test cannot tell you by itself whether your ISP peers at a particular IXP. Speed-test servers may be hosted inside an ISP network, at a data centre or behind a content delivery network. A nearby test server can measure the local access path well but may not represent the route to a distant application.

For users, practical checks include testing more than one destination, comparing performance at different times, using wired Ethernet to isolate Wi-Fi issues and checking whether a problem affects only one service. If only one platform is slow, the issue may be its server, CDN, route or interconnection rather than the entire broadband connection.

IXP myths and common misunderstandings

“An IXP is another name for an ISP.”

No. An ISP sells or provides internet access to customers. An IXP provides a shared facility where independent networks interconnect. Some organisations may operate more than one type of service, but the functions are different.

“Every network at an IXP peers with every other network.”

No. Participants decide which networks to peer with and what routes to exchange. Some use bilateral agreements; others use route servers; some participants may not exchange traffic with particular members.

“The route server forwards all IXP traffic.”

Usually not. A route server distributes routing information through BGP. User packets normally travel directly across the IXP switching fabric between participant routers.

“Peering is always free.”

Settlement-free peering is common, but paid peering and other commercial arrangements exist. Port, cross-connect, transport and membership costs may also apply.

“Joining an IXP guarantees lower ping.”

It creates a potential direct route. The networks must peer, advertise suitable routes and select that path. Server location, congestion and routing policy still matter.

“An IXP keeps the whole internet running during an international outage.”

It may help local networks continue exchanging reachable local traffic, but it cannot restore disconnected access links, unavailable services or routes that depend on the failed international connection.

The future of internet exchange points

Internet traffic continues to grow and change as video, cloud computing, artificial intelligence, connected devices and real-time applications increase demand for network capacity. IXPs are adapting through higher-capacity switching, more distributed locations, remote peering, automation, telemetry and stronger support for IPv6.

Content delivery networks and cloud platforms increasingly place infrastructure closer to users. Local interconnection can help these services exchange traffic with access networks without sending every request through distant transit routes. Edge computing may create additional reasons for networks to connect in regional facilities, although not every edge workload requires an IXP.

Resilience and security are also receiving more attention. Operators need to protect management systems, control physical access, monitor routing changes and coordinate responses to incidents. Route filtering and validation remain responsibilities of each network; an exchange fabric alone cannot prevent route leaks or malicious announcements.

IXPs are also part of a wider policy and development conversation. A successful exchange needs more than a switch: it needs participating networks, neutral governance, technical skills, reliable data-centre facilities, sustainable operating costs and trust. In areas with limited interconnection, support for local peering ecosystems can help make connectivity more efficient and resilient.

The bottom line

An Internet Exchange Point is a shared interconnection facility where independent networks can exchange traffic. It helps ISPs, mobile operators, content networks and other Autonomous Systems establish public peering over common switching infrastructure. Route servers can make it easier to exchange routing information with many participants, while the actual packets generally move directly between participant routers across the exchange fabric.

IXPs complement transit and private interconnection rather than replacing them. They can help keep local traffic local, reduce reliance on paid upstream capacity, improve performance for some routes and provide additional resilience. The benefits depend on which networks participate, the routes they exchange, capacity, location, operational quality and the policies each network applies.

For everyday users, an IXP is part of the hidden infrastructure behind a responsive and resilient internet. It is not a speed booster that can be switched on at home, but a key meeting point in the network of networks that makes global connectivity possible.

Frequently asked questions

What does IXP stand for?

IXP stands for Internet Exchange Point. It is a network facility where multiple independent networks interconnect and exchange internet traffic.

Is an IXP the same as an ISP?

No. An ISP provides internet access to customers. An IXP provides shared infrastructure for networks to interconnect with one another.

Does an IXP provide internet to homes?

Usually not. Home users receive internet access from an ISP or another access provider. The provider may connect to content networks and other ISPs through an IXP.

What is the difference between peering and transit?

Peering is an agreement between networks to exchange traffic between their networks and customers. Transit is a paid service that carries traffic to and from the wider internet.

Do all IXP members exchange traffic with one another?

No. Each participant decides which peers to connect with and which routes to accept or announce. A route server can simplify multilateral peering but does not force participation.

Does a route server carry the data packets?

Normally no. A route server distributes BGP routing information. User traffic usually passes directly between participant routers over the IXP switching fabric.

Can an IXP reduce internet latency?

It can help when two networks exchange traffic locally through the IXP instead of using a longer route. The actual result depends on peering agreements, routing policy, capacity and destination.

Is peering always free?

No. Settlement-free peering is common, but paid peering and other commercial terms exist. Participants also pay for ports, cross-connects, transport and operations.

Can an IXP keep local internet working during an international cable outage?

It can help connected local networks exchange traffic that remains reachable locally, but it cannot restore international destinations or access links that depend on the failed cable.

Can a home user choose which IXP their internet traffic uses?

Usually not directly. The ISP and destination networks decide their interconnection and routing policies. Users can compare provider performance, but cannot normally select an IXP in home settings.
