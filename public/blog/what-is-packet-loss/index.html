Internet Problems & Fixes

What Is Packet Loss? Causes, Tests and How to Fix It

Learn what packet loss means, why it disrupts gaming, video calls and browsing, how to test it, and how to isolate Wi-Fi, router, device or ISP problems.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Packet loss happens when data packets sent across a network fail to reach their intended destination. It can make a video call freeze, turn a smooth online game into a stuttering experience, interrupt a remote desktop session or force an application to resend information. A connection can show a high download speed and still feel unreliable when packets disappear along the way.

This guide explains packet loss in practical terms, how it differs from latency and jitter, what causes it, how to test it without jumping to conclusions, and how to work out whether the fault is in your device, Wi-Fi, router, internet provider or a remote service. The goal is not to chase a perfect-looking number; it is to identify repeatable loss that affects the applications you use.

What is a network packet?

When your device communicates over the internet, information is divided into smaller units called packets. A packet carries a portion of the data along with addressing and control information that helps networks deliver it. A video call, web page, game session or file transfer may involve many packets travelling in both directions.

Routers and other network equipment examine packet information and forward packets towards the next network hop. The packets do not necessarily travel as one continuous physical stream or follow an identical path every time. At the destination, protocols and applications use the received information to reconstruct data, keep a session running or decide whether missing information needs to be sent again.

If a packet is dropped, corrupted beyond recovery or never reaches the destination, that portion of the communication is missing. Some protocols can recover by retransmitting data. Other real-time applications may conceal the gap, skip information or continue with a degraded experience because waiting for a replacement would be worse than moving on.

What does packet loss percentage mean?

Packet loss is commonly expressed as a percentage: the number of packets that did not arrive divided by the number sent, multiplied by 100. If a test sends 100 packets and 2 receive no response, the measured loss is 2 percent. If 1 packet out of 200 is missing, the measured loss is 0.5 percent.

That calculation is simple, but the result is only meaningful when you understand the test. It may represent ICMP echo requests, application media packets, or a specific monitoring probe. A short test can miss an intermittent fault. A test to one destination may show a different result from a test to another destination because the routes, filtering policies and server behaviour differ.

There is no universal percentage that guarantees every application will work well. A small amount of loss can be noticeable in interactive voice, video or gaming, particularly when it occurs in bursts. A file transfer may recover through retransmission and complete successfully, but take longer. The timing, direction, duration and type of traffic matter as much as the average percentage.

Packet loss, latency and jitter are not the same

These three measurements describe different aspects of network quality. Latency is the time it takes data to travel between endpoints and back when measured as round-trip time. Jitter describes variation in packet delay. Packet loss means packets fail to arrive at the destination or are discarded before an application can use them.

A connection can have low average latency but still suffer occasional packet loss. It can have no observed loss in a short test but high latency that makes controls feel delayed. It can also have acceptable average delay while jitter causes voice or video packets to arrive unevenly. The symptoms can overlap: a call may sound broken because of loss, jitter, congestion, or a combination.

For a useful diagnosis, record download and upload throughput, latency, jitter and packet loss where available. A speed test alone is not a complete network-health test. For background on these related measurements, see the TEZZ CONNECT guides to video-call lag and different speed-test results.

What packet loss feels like in everyday use

Video and voice calls: Missing media packets can produce clipped words, robotic audio, frozen video, brief silences or a participant who appears to jump from one position to another. Real-time communication tools cannot always pause long enough to recover every missing packet without making conversation feel unnatural.

Online gaming: You may see rubber-banding, delayed actions, characters teleporting, inconsistent hit registration or sudden disconnects. The game may continue to report a good frame rate because rendering performance and network delivery are separate systems. A fast connection does not guarantee that every game update arrives on time.

Streaming: Many streaming services buffer ahead and can request missing data again. That recovery can hide brief loss, but sustained disruption may cause buffering, a reduction in picture quality or playback errors. Live streams have less time to build a buffer than on-demand programmes.

Browsing and downloads: A web page may load slowly, a secure connection may retry, or a download may take longer than expected. Reliable transport protocols can retransmit missing data, so the user may notice delay rather than an obvious missing piece of content.

Remote work and cloud apps: Remote desktop sessions, VPN connections, file synchronisation and interactive business applications can feel unstable. Repeated retransmissions and timeouts may interrupt a workflow even when a basic speed test reports a high rate.

Common causes of packet loss

Network congestion: When traffic arriving at a link or device exceeds the capacity available to forward it, queues can fill. Equipment may then discard packets. Congestion can happen on a home Wi-Fi channel, a router's WAN connection, an ISP access network, an interconnection point or a remote service network. It may be more visible at busy times, but time-of-day patterns alone do not prove congestion.

Weak or interfered-with Wi-Fi: Wireless signals share radio airtime. Distance, walls, floors, interference from nearby networks, channel contention and poorly placed access points can cause frames to be corrupted or retransmitted. A device may show connected Wi-Fi bars while the wireless link is still unstable. Test near the router and compare with Ethernet before blaming the broadband line.

Faulty cables or connectors: Damaged Ethernet cables, loose connectors, poor fibre terminations, damaged coaxial wiring or failing ports can create errors or link instability. A cable may work intermittently, so inspect physical connections and try a known-good cable when practical.

Router, modem or access point problems: Overheating, old firmware, resource exhaustion, failing hardware, overloaded wireless radios or configuration errors can affect packet forwarding. Reboots may temporarily clear a problem but do not establish the root cause. If the issue repeatedly returns, collect evidence before replacing equipment.

Device limitations or software: Outdated network drivers, faulty adapters, power-saving settings, security software, VPN clients, firewall policies or a heavily loaded device can interfere with traffic. Microsoft documents local packet drops caused by resource limits, policy inspection, invalid packets and missing routes.

ISP routing, maintenance or upstream faults: Loss can occur beyond the home router, including on an access link, a provider's internal network or an upstream route. A local user generally cannot see every router or queue on that path. The ISP may need to correlate timestamps and test results with its own monitoring.

Remote destination behaviour: A server, firewall, content delivery network or service may rate-limit or deprioritise certain probes. Some routers deliberately do not answer every ping request addressed to their control plane. A missing ping response does not automatically mean ordinary application traffic is being dropped at that hop.

Can packet loss happen on download and upload separately?

Yes. Network traffic travels in both directions, and the cause may affect one direction more than the other. A weak Wi-Fi uplink from a phone to a router can affect packets sent from the device, while congestion or a fault further along the path may affect packets returning to it. Video calls need a usable path in both directions, so one-way loss can still damage the conversation.

When testing, note which endpoint sends the probes and which endpoint receives them. A simple ping reports whether replies returned to the sender; it does not always tell you whether the request or reply was lost. Application diagnostics, endpoint packet captures or tests from both sides can help distinguish direction when the distinction matters.

How to test for packet loss at home

Start with a repeatable baseline. Stop large downloads, cloud backups and unnecessary streaming for the first test. Record the date, local time, device, connection type, destination and result. If the problem happens only during a call or game, also note the application and the time the symptom occurred.

Step 1 — Compare more than one device. Test from a second phone or computer on the same network. If only one device shows symptoms, investigate its adapter, drivers, software, VPN and local settings before changing the router.

Step 2 — Compare Wi-Fi with Ethernet. If possible, connect a computer directly to a router LAN port using a known-good cable. Disable Wi-Fi on that computer during the wired test so the route is clear. If wired performance is stable but Wi-Fi is not, focus on wireless coverage, interference, access-point load and client hardware. If both are affected, continue upstream.

Step 3 — Run a sustained ping test. On Windows, open Command Prompt and run ping -n 100 1.1.1.1 or ping -n 100 8.8.8.8. On macOS or Linux, use ping -c 100 1.1.1.1. These are examples of public destinations, not guaranteed diagnostic authorities. Check the summary for sent, received and lost packets. Repeat the test at another time and, if possible, to a second destination.

Step 4 — Test your router's local address. Find the default gateway address in your device's network details and ping that address. Loss between a device and its gateway points toward the local device, Wi-Fi, cable or router LAN side. No loss to the gateway but loss to multiple external destinations suggests the issue may be further along the path, although it is not proof of an ISP fault.

Step 5 — Compare during and outside the problem. Repeat the same test when the connection feels normal and when symptoms occur. A single clean one-minute test cannot rule out a fault that happens for a few seconds every ten minutes. Keep a small log rather than relying on memory.

Example ping commands and how to read them

On Windows, this command sends 100 ICMP echo requests to the example address:

ping -n 100 1.1.1.1

On macOS or Linux, the equivalent count option is commonly:

ping -c 100 1.1.1.1

Look for the packet summary. If 100 packets were sent and 100 replies were received, the test observed zero loss for that destination and period. If 98 replies arrived, the observed loss was 2 percent. Record the minimum, maximum and average round-trip times too, but do not confuse delay variation with packet loss.

Some networks block or rate-limit ICMP. A destination may ignore ping while still serving websites and apps correctly. Conversely, a destination can respond to ping while a particular application protocol has trouble. Treat ping as one useful signal, not a definitive verdict on the entire internet connection.

Use traceroute or MTR carefully

Traceroute (called tracert on Windows) shows the sequence of network hops that respond to probes on a route towards a destination. MTR combines repeated probes with a route view, making it useful for observing latency and response patterns over time. These tools can help support teams understand where a path changes or where a problem may begin.

However, intermediate routers may deprioritise or filter diagnostic probes addressed to themselves while forwarding normal traffic. A hop showing 20 percent unanswered probes does not establish 20 percent end-to-end packet loss if later hops and the final destination respond normally. Look for loss that continues to later hops and the destination, and compare results across multiple runs. Even then, route changes and probe handling can complicate interpretation.

Cloudflare's troubleshooting guidance recommends traceroute for connection timeouts and MTR for more detailed route and packet-loss observations. It also notes that ping timeouts can occur because ping requests are limited. Share complete output and timestamps with support rather than sending only a cropped line that appears to show loss.

Why a speed test may not show packet loss

A speed test primarily estimates throughput under its own test conditions. Some tests include latency or loaded-latency measurements, and some provide packet-loss diagnostics, but the feature set varies. A short throughput test may not expose a brief loss burst, a route-specific issue or a problem limited to a real-time application.

Test servers also matter. A nearby server may use a different route from the game server, meeting platform or cloud application that is causing trouble. A good result to one server is useful evidence, but it does not guarantee every destination is reachable with the same quality.

Use speed tests to understand capacity, and use repeatable ping, application statistics or network diagnostics to investigate loss. The most useful evidence comes from tests that reproduce the real problem and compare the local network with the wider path.

How to reduce packet loss on Wi-Fi

Move closer to the router and test in the same room. Place the access point in an open, central location rather than inside a cabinet, behind a television or beside large metal objects. If the router supports multiple bands, compare 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz where available. Higher-frequency bands may offer more capacity at shorter range; they are not automatically better through several walls.

Reduce unnecessary wireless contention during diagnosis. Pause large transfers, disconnect devices that are not needed for the test and check whether the problem changes when other household members stop streaming or uploading. If the router offers a channel-selection feature, use its recommended settings or consult its documentation rather than repeatedly changing channels at random.

Update router firmware and device network drivers from the manufacturer's official source. Restart equipment after recording the fault and checking for service notices. If only one room has trouble, consider access-point placement or a properly connected additional access point rather than immediately buying a faster broadband plan.

How to reduce packet loss on a wired connection

Reseat Ethernet connectors and inspect the cable for sharp bends, crushed sections, damaged clips or loose wall sockets. Test with a short, known-good cable and another router port. Check the negotiated link speed in the device's network settings. A link that repeatedly disconnects or negotiates at an unexpected rate deserves attention.

For fibre broadband, do not open optical connectors or look into fibre ports. Check only the visible status indicators and cables that the provider's instructions identify as user-serviceable. If the optical network terminal reports an alarm, or the connection repeatedly drops, contact the provider. Fibre and coaxial faults may require specialised tools and a technician.

On a computer, check for adapter driver updates and obvious device resource problems. Security tools and VPN software can alter traffic handling, but do not disable protections permanently. If a temporary controlled test is needed, follow the software vendor's safe diagnostic instructions and restore the normal protection settings afterwards.

When the ISP may need to investigate

Contact your internet provider when loss appears on multiple devices, occurs over both Wi-Fi and Ethernet, affects more than one external destination, or coincides with repeated disconnections. Provide the time window, local time zone, connection type, router or ONT status, test destinations, packet counts and results. Include whether the problem affects upload, download or both if you have evidence.

Ask the provider to check line or optical levels, access-link errors, modem or ONT event logs, congestion and upstream routing where relevant. A provider may have visibility into equipment and network segments that a home user cannot inspect. Keep the support reference number and repeat tests after any repair or configuration change.

A single ping result is not enough to prove that an ISP is at fault. A well-documented pattern across wired devices and several destinations is more useful. If the provider asks for traceroute or MTR output, provide the full results and exact timestamps, and avoid interpreting every non-responsive intermediate hop as packet loss.

Packet loss in gaming, calls and streaming: what to prioritise

For competitive gaming and interactive calls, stability and low delay variation often matter more than peak download speed. Prefer Ethernet for stationary devices where practical. Reduce competing uploads, such as large cloud backups, while testing. If the router supports sensible traffic prioritisation, use it carefully and remember that QoS cannot create extra capacity on an overloaded ISP link.

For video meetings, check the application's own call-health statistics. Microsoft Teams, for example, distinguishes packet loss from jitter and round-trip time when reviewing call quality. The application may show whether the problem affected incoming or outgoing media and can help separate a device issue from a network-wide problem.

For streaming, check whether the issue repeats across different services and devices. If one service alone buffers while other applications remain stable, the cause may be service-specific, content-delivery-related or route-specific rather than general packet loss on the home connection.

Is zero packet loss always necessary?

Not every diagnostic probe must receive a reply for a network to be usable. Some devices limit responses to ping, some monitoring tools sample traffic, and certain congestion-control behaviours use loss as feedback. A small isolated result in an ICMP test may not represent loss affecting your applications.

At the same time, persistent or bursty loss that coincides with broken calls, gaming interruptions or failed transfers should not be dismissed as harmless. The right question is whether the measured loss is repeatable, end-to-end and relevant to the application. Compare tests, endpoints and actual user experience before deciding that a percentage is acceptable.

A practical troubleshooting checklist

  • Record the time, application, device and symptoms.
  • Compare at least two devices on the same connection.
  • Test near the router and compare Wi-Fi with Ethernet if possible.
  • Ping the local gateway and more than one external destination.
  • Repeat tests long enough to capture intermittent behaviour.
  • Use traceroute or MTR as supporting evidence, not a stand-alone verdict.
  • Check cables, router status, firmware and device drivers.
  • Contact the ISP with full results if wired devices also show repeatable external loss.

Change one thing at a time and repeat the same test after each change. If you move the router, replace a cable and alter several settings simultaneously, it becomes difficult to know which change mattered. A simple before-and-after record makes troubleshooting faster and helps a support technician follow your findings.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good packet loss percentage?

For a stable interactive connection, users generally want packet loss to be as close to zero as practical. There is no single threshold that guarantees good quality for every application. Even a small burst can disrupt real-time audio or gaming, while a transfer may recover through retransmission. Consider the application, duration, direction and whether the loss is repeatable.

Can I have packet loss with fast internet?

Yes. Download speed measures how quickly data can be transferred under test conditions; packet loss measures packets that fail to arrive. A connection can deliver high throughput during a test and still drop packets intermittently, on a particular route or during a real-time session.

Does packet loss mean my router is faulty?

No. Loss can originate in a device, Wi-Fi link, cable, router, ISP network, upstream route or remote destination. Compare multiple devices, test the local gateway, and use Ethernet if available to isolate the likely segment before replacing hardware.

Can Wi-Fi cause packet loss?

Yes. Interference, weak signal, congestion, distance, obstacles and overloaded access points can lead to wireless retries and dropped traffic. Compare a wired test with Wi-Fi and repeat the test close to the router to see whether the local wireless link is involved.

Does a ping test prove packet loss?

A ping test reports missing replies to its probes, which is useful evidence but not a complete measure of all application traffic. Firewalls and routers may filter or rate-limit ICMP. Use multiple destinations and compare the results with application behaviour and, when possible, other diagnostic tools.

Why does packet loss happen only at night?

Evening-only symptoms can be consistent with congestion, but timing alone does not prove it. Household uploads, Wi-Fi contention, provider access-network load, remote service demand or scheduled activity may contribute. Compare wired and wireless tests at busy and quiet times and share timestamps with your provider.

Can a VPN fix packet loss?

A VPN changes the route and encapsulates traffic, so it may avoid a problematic route in some cases. It can also add overhead, delay or another point of failure. A better result through a VPN is a clue about routing, not proof of the exact cause. Do not use it as a substitute for documenting the underlying issue.

Should I upgrade my broadband plan to fix packet loss?

Not automatically. More bandwidth may help if congestion inside your home connection is the cause, but it will not repair a damaged cable, unstable Wi-Fi, failing router, faulty access link or remote routing issue. Diagnose the affected segment before paying for a faster plan.

What to remember

Packet loss is a delivery problem, not simply a speed problem. It can affect calls, games, streaming and ordinary browsing in different ways, and the cause may be local or far beyond the home. Start with repeatable tests, compare Wi-Fi and Ethernet, check the local gateway and more than one destination, and record the times when symptoms occur. Use traceroute and MTR carefully, because intermediate devices may not answer diagnostic probes even while forwarding normal traffic. When evidence points beyond your equipment, share the full results with your ISP instead of guessing or replacing hardware at random.
