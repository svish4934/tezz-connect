How GPS Works: GNSS, A-GPS, NavIC and Phone Location Explained

Your phone can show a blue dot on a map, guide you through unfamiliar streets and record a running route. But how does it know where it is? The answer begins with extremely precise clocks aboard navigation satellites. By measuring how long radio signals take to reach a receiver, a phone can calculate its position. Modern devices combine several satellite constellations with network assistance and sensors, which is why the familiar word “GPS” is only part of the story.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

GPS is one system; GNSS is the bigger picture

GPS stands for Global Positioning System. It is the satellite navigation system owned and operated by the United States. The system broadcasts signals that receivers can use to calculate position, navigation and timing. It is widely used in phones, cars, aircraft, ships, surveying equipment, asset trackers and infrastructure that needs accurate time.

GNSS means Global Navigation Satellite Systems. It is the umbrella term for satellite navigation constellations operated by different countries and organisations. These include the United States' GPS, Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo and China's BeiDou. Regional systems include India's NavIC and Japan's QZSS. A modern phone may receive signals from more than one constellation at the same time.

This distinction matters in India. A phone app may label its location indicator as “GPS”, but the underlying position can be calculated using several GNSS constellations and other inputs. ISRO explicitly explains that GPS is not a generic name for every satellite navigation system; GNSS is the broader term. The most accurate everyday description of the result is often simply “device location”.

Navigation satellites do not normally receive your phone's location request and send a personalised answer back. They broadcast signals in a way that allows compatible receivers to listen. The receiver performs the calculations locally. This one-way broadcast design means that a constellation can serve a very large number of users without needing a separate two-way satellite connection for every phone.

The basic idea: turn radio travel time into distance

Every navigation satellite continuously broadcasts a radio signal containing information about the time the signal was transmitted and the satellite's position. The signal travels at approximately the speed of light. A receiver records when it arrives and compares that arrival time with the time information encoded in the message. The difference gives an estimate of how long the signal travelled.

Distance is calculated using a simple relationship:

Distance = signal speed × travel time

Because the signal moves so quickly, even a tiny timing error can create a large distance error. A timing error of one microsecond corresponds to roughly 300 metres of light travel. Navigation systems therefore rely on highly stable atomic clocks in satellites, carefully maintained time references and receiver algorithms that account for clock differences and signal delays.

The receiver also needs to know where each satellite was when it transmitted the signal. Satellite orbit and clock information is carried in navigation messages. Combining the estimated distances with the satellites' known positions allows the receiver to calculate where it must be on Earth.

The measured distances are often called pseudoranges rather than perfect geometric distances. The receiver's clock is not as accurate as the atomic clocks aboard satellites, and signal propagation introduces delays. The receiver solves for its clock bias along with its position, which is why more than three satellites are normally required for a full three-dimensional fix.

Why four satellites are normally needed

Imagine that you know your distance from one satellite. You could be anywhere on an enormous sphere around that satellite. A second distance narrows the possible positions to an intersection of spheres. A third measurement can narrow the possibilities further. But a real receiver also has an unknown clock offset, so it needs another independent measurement to solve the complete problem.

In a basic three-dimensional navigation solution, the receiver estimates four unknowns: latitude-related position, longitude-related position, altitude-related position and receiver clock bias. Signals from at least four satellites provide the minimum set of independent measurements under normal assumptions. Additional satellites improve the solution by adding redundancy and, when well distributed in the sky, improving geometry.

This process is called trilateration, not triangulation. Triangulation uses angles. Satellite navigation primarily uses measured distances derived from signal travel times. The distinction is more than vocabulary: a receiver is not simply looking at the angle between satellites. It estimates ranges and solves a geometric problem using the known satellite coordinates.

More satellites do not automatically guarantee perfect accuracy. If all visible satellites are clustered in one part of the sky, the geometry is weak. A receiver with fewer but well-spread satellites can sometimes have a better geometric arrangement than one tracking many satellites in a poor distribution. Navigation engineers describe this effect using dilution of precision (DOP).

What happens inside a phone's GNSS receiver?

A smartphone contains a GNSS radio receiver and antenna system. The antenna collects very weak satellite signals. The receiver searches for known signal patterns, identifies satellites, tracks their timing and frequency, and extracts navigation data. It then estimates pseudoranges and combines them with satellite orbit and clock information to calculate a position and time.

The receiver may track multiple frequencies and constellations. This gives it more measurements and can help mitigate some errors. Dual-frequency receivers can estimate ionospheric delay by comparing how signals at different frequencies are affected. Support depends on the phone's chipset and antenna design; not every phone can use every GNSS signal or frequency.

The operating system exposes location services to apps. On Android, GNSS satellite positioning is one provider, while a fused location provider may combine several location sources to provide an appropriate fix. These can include GNSS, Wi-Fi, cellular networks and device sensors, depending on the device and software. An app that requests location does not necessarily receive a raw satellite-only solution.

Maps then display the calculated coordinates on a digital map. The blue dot, road snapping, compass direction and route line are application features built on top of location data. A map may snap a slightly inaccurate point to a nearby road, infer a likely route or smooth movement between updates. The visual result can look more precise than the underlying measurement actually is.

GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou and NavIC

Each GNSS constellation has its own satellites, signal structure, frequencies, control network and service characteristics. A compatible receiver can use multiple systems together. This multi-constellation approach can increase the number of satellites visible to the receiver and improve availability in difficult environments, although actual performance depends on which signals the device supports and can track.

Galileo, for example, is designed as a civilian-controlled European GNSS. Its open signals are available to compatible receivers, while additional services provide capabilities for specialised users. BeiDou and GLONASS also provide independent satellite navigation services. The receiver does not need to choose one system exclusively if its hardware and software support several.

India's NavIC, formerly called the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System (IRNSS), is a regional system developed by ISRO. It is designed to provide positioning, navigation and timing over India and a region extending beyond the Indian mainland. Its civilian Standard Positioning Service is distinct from its restricted service for authorised users. Receiver compatibility is essential: a phone cannot use a signal its GNSS chipset does not support.

ISRO's published information describes NavIC's space, ground and user segments, its L5 and S-band services, and the introduction of a civilian L1 signal in newer-generation satellites. NavIC signals are designed to be interoperable with other GNSS signals. For a consumer buying a phone, the useful question is not whether the marketing material says “GPS” but which GNSS constellations and frequency bands the exact model supports.

What is A-GPS and why does it make location faster?

Assisted GPS, usually abbreviated A-GPS, uses information delivered over a network to help a receiver acquire satellite signals or calculate a position more quickly. A phone may download satellite orbit and clock assistance data, approximate time, a rough location or other information that helps it search for the correct signals. This can reduce the delay before the first usable fix, especially when the receiver has not been used recently.

A-GPS does not mean that mobile towers replace satellites in every GPS calculation. The satellite signals still provide the ranging measurements in a satellite-based fix. Cellular or internet connectivity can supply assistance data and can also provide an independent network-based location estimate. These are different mechanisms that may be combined by the phone's location service.

When a phone has recently been operating, it may already know approximate satellite positions and timing data. This can help it reacquire satellites quickly. If it has been powered off for a long time, moved a large distance or lost current assistance data, it may take longer to obtain a fresh fix. This is one reason a phone can appear to find location almost instantly in one situation and take noticeably longer in another.

Satellite positioning itself does not require mobile data. A receiver can calculate a position using broadcast satellite signals and valid navigation data without opening a web connection. However, online maps, live traffic, cloud search, location sharing and network-delivered assistance may need mobile data or Wi-Fi. Downloading an offline map can make navigation more useful when internet access is unavailable, but it does not create satellite reception indoors.

How phone location combines satellites, Wi-Fi, towers and sensors

Modern phone location is often a blended estimate. GNSS provides an absolute outdoor position when satellite signals are usable. Nearby Wi-Fi access points can help estimate location using known network observations. Cellular networks can provide a coarser position based on serving cells and other measurements. Bluetooth beacons may help in carefully instrumented buildings. Accelerometers, gyroscopes, magnetometers and barometers can help estimate movement, orientation or altitude changes.

These sources have different strengths. GNSS can provide global outdoor coordinates without needing a local Wi-Fi database. Wi-Fi may work indoors where satellite signals are weak, but accuracy depends on access-point density, database quality and environmental change. Cellular positioning is broadly available but can be less precise, especially where cells cover large areas. Sensors can track short-term movement but accumulate drift if not corrected by an external reference.

A fused location provider can select or combine sources based on the app's requested accuracy, power budget, device capability and current conditions. A weather app may not need a high-precision continuous fix. A turn-by-turn navigation app may request frequent updates. A fitness app may use GNSS to record an outdoor route but reduce sampling when the phone is stationary to save battery.

Because of this blending, the location displayed by an app may remain available even when a satellite fix is temporarily lost. The phone might estimate movement from sensors or use Wi-Fi and cellular signals. That can be useful, but the displayed dot may be less reliable than a fresh GNSS solution. Apps should communicate uncertainty rather than imply that every point is equally accurate.

Why GPS can be inaccurate in cities and indoors

GNSS signals arrive at Earth extremely weak after travelling thousands of kilometres. A clear view of the sky helps the receiver collect direct signals. Tall buildings, roofs, tunnels, dense tree cover and steep terrain can block signals or reduce the number of satellites available. Indoors, the building structure can attenuate the signal so strongly that the receiver cannot maintain a reliable satellite fix.

Urban canyons create a special problem. Signals may reflect off glass, concrete and metal before reaching the phone. This is called multipath. The reflected signal travels farther than the direct path, which can distort the measured arrival time and create a position error. A phone standing beside a tall building may therefore show a blue dot on the wrong side of a street or jump between nearby roads.

Atmospheric delays also affect the signal. The ionosphere contains charged particles that alter radio propagation, and the troposphere adds further delay. Receiver models and corrections reduce these effects, but they are not eliminated in every condition. Satellite clock and orbit errors, receiver noise, antenna design and poor satellite geometry contribute as well.

Inside a building, a phone may show a location based on Wi-Fi, cellular data or other sensors rather than a strong satellite fix. Large shopping centres, airports and campuses sometimes deploy indoor positioning infrastructure. Without that infrastructure, an app may know the general building or street but not the exact floor, shop or corridor.

Accuracy: what does a few metres really mean?

There is no single accuracy number that applies to every phone, location and moment. GPS.gov explains that performance depends on the receiver, satellite geometry, atmosphere and surrounding environment. NIST's consumer explanation notes that standard GPS service can provide metre-scale positioning in suitable conditions, while advanced receivers and additional signals or corrections can improve precision further.

Accuracy is often expressed as a statistical measure, not a guarantee that every reading will fall inside a circle of a fixed radius. A device may report an estimated horizontal accuracy value, but that estimate is not the same as a survey certification. A location suitable for finding a restaurant is not necessarily suitable for marking a legal property boundary.

Professional surveying can use carrier-phase measurements, reference stations and correction services to achieve centimetre-level results under suitable conditions. Real-time kinematic (RTK) and precise point positioning (PPP) are examples of techniques that improve positioning beyond a standalone phone fix. They require specialised receivers, correction data and more demanding processing.

For ordinary navigation, the most important factors are a clear sky view, good satellite geometry, supported signals and a stable receiver. If the phone's estimated accuracy is poor, wait for additional satellites or move away from obstructions. Avoid treating the first location after leaving a tunnel or building as a final measurement.

India's NavIC and GAGAN: two different roles

NavIC is India's independent regional satellite navigation system. It is intended to provide positioning, navigation and timing services over India and a surrounding region. It has a space segment of navigation satellites, a ground segment that monitors and controls the system, and a user segment made up of compatible receivers. Its Standard Positioning Service is intended for civilian users; a restricted service is reserved for authorised users.

NavIC is not the same as GAGAN. GAGAN, or GPS Aided Geo Augmented Navigation, is a satellite-based augmentation system developed by ISRO and the Airports Authority of India for civil aviation in the Indian region. It broadcasts correction and integrity information that can improve the use of GPS signals for aviation applications. NavIC is an independent regional navigation constellation; GAGAN augments GPS. They solve related but different problems.

For everyday phone users, NavIC support depends on the device's chipset, antenna and software. A phone may support GPS, Galileo and BeiDou but not every NavIC signal. Even when a phone supports NavIC, the app may not show a separate “NavIC” indicator because the operating system can present a combined location result.

ISRO's public documentation is the best place to check current system architecture, signal bands and service details. NavIC capabilities and satellite availability can evolve as new spacecraft are launched and commissioned. Avoid relying on old phone marketing lists or outdated constellation counts when assessing current compatibility.

What can disrupt GNSS? Jamming, spoofing and interference

GNSS receivers depend on weak signals, so local radio interference can affect reception. Jamming involves transmitting energy that overwhelms or disrupts the receiver's ability to detect authentic navigation signals. It can cause loss of position, reduced satellite tracking or unreliable timing. Jamming may be intentional or caused by faulty equipment and other radio emissions.

Spoofing is different. A spoofing source attempts to transmit counterfeit signals that make a receiver calculate an incorrect position or time. A receiver may continue to display a plausible location even though the solution is false. More advanced systems monitor signal consistency, compare multiple constellations and frequencies, and use external sensors or trusted references to detect anomalies.

Interference and spoofing are not only aviation or military concerns. GNSS timing supports telecommunications, power systems, financial infrastructure and transport. Critical operators may use monitoring, backup timing sources, holdover clocks, multi-frequency receivers, antenna protection and independent positioning methods to improve resilience.

Ordinary users should not try to defeat or investigate suspected interference themselves. If a navigation app behaves strangely in a particular area, compare with another trusted device or service, move to a safe open location and report the issue through appropriate channels where relevant. Never rely on a consumer map app as the sole source for safety-critical navigation.

How to improve location accuracy on your phone

Move into an open area. If it is safe, step away from tall buildings, dense tree cover, roofs and metal structures. A clearer view of the sky usually helps the receiver track more satellites and improves signal geometry.

Allow the correct location permission. Check whether the app has permission to use precise location, and whether the operating system's location service is enabled. Permission names and settings differ between Android and iOS versions.

Keep software current. Operating-system updates can include GNSS driver, location-provider and sensor improvements. Update navigation apps and device firmware through official channels.

Give the receiver time. After a long period without use, a phone may need time to acquire satellites and download current navigation data. Wait for the accuracy estimate to stabilise before relying on a route or recording a track.

Check device capability. Different models support different constellations and frequencies. A device with multi-frequency GNSS may perform better in some difficult environments, but antenna placement and software remain important.

Do not confuse internet speed with satellite reception. A fast mobile data connection does not make a blocked GNSS signal stronger. Network assistance can help acquisition, but it cannot turn a phone with no usable satellite view into a reliable outdoor GNSS receiver.

Location privacy: what happens when an app asks for GPS?

Location is sensitive because it can reveal where someone lives, works, travels, worships, shops or receives medical care. An app should request location only when it needs it and explain why. Users should review whether an app needs precise location, approximate location, foreground access or background access.

Location permissions are not all-or-nothing. Modern operating systems offer controls that limit access to approximate location or only while an app is in use. A weather app may work with an approximate area. A turn-by-turn navigation app may need precise location while the route is active. A social app that requests continuous background location should have a clear and necessary purpose.

Turning off an app's location permission does not necessarily disable every form of network-based inference. Websites and services may still infer a broad region from an IP address, and apps can collect other signals subject to platform rules and applicable law. Users should review app permissions, account settings and privacy policies rather than assume one switch controls all data collection.

For organisations, location data should be protected with access controls, retention limits, encryption and a documented purpose. Employee tracking, fleet monitoring and customer analytics require transparency and appropriate legal review. A precise location database can create significant risk if exposed or misused.

Frequently asked questions

1. What does GPS stand for?

GPS stands for Global Positioning System. It is the satellite navigation system operated by the United States. GNSS is the broader term for satellite navigation systems around the world.

2. How does a phone calculate its GPS location?

A phone receives timed radio signals and orbit information from navigation satellites. It estimates signal travel distances and combines measurements from at least four satellites to solve for three-dimensional position and receiver clock offset.

3. Why are at least four satellites needed?

A receiver generally needs to solve for latitude, longitude, altitude and its own clock error. Four independent satellite measurements provide the minimum information for those four unknowns, although additional satellites can improve geometry and reliability.

4. What is the difference between GPS and GNSS?

GPS is one satellite navigation system. GNSS means Global Navigation Satellite Systems and includes GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou and regional systems such as India's NavIC and Japan's QZSS.

5. Does A-GPS work without internet?

A phone can calculate a satellite-based position without a live internet connection if it has usable satellite signals and navigation data. Assisted GPS uses network-delivered information to speed acquisition or improve availability, so some assistance features may be unavailable offline.

6. What is NavIC and does it work in India?

NavIC, developed by ISRO, is India's regional satellite navigation system. Its service area covers India and a region beyond the Indian mainland. A phone or receiver must support the relevant NavIC signals to use them.

7. Why is GPS inaccurate inside buildings?

Satellite signals are weak by the time they reach Earth and can be blocked or reflected by roofs, walls and nearby structures. Indoor devices may rely more on Wi-Fi, cellular networks, Bluetooth or other sensors, which provide a different kind of location estimate.

8. Does GPS need mobile data?

Satellite positioning itself does not require mobile data. Maps, live traffic, online search, cloud location sharing and assisted data downloads may need an internet connection.

9. Can GPS be jammed or spoofed?

Yes. Jamming can overwhelm or disrupt satellite signals, while spoofing can transmit misleading signals that cause a receiver to calculate a false position or time. Critical systems use monitoring, multiple signals and other resilience measures.

10. How can I improve location accuracy on my phone?

Move to an open area with a clear view of the sky, enable the device's precise location setting, update system software and allow the app appropriate permissions. Avoid judging satellite accuracy from a single reading near tall buildings or indoors.
