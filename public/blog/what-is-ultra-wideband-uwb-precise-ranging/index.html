What Is UWB? Ultra-Wideband Positioning, Ranging and Digital Keys

A phone that points towards a lost tracker, a car that recognises where its digital key is standing, or a warehouse system that locates equipment indoors may use Ultra-Wideband. UWB is not simply another way to connect headphones or browse the internet. Its distinctive strength is measuring the time radio signals take to travel, helping compatible devices estimate distance and direction with fine precision.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

What is Ultra-Wideband?

Ultra-Wideband, usually written as UWB, is a radio technology that uses an unusually wide slice of radio spectrum to transmit carefully timed signals. In modern consumer and industrial systems, one of its most important jobs is precise ranging: estimating how far one compatible device is from another. Depending on the system, it can also help determine direction or calculate a device's position relative to several fixed reference points.

That makes UWB different from the wireless technologies people use most often. Wi-Fi is built primarily to move network data. Bluetooth supports discovery, accessories, audio and low-energy sensor communication. NFC is designed for deliberate, very close tap interactions. UWB can carry data, but current consumer interest centres on its ability to establish spatial context: not just that another device is nearby, but approximately how far away it is and, with suitable antenna arrangements, where it is.

UWB signals occupy bandwidths of at least 500 MHz under common regulatory and technical definitions, or a substantial fraction of the carrier frequency. Instead of relying only on received signal strength, many ranging systems use very short radio pulses and precise timestamps. The wide bandwidth helps the receiver distinguish signal timing and multipath components more effectively than narrowband systems in suitable conditions.

UWB is not a universal centimetre-accuracy guarantee. Results depend on the radio design, antennas, calibration, ranging protocol, device orientation, line of sight, reflections, interference, regional spectrum rules and software. A well-engineered installation can provide very fine measurements; a phone in a pocket surrounded by people and reflective surfaces may behave less ideally.

How UWB measures distance: time of flight

Radio waves travel at approximately the speed of light. If a device can accurately measure how long a signal takes to travel between two points, it can estimate the distance. This is called time of flight, often abbreviated ToF. The challenge is that radio signals cross a one-metre gap in only a few nanoseconds. Measuring that interval requires specialised radio hardware, timestamping and carefully designed exchanges.

A common approach is two-way ranging. An initiating device sends a poll or request. The responding device receives it and sends a response after a known or measured delay. The initiator records timestamps for the outgoing and returning messages and accounts for the responder's turnaround time. In a simplified exchange, the round-trip time minus the responder's reply delay is divided by two to estimate one-way propagation time. Multiplying that time by the speed of light produces an estimated distance.

Real protocols use more sophisticated timestamp exchanges and corrections to reduce errors caused by clock offsets, processing delays and imperfect hardware. Some methods use several messages and calculate a more robust result. The exact procedure depends on the standard, device roles and session configuration. The important principle is that UWB measures timing rather than treating signal strength as a direct ruler.

Signal-strength methods can be misleading. A strong signal might come from a nearby device, but walls, antenna orientation, transmit power and reflections can change received strength. A device farther away with a clear path can sometimes appear stronger than a closer device behind an obstruction. Time-based ranging is less dependent on those assumptions, although it is not immune to radio propagation problems.

A simple example

Imagine a phone and a compatible tracker in the same room. The phone initiates a secure ranging session and exchanges timed packets with the tracker. The radio estimates the distance; the phone's application can combine that measurement with antenna information and user-interface guidance. The screen may show that the tracker is roughly two metres away and indicate a direction. The displayed arrow is a product feature built from ranging, antenna geometry, device sensors and software—not a raw UWB signal that independently knows the room layout.

Why wide bandwidth matters

UWB's broad spectrum is a defining technical characteristic. A wide signal can provide fine time resolution because its pulses and channel response contain timing detail across a broad range of frequencies. In practical terms, this can help a receiver separate the earliest arriving signal from later echoes, which is important indoors where radio energy reflects from floors, ceilings, walls, furniture and metal objects.

UWB devices generally transmit at low power spectral density so that they can share spectrum with other services under applicable regulations. This is one reason UWB is not intended to replace Wi-Fi as a high-throughput home networking system. Its value lies in reliable ranging and short-range spatial applications, not simply in sending the largest possible files.

Different countries and regions regulate UWB frequency bands, transmit power and permitted use. Device manufacturers must design radios and firmware to comply with local requirements. A product's supported channels or ranging performance may therefore vary by market. A global product should not assume that one radio configuration is legal or optimal everywhere.

The wideband waveform can improve the ability to resolve paths in a multipath environment, but it does not make walls transparent or eliminate reflections. If the direct path is blocked, the receiver may detect a reflected path that travelled farther. That can create a distance estimate that is longer than the true straight-line separation. Good systems detect poor measurements, apply quality checks and avoid presenting false precision.

UWB versus Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, NFC and GPS

These technologies solve different problems and frequently work together. UWB is not a replacement for every radio in a phone. A complete product may use Bluetooth Low Energy to discover another device, exchange setup information and wake the UWB radio. UWB then performs the precise ranging. Wi-Fi or mobile data may connect the product to cloud services, while GPS may provide outdoor geographic context.

Bluetooth RSSI can provide a rough proximity estimate by observing received signal strength. It is useful for discovery and broad proximity but is affected by transmit power, antenna orientation, obstacles and reflections. Bluetooth Channel Sounding is a newer capability that uses radio measurements to improve distance estimation; it is a separate technique from UWB and device support varies.

Wi-Fi positioning can use access-point information, round-trip timing or other measurements, depending on the system. It can be useful in buildings where Wi-Fi infrastructure already exists, but accuracy and availability vary. GPS/GNSS is excellent outdoors with suitable sky visibility but can be unreliable inside buildings. UWB can fill a different role at room or object scale when both ends have compatible hardware.

NFC is especially close-range. A tap may be used to begin a setup flow or provide an access fallback, while UWB verifies distance in a hands-free interaction. For more on the other short-range technologies, read our guides to Bluetooth Low Energy and GATT and NFC contactless communication.

How UWB works with Bluetooth and other radios

UWB ranging sessions need both devices to know how to communicate. Before precise ranging begins, they must discover one another and exchange session parameters such as device addresses, channel information, roles and cryptographic material. That exchange is often performed over an out-of-band connection, such as Bluetooth Low Energy, or through another secure mechanism defined by the product.

A common user experience therefore involves more than one radio. Bluetooth can advertise that a device is available, allow a phone to discover it and carry the information needed to start a UWB session. UWB then measures distance. The app can end the session when the user walks away or when the task is complete. The radio hand-off is usually invisible to the user.

Discovery and ranging should not be confused. A Bluetooth advertisement may indicate that a tracker exists nearby, but it does not prove the tracker's exact distance. A UWB ranging session can refine the spatial estimate after the devices have securely established the session. Similarly, seeing a device in an app does not guarantee that the phone has the UWB hardware or permissions needed for precise direction finding.

Android's developer documentation describes UWB as a ranging technology and explains the need for an out-of-band mechanism to discover peers and exchange parameters. Developers must check device capability, permissions and supported session configurations rather than assume every Android phone supports UWB. The API and background behaviour can change across Android releases, so apps need capability detection and graceful fallback.

Secure ranging and the Scrambled Timestamp Sequence

Distance measurement can be security-sensitive. Consider a car that unlocks when its digital key is close to the driver's door. If the system only detects a Bluetooth signal, an attacker may attempt to relay that signal between the key and vehicle, making them appear closer than they really are. A secure ranging system aims to make such distance manipulation more difficult.

UWB systems can use a Scrambled Timestamp Sequence (STS), a cryptographically generated sequence integrated into the ranging exchange. With appropriate key provisioning and protocol design, the receiver can verify the expected sequence and make it harder for an outsider to predict, replay or manipulate the timing measurements. Android's UWB documentation distinguishes static and provisioned STS configurations and notes that provisioned STS mitigates relay and replay threats that static configurations may remain susceptible to.

That does not mean every UWB product is automatically secure. Security depends on how keys are created, exchanged, stored, rotated and revoked; whether the session is authenticated; how the device handles failures; and whether the application accepts stale or unauthenticated measurements. A poorly designed system can undermine a strong radio feature.

Relay resistance is also not a synonym for complete protection against every attack. Attackers may target account recovery, phone malware, vehicle software, lost credentials, weak provisioning or the physical access system. UWB is one security component in a larger design. For high-value access, use the complete certified ecosystem and follow manufacturer security updates.

Digital car keys and hands-free access

Digital car keys are one of the most visible UWB applications. A compatible phone or wearable stores a credential that a vehicle can recognise. Bluetooth may help the vehicle discover the device and begin the process. UWB can then measure the distance and, in some systems, direction of the key relative to the car. The vehicle can use that information to decide whether to unlock a door, enable passive entry or support another action.

Location context can improve convenience and security. A car may distinguish a key approaching the driver's door from one left inside the cabin or standing on the other side of the vehicle. The precise behaviour depends on the vehicle, phone, key specification and antenna layout. A car's user manual remains the authority for supported doors, trunk behaviour, battery fallback and emergency access.

Digital key systems are built around standards and industry collaboration. The Car Connectivity Consortium develops digital key specifications for interoperability across vehicles and devices. UWB may be combined with NFC and Bluetooth: NFC can provide a close-tap fallback in some products, Bluetooth supports discovery and communication, and UWB contributes secure distance awareness.

Before relying on a phone as the only car key, check whether the vehicle supports the phone model and operating-system version, whether the credential can be shared with family members, how lost devices are revoked and what happens when the phone battery is depleted. Keep a physical or manufacturer-approved backup method available when recommended.

Finding lost devices and directional tracking

UWB can make compatible item-finding experiences more precise than a simple “nearby” indicator. A phone may display both distance and a directional cue to a tracker or another device. The user can move around a room and watch the distance estimate change, helping narrow down the location of a lost item such as keys, a bag or a remote control.

The direction estimate may use multiple antennas, phase or timing information, device orientation sensors and software filtering. It is therefore a system-level feature. If a phone is held at an unusual angle, if the tracker is obstructed or if the room contains strong reflections, the arrow may fluctuate. The interface should be treated as guidance rather than a survey-grade measurement.

Not every Bluetooth tracker has UWB. Some tags broadcast Bluetooth identifiers and rely on nearby phones in a crowdsourced finding network. Others include UWB for close-range precision finding. A product can support a large finding network without supporting UWB, and a UWB tracker may still rely on Bluetooth or a cloud network for discovery when it is far away.

Privacy is important in tracking products. Responsible systems include unwanted-tracking alerts and mechanisms to identify unknown trackers travelling with a person. Keep the operating system and tracking app updated, understand how shared items are managed and follow local guidance if an unknown tracker appears to be following you. A tracker is not a substitute for consent when monitoring people.

Indoor positioning, warehouses and real-time location systems

UWB can also be used in commercial buildings, hospitals, factories, warehouses and logistics centres. A real-time location system (RTLS) may use fixed anchors installed around an area and mobile tags attached to equipment, tools, vehicles or staff badges. The system estimates the tag's position relative to the anchors and displays it on a site map.

One approach uses two-way ranging between a mobile tag and one or more anchors. Another uses time-difference-of-arrival methods, where multiple synchronised anchors observe a signal and the system estimates the source position from differences in arrival time. Angle-of-arrival measurements can add directional information when the antenna system supports them. The choice depends on scale, update rate, infrastructure cost, accuracy target and battery requirements.

Unlike satellite navigation, indoor positioning depends on the geometry and calibration of the installed infrastructure. Anchor locations must be measured and recorded accurately. Poor anchor placement, unsynchronised equipment, metal racks, moving machinery and dense human activity can affect performance. A successful pilot should test the actual building, not just an empty demonstration room.

Organisations should define the business problem before installing tags. Tracking high-value mobile equipment may justify a different update rate and accuracy from monitoring pallets moving through a loading area. Staff location systems raise privacy and labour concerns and should have clear policies, access controls, retention limits and consultation with affected workers.

UWB can reduce time spent searching for equipment and support workflow automation, but it does not automatically make a warehouse more efficient. The system needs reliable tag batteries, anchor maintenance, map updates, network integration and operational ownership. Measure the result against a baseline such as search time, lost asset incidents or process delays.

UWB for smart homes, access control and healthcare

In a smart home, UWB can help a system understand where a person or device is relative to a room or appliance. A phone might point toward a speaker to select it, or a compatible lock might use secure ranging to decide whether an authorised device is at the entrance. These interactions can feel more natural than choosing a device from a long list.

For residential and commercial access control, UWB may be combined with Bluetooth Low Energy and NFC. Bluetooth can wake the reader and exchange setup information; UWB can provide secure ranging; NFC may offer a close-contact fallback. The system still needs identity management, revocation, door-controller security, audit logging and a safe response when communications fail.

Healthcare and assisted-living settings may use UWB tags to locate equipment or support workflow awareness. Any system involving patients or staff requires careful privacy design, risk assessment and compliance with applicable health and data-protection rules. Location data can be sensitive even when the radio itself does not carry a person's name.

Industrial use cases include tool tracking, vehicle positioning, worker safety zones and automation. UWB's timing-based ranging can provide useful spatial information in areas where GPS is unavailable. However, safety-critical systems need validated performance, fail-safe behaviour and independent safety controls. A consumer tracker should never be repurposed as a certified worker-protection system.

What affects UWB accuracy?

Line of sight: The most direct radio path usually provides the best ranging conditions. If a person, wall or metal object blocks that path, the receiver may detect a reflected path instead. Non-line-of-sight errors can make the measured distance appear longer or become unstable.

Multipath reflections: Indoor signals bounce off surfaces. UWB's wide bandwidth can help distinguish paths, but reflections still complicate measurement. A room with metal shelves or machinery may behave differently from a small furnished office.

Antenna design and orientation: Antenna placement, polarisation, enclosure materials and how a phone is held influence the radio link. Manufacturers calibrate their devices, but a case or accessory can alter the real-world environment.

Anchor geometry: A multi-anchor location system needs anchors positioned so that the tag can be measured from useful directions. Anchors clustered in one corner may produce poor geometry across a large space. Surveying and calibration are part of the installation, not optional finishing tasks.

Clock and timestamp quality: Time-of-flight measurement depends on accurate timing. Hardware clocks, timestamp resolution, synchronisation, antenna delays and protocol implementation all affect the final estimate.

Movement and update rate: A fast-moving tag can change position between measurements. Systems must balance update frequency, battery use, radio airtime and smoothness. A location display may intentionally filter measurements, which can add a little delay while reducing visual jitter.

Battery use, range and practical limits

UWB is designed to operate with low transmit power, but the battery impact depends on how often a device ranges, how long sessions remain active and how the manufacturer manages the radio. A tracker that wakes briefly when requested has a different energy profile from a tag that reports location many times each second. Bluetooth discovery and application processing also contribute to overall power use.

There is no single universal UWB range. Consumer products may be designed for room-scale or nearby-object interactions, while specialised systems and newer standards can target different environments and distances. Regulatory limits, antennas, transmit power, receiver sensitivity, channel selection and obstructions all matter. A vendor's maximum range figure should not be treated as guaranteed indoor coverage.

UWB does not provide internet access by itself. It can operate locally between compatible devices without GPS or a cloud connection. A product may nevertheless require internet for account login, maps, firmware updates, remote sharing, device registration or finding-network services. Separate the local radio capability from the online features in a product specification.

Compatibility is another practical limit. A phone needs a UWB-capable chipset and antenna system, and the operating system must expose the required APIs. A tracker or lock also needs compatible hardware and a supported protocol. Two devices can both advertise UWB support yet fail to interoperate if they use incompatible profiles or application ecosystems.

How to check whether your phone supports UWB

Do not assume that a premium price or a recent release means a phone includes UWB. Check the exact model number and regional variant on the manufacturer's specification page. Look for an explicit Ultra-Wideband or UWB entry; Bluetooth, NFC, Wi-Fi 6E or 5G support does not prove UWB capability.

On Android, developers can query whether the device advertises the UWB system feature and check the supported ranging capabilities. The Android developer documentation describes the UWB Jetpack library and the steps needed to discover a peer, exchange session parameters and begin ranging. Supported models vary, and background ranging restrictions may differ between devices and Android releases.

On Apple devices, check Apple's current technical specifications and support documentation for the exact iPhone, Apple Watch or accessory model. UWB availability and the features exposed by Apple's ecosystem vary by generation and product. A device can contain a UWB chip but not support every third-party app or interoperability profile.

Before buying a tracker, digital key accessory or smart lock, verify compatibility at both ends. Check the phone model, OS version, region, app, accessory generation and required account. Look for explicit support for precision finding or secure ranging rather than relying on a generic “Bluetooth compatible” label.

Choosing an UWB system: a practical checklist

Start by identifying the problem. Is the goal to find a personal item, unlock a vehicle, locate industrial assets, guide a person indoors or control a smart-home device based on direction? Each use case has different requirements for range, accuracy, privacy, battery life, update rate and infrastructure.

For a consumer tracker, compare phone compatibility, supported finding networks, battery replacement, audible alerts, unwanted-tracking safeguards, water resistance and the availability of close-range UWB guidance. For digital keys, review the vehicle manufacturer's supported devices, sharing process, credential recovery and emergency entry procedure.

For an enterprise RTLS deployment, test anchor placement, tag battery life, update rate, map accuracy, network integration, API availability, security architecture and total lifecycle cost. Include installation, calibration, maintenance, software subscriptions if any, replacement tags and staff training in the business case. Do not compare products only by a laboratory accuracy number.

Ask suppliers how they handle non-line-of-sight conditions, interference, key management, firmware updates and loss of infrastructure. Request a pilot in the actual building or vehicle environment and define acceptance criteria before purchase. If the system is safety-critical, require independent validation and do not rely on a consumer-grade location estimate.

Privacy, consent and responsible location design

Precise location data can reveal routines, work patterns, visits and relationships. A UWB system should collect only the location information needed for its purpose, restrict who can see it and define how long records are retained. Users should understand when ranging is active and what information is shared with a cloud service.

Personal trackers should include protections against unwanted tracking and clear ways to identify or disable an unknown device. Employers should not quietly expand an equipment-tracking deployment into continuous worker surveillance. Smart locks should avoid exposing access credentials or detailed movement histories to unnecessary third parties.

Security design should cover the complete lifecycle: device identity, pairing, session key generation, secure ranging, firmware updates, lost-device response, access revocation and secure disposal. Radio precision does not replace privacy law, organisational governance or user consent.

Frequently asked questions

1. What does UWB stand for?

UWB stands for Ultra-Wideband. It is a radio technology that uses a very wide bandwidth and is widely used for precise distance and position measurement between compatible devices.

2. How accurate is UWB positioning?

Supported UWB systems can achieve accuracy on the order of centimetres in suitable conditions, but actual performance depends on hardware, antenna design, calibration, geometry, obstacles, multipath and the ranging protocol.

3. How does UWB measure distance?

UWB systems can measure radio signal time of flight. By timing a carefully structured exchange between devices and accounting for response delays, the system estimates the distance the signal travelled.

4. Is UWB the same as Bluetooth?

No. Bluetooth is commonly used for discovery, connectivity and data exchange. UWB is especially useful for precise ranging and spatial awareness. Devices can use Bluetooth to discover one another and exchange parameters before starting a UWB ranging session.

5. Does every smartphone support UWB?

No. UWB requires dedicated radio hardware, antennas, operating-system support and compatible applications. Availability varies by manufacturer, model, region and software version.

6. Can UWB find a phone or lost item indoors?

A compatible phone and tracker can use UWB to estimate direction and distance at close range. The experience depends on supported devices, the tracking network, app permissions and the environment; ordinary Bluetooth trackers work differently.

7. Is UWB secure enough for digital car keys?

UWB can support secure distance measurement and help resist certain relay attacks when implemented with authenticated protocols and cryptographic ranging. Overall security still depends on credential provisioning, device security, access policy and the vehicle system.

8. Does UWB need GPS or internet?

A local UWB ranging exchange does not inherently require GPS or an internet connection. A complete product may use internet services for setup, account management, maps, cloud tracking or credential provisioning.

9. Can UWB work through walls?

UWB radio can sometimes operate through common building materials, but walls, metal, people and other obstacles can weaken or distort signals and reduce accuracy. Reliable positioning requires system design and calibration for the environment.

10. What is the difference between UWB and NFC?

NFC is designed for very close tap-based communication, while UWB is used for precise ranging and spatial awareness over a greater practical distance. NFC can initiate or provide a fallback for some access workflows, while UWB can help verify where a digital key is located.
