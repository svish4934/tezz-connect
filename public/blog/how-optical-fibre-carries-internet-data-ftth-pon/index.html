How Optical Fibre Carries Internet Data: Light, FTTH and Fibre Broadband

Learn how optical fibre carries internet data using light, how FTTH and passive optical networks work, and what GPON, XGS-PON, ONTs, splitters and fibre faults mean.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Optical fibre carries internet data as patterns of light travelling through very thin strands of glass. Instead of sending electrical signals through copper, a fibre link converts digital information into optical signals, guides those signals through a glass core and converts them back into electrical data at the receiving equipment. This approach supports high capacity, long distances and resistance to electromagnetic interference.

When an internet provider advertises a fibre connection, the phrase usually means that optical fibre forms a substantial part of the access network. In a typical fibre-to-the-home (FTTH) installation, a fibre cable reaches the building or home and connects to an optical network terminal (ONT), sometimes called an optical network unit (ONU). The ONT provides an Ethernet connection to a router or directly to a compatible device. The details between the provider’s exchange and the home depend on the network architecture.

This guide follows the signal from the light source to the home, explains the physics without assuming an engineering background, compares common fibre access technologies and shows how technicians investigate faults. It also separates what fibre can deliver in theory from the internet speed a household actually experiences.

1. What is optical fibre?

An optical fibre is a thin, flexible strand made primarily from specially manufactured glass. Some applications use plastic optical fibre, but telecommunications networks commonly use glass because it can carry signals over much longer distances with low loss. The fibre itself is not the whole cable. A finished cable may contain one or many fibres, protective coatings, strength members, water-blocking materials and an outer jacket chosen for the environment in which it will be installed.

At the centre of a glass fibre is the core, the region through which most of the optical power travels. Around it is the cladding, glass with a slightly lower refractive index. A protective coating and buffer system help protect the delicate glass from moisture, abrasion and handling damage. The cable jacket and additional structural layers protect the fibre during installation and service.

The difference between the refractive indices of core and cladding is essential. It allows light launched into the fibre within an appropriate range of angles to remain guided along the core. The fibre is designed as an optical waveguide, not as a hollow tube with a mirror-like lining. The light travels through the glass itself.

2. How does light stay inside a fibre?

The basic principle is total internal reflection. When light travelling in a material with a higher refractive index reaches a boundary with a material of lower refractive index at a sufficiently shallow angle, it can be reflected back into the first material rather than passing through the boundary. In a conventional step-index fibre, the core has a higher refractive index than the surrounding cladding. This arrangement confines the guided light.

Imagine a beam entering a narrow glass path. If its launch conditions fall within the fibre’s acceptance range, the light remains guided as it travels along the cable, including around gentle curves. The light does not bounce perfectly without loss forever. Some optical power is absorbed or scattered, and bending or imperfect connections can add further loss. The network designer must keep the total loss within the limits supported by the transmitters and receivers.

Modern fibre systems also describe light in terms of electromagnetic modes. A mode is a permitted field pattern that propagates through the waveguide. This becomes important when comparing single-mode and multimode fibre. The simplified reflection picture is useful for understanding confinement, while the mode description gives engineers a more complete way to analyse propagation.

Fibre is not immune to physical damage. A sharp bend, crush, poorly made splice, contaminated connector or break can reduce the received optical power. A cable can look intact from the outside and still have a problem inside. This is why technicians use optical test instruments rather than judging a link only by appearance.

3. How does a light signal represent internet data?

Internet traffic is digital: information is represented as bits and grouped into frames and packets. At an optical transmitter, electronic circuitry encodes the outgoing data and drives a light source, commonly a laser in telecommunications equipment. The transmitter changes the optical signal according to the encoded data. The receiver uses a photodetector to convert incoming light into an electrical signal, after which electronic circuitry recovers the transmitted information.

It is tempting to picture every bit as one isolated flash of light, but real optical communication is more sophisticated. Systems use defined line coding, modulation, timing, framing and error-control methods. Depending on the technology, information can be represented through changes in optical intensity, phase or other signal properties. The receiver must distinguish the intended signal from noise and distortion.

Fibre links are often full duplex: data can travel in both directions. A system may use separate fibres for each direction, or it may use different optical wavelengths on a shared fibre. Wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) combines multiple optical channels at different wavelengths. In a passive optical network, downstream and upstream traffic use specified wavelength plans so that the two directions can share the optical distribution network.

Optical signals are not the same thing as radio waves travelling through the air. Both are electromagnetic phenomena, but the fibre confines light inside a physical waveguide, whereas Wi-Fi and mobile networks radiate radio-frequency signals through space. At home, the ONT may receive fibre and the router may then distribute connectivity over Ethernet and Wi-Fi. A fast fibre connection therefore does not guarantee that every wireless device will achieve the same speed.

4. Single-mode vs multimode fibre

Single-mode fibre (SMF) has a very small core and is designed to support one principal spatial mode at the operating wavelength. It is widely used for telecommunications access, metropolitan, long-haul and submarine links because it supports long reach and high capacity when paired with suitable optical equipment. Common ITU-T fibre families include G.652 and bend-insensitive G.657 variants used in access environments.

Multimode fibre (MMF) has a larger core and supports multiple propagation modes. It is common in many enterprise buildings and data-centre environments for shorter optical links. Different multimode categories, such as OM3, OM4 and OM5, have defined performance characteristics and are used with compatible transceivers and reach specifications.

Neither type is universally “faster” in isolation. The achievable data rate and distance depend on the complete optical system: fibre category, wavelength, transmitter, receiver, connector quality, link length and chosen Ethernet or transport standard. Single-mode is generally the practical choice for provider access networks and long distances; multimode can be economical for suitable short-reach premises and data-centre connections.

Do not select a transceiver by connector shape alone. A connector that physically fits does not prove that the optical wavelength, fibre type, transmit power, receiver sensitivity or protocol are compatible. Network equipment documentation should specify the supported optics and link budget.

5. What do FTTH, FTTB and FTTC mean?

FTTx is a family of terms describing how far optical fibre reaches. The final letter identifies the endpoint. FTTH means fibre to the home: the optical access network reaches an individual residence. FTTB means fibre to the building, where the fibre may terminate in a shared communications room and another medium distributes service inside the building. FTTC commonly means fibre to the cabinet or curb, with the final section using another access technology. FTTN describes fibre to a neighbourhood node, with a longer copper or coaxial segment remaining.

These labels matter because two plans marketed as fibre broadband may have different physical paths. In FTTH, the optical path can reach the customer’s ONT. In a hybrid arrangement, fibre may reach a street cabinet while existing copper or coaxial cable carries the final segment. The actual speed, upload capacity, latency and reliability depend on the entire access system and provider configuration.

When checking a plan, ask the provider whether fibre reaches the premises or only a nearby cabinet, what equipment will be installed, whether the ONT is included, and what upload and download speeds are provisioned. Also check the plan’s fair-use policy, router capability, installation charges and service-level terms. The word “fibre” alone does not describe every detail of the retail service.

6. How does an FTTH network reach a home?

A common FTTH design uses a passive optical network (PON). At the provider’s central office or exchange, an optical line terminal (OLT) connects the access network to the provider’s wider IP network. A feeder fibre runs from the OLT location into the optical distribution network. Passive optical splitters divide the optical signal so that one OLT port can serve multiple customer branches. Distribution and drop cables then lead towards individual homes, where an ONT receives the signal.

The word passive is important: the splitter does not need a powered electronic switch at the roadside to forward each customer’s optical signal. It divides optical power among output paths. This can reduce the need for powered field equipment, but the split introduces insertion loss. The optical budget must account for the splitter, fibre length, connectors, splices and engineering margin.

Downstream transmission is typically broadcast across the branches associated with a PON port, with the network and ONTs ensuring that each subscriber receives the traffic intended for them. Upstream transmission is coordinated so that ONTs share the optical medium without transmitting over one another. The OLT schedules upstream opportunities and manages the PON. This is one reason a PON is not simply an ordinary Ethernet switch connected to a passive splitter.

A simplified path looks like this:

  1. Provider IP network and service edge.
  2. OLT in the exchange or headend.
  3. Feeder fibre into the optical distribution network.
  4. Passive splitter in a cabinet, closure or building distribution point.
  5. Distribution and drop fibre to the premises.
  6. ONT/ONU at the customer location.
  7. Ethernet to the home router, followed by wired or Wi-Fi connections.

Actual layouts vary. Some networks use a centralised splitter; others cascade splitters at more than one location. The provider’s design must balance subscriber count, distance, optical power, capacity and the ability to test and maintain the plant.

7. What are OLT, ONT and ONU?

The OLT is the provider-side equipment that controls the PON interface. It communicates with subscriber-side optical units, manages ranging and upstream transmission, and connects customer traffic to the provider network. OLTs are typically installed in a central office, exchange, headend or suitable aggregation site.

An ONT is the optical network terminal at the customer premises. It terminates the optical access link and presents one or more service interfaces, often Ethernet and sometimes telephone or other interfaces depending on the provider. An ONU, or optical network unit, is a broader term for subscriber-side optical equipment. In everyday FTTH usage, ONT and ONU are sometimes used interchangeably, although standards and deployment contexts can distinguish them.

The home router is not necessarily the ONT. Some providers supply two separate devices: a small optical terminal and a Wi-Fi router connected by Ethernet. Other installations use an integrated gateway that combines optical termination, routing, switching and Wi-Fi. If the internet is down, knowing which box is the ONT helps identify whether the optical access link or the home network is at fault.

Do not unplug or handle the fibre connector casually. Fibre ends and optical ports need clean, compatible connectors, and active optical equipment can emit light that is not visible to the eye. Never look into a fibre connector or optical port. Leave inspection and cleaning to trained personnel using the correct tools and safety procedures.

8. GPON, XG-PON and XGS-PON explained

GPON is a widely deployed gigabit-capable passive optical network technology standardised by ITU-T. Its nominal line rates are approximately 2.488 Gbit/s downstream and 1.244 Gbit/s upstream, shared across the users on a PON port before protocol overhead and service allocation. It is not a promise that one household will always see those rates in a speed test.

XG-PON provides a nominal 10 Gbit/s downstream and 2.5 Gbit/s upstream line rate. XGS-PON provides nominal 10 Gbit/s in both downstream and upstream directions. ITU-T Recommendation G.9807.1 defines XGS-PON for point-to-multipoint optical access networks and supports residential, business and mobile-backhaul applications. The standard’s 10 Gbit/s figures describe the PON line rate, not an automatic per-subscriber retail speed.

Because PON capacity is shared, the number of subscribers, their traffic patterns, split ratio, provider provisioning and aggregation capacity all affect the service experience. Providers may also use traffic management and service profiles. An XGS-PON deployment can support higher-capacity plans, but the ONT, router, Ethernet ports, customer devices and upstream provider network must all be capable of delivering the chosen speed.

Different PON generations can use wavelength plans that allow coexistence on a shared optical distribution network, subject to compatible equipment and design rules. A customer cannot upgrade a GPON ONT to XGS-PON merely by changing a setting if the OLT port, ONT optics and service provisioning do not support it. The provider must confirm compatibility and carry out any required equipment change.

9. Why fibre broadband can be fast but a speed test can be slower

Fibre offers high-capacity transmission, but a household speed test measures the end-to-end path from a device to a test server. It includes the ONT, router, Ethernet or Wi-Fi link, device hardware, provider aggregation, internet routing and the test server itself. A weak Wi-Fi connection can become the bottleneck even when the optical link is healthy.

For a useful baseline, connect a capable computer to the router with a suitable Ethernet cable. Confirm that the computer’s Ethernet adapter and router port support the plan’s speed. Pause large downloads, cloud backups and streaming on other devices, then run more than one test using a nearby, reputable server. Compare results at different times without assuming one isolated reading proves a provider fault.

Check upload as well as download. Video calls, cloud backups, sending large files and remote work can depend heavily on upstream capacity. Also pay attention to latency, jitter and packet loss. A high download figure does not rule out congestion, unstable Wi-Fi or packet loss that makes interactive applications feel poor.

For more on measuring the connection, see why different speed tests show different results and why video calls can lag despite good internet speed. If Wi-Fi is the suspected bottleneck, compare a wired test with a wireless test near the router before changing the fibre plan.

10. What causes optical signal loss?

Optical power decreases as light travels through fibre and passes through components. This is called attenuation and is commonly expressed in decibels (dB). Fibre attenuation is specified per unit length and varies with fibre type and wavelength. Connectors, splices, splitters, bends and other passive components add loss. The total link loss must stay within the transmitter and receiver power budget.

A connector is a detachable interface. It makes equipment replacement and patching convenient, but a dirty end face, poor mating, damaged ferrule or incorrect connector type can add loss or reflections. A splice joins two fibre ends permanently. Fusion splicing aligns and melts the glass ends together using specialised equipment. A good splice has low loss, but poor preparation, contamination or misalignment can create a weak point.

Excessive bending can cause light to escape from the guided path. This is called bend loss. Fibre routing therefore follows minimum bend-radius requirements specified by the cable and equipment manufacturer. Do not coil, kink, crush or sharply fold an optical patch cord behind a router. Bend-insensitive fibre types help with constrained installations, but they do not make fibre indestructible.

Optical power that is too low can prevent the receiver from decoding the signal reliably. Too much power can also exceed the receiver’s permitted range and cause problems. Engineers calculate a loss budget using transmitter output, receiver sensitivity, component losses and safety margin. A technician should compare measured power with the limits for the actual equipment rather than assuming that a more negative or less negative dBm reading is always good.

11. How do technicians test and repair fibre?

Technicians use different instruments for different questions. An optical power meter measures received optical power at a specified wavelength. A light source and power meter can measure insertion loss across a link. A PON power meter is designed for relevant PON wavelengths and can help check live access signals. An optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) sends pulses into a fibre and analyses returned light to estimate events such as breaks, splices, connectors and changes in attenuation along the route.

OTDR testing on a PON network is more complicated than testing a simple point-to-point fibre because splitters divide the signal and create multiple branches. A trace can show a large loss at the splitter, and downstream branches may overlap in the measurement. Technicians need the network diagram, correct test wavelength, suitable pulse width and an understanding of where splitters are located. A single OTDR trace may not identify every fault on every branch.

Connector inspection and cleaning are fundamental steps. Dust and oil on a fibre end face can cause loss, reflections or even damage when connectors are mated. The correct process uses a fibre inspection microscope and approved cleaning tools. Never use fingers, household cloth or improvised liquids on an optical connector.

When a fault affects one home but neighbouring customers remain online, the problem may be in that customer’s drop cable, connector, ONT or home Ethernet connection. If many customers behind one splitter lose service together, a shared distribution fibre, splitter, feeder or provider-side port may be involved. These are clues, not a definitive diagnosis; the technician must measure the network.

12. Common FTTH problems and what customers can safely check

LOS or optical alarm light is red

An optical loss-of-signal indicator commonly means the ONT is not receiving an acceptable optical signal. Check whether the fibre cable has been visibly pulled out or crushed, but do not open closures or inspect live fibre ends. Contact the provider and report the exact light pattern and when it began.

ONT shows PON connected but Wi-Fi has no internet

If the optical status appears normal, the issue may be the router, Ethernet link, account provisioning, DNS or provider service. Restart equipment only according to the provider’s instructions. Check whether a wired device can connect and whether multiple devices are affected. Avoid repeatedly factory-resetting the ONT or router because that may erase provider settings.

Internet disconnects when the cable is moved

A loose connector, damaged patch cord or stressed bend may be involved. Stop moving the cable and ask the provider to inspect it. A fibre connector is a precision optical interface, not a normal household cable plug.

Speed is inconsistent at different times

Compare wired tests at consistent times and record download, upload, latency and test server. If the optical signal is within specification, congestion at the PON segment, provider aggregation or wider internet route may still affect performance. A speed test alone cannot identify the exact location.

Only one room has poor performance

The optical connection may be healthy while the router’s Wi-Fi coverage is not. Consider router placement, walls, interference, mesh backhaul and Ethernet wiring. Fibre brings the provider connection to the premises; it does not automatically solve indoor wireless coverage.

13. Is fibre broadband immune to weather, power cuts and outages?

Fibre is not affected by electromagnetic interference in the same way as copper electrical cabling, and the glass itself does not conduct electricity. However, fibre routes can still be damaged by excavation, construction, water ingress into poorly protected cable systems, fire, vehicle impact, rodent damage or severe environmental conditions. Submarine and underground cables need protection and repair when damaged.

FTTH equipment at the home also needs electricity. The ONT and router usually stop working during a power cut unless they have suitable backup power. Provider exchanges and network sites may have backup systems, but that does not keep a customer’s powered equipment running automatically. A small UPS can keep compatible home equipment operating for a limited period, depending on its capacity and the devices’ power draw.

Fibre can improve resilience when networks use diverse routes, protected infrastructure and well-planned backup links. A single fibre route is still a physical dependency. Ask providers about outage reporting and restoration procedures if connectivity is essential for work or business.

14. Fibre vs copper: what are the practical differences?

Fibre carries optical signals through glass; copper carries electrical signals through conductive metal. Fibre generally offers much higher capacity over longer distances and is not susceptible to electromagnetic interference in the same way. It is also electrically non-conductive along the glass path, which can be useful in environments where electrical isolation matters.

Copper can be easier to terminate and power in some short-distance applications, and Ethernet over twisted-pair copper remains common inside homes and offices. Fibre equipment and installation can require specialist skills, cleaning and test instruments. The right medium depends on distance, capacity, environment, cost, maintainability and the equipment already deployed.

In many homes the best arrangement is a combination: fibre from the provider to the ONT, then Ethernet copper from the ONT to the router and from the router to wired devices, plus Wi-Fi for mobile devices. The existence of copper inside the home does not mean the provider’s access network is not fibre.

15. What should you check before choosing a fibre plan?

  • Technology to the premises: ask whether fibre reaches your home or building, and where any copper or coaxial segment begins.
  • Download and upload: compare both advertised speeds and check whether the upload is symmetrical or substantially lower.
  • Peak-hour performance: ask about typical performance and congestion management, not only headline maximum speed.
  • ONT and router: confirm whether devices are included, rented or chargeable, and whether their Ethernet ports and Wi-Fi support your plan.
  • Data policy: check fair-use limits, post-quota speeds and any restrictions on particular traffic or services.
  • Installation and support: confirm installation fees, wiring charges, repair commitments and how faults are reported.
  • Power backup: decide whether you need a UPS for the ONT and router during short outages.
  • Building permissions: in apartments, confirm that the provider can route the drop cable and that the building has suitable access infrastructure.

Use the same practical comparison for every provider. A higher advertised number may not be the best value if the router is weak, installation costs are high, upload capacity is limited or local support is unreliable. Read the plan terms and ask for written confirmation of important details.

16. What is changing in fibre access networks?

Fibre access technology continues to evolve. XGS-PON supports nominal 10 Gbit/s line rates in both directions, and standards work continues on higher-capacity optical access systems. Operators can also use wavelength division, coexistence arrangements and more advanced optical components to increase capacity while reusing parts of existing outside plant where the standards and engineering design permit.

More bandwidth is only one part of the story. Operators need accurate network records, automated monitoring, reliable optical power measurements and efficient fault localisation. As networks become denser and serve more applications, maintenance quality and route resilience remain as important as the headline line rate.

For customers, the useful question is not simply whether a technology is labelled next-generation. Ask whether it is available at your address, what service profile is offered, whether the ONT and router support it, and whether the provider can sustain the promised performance at the times you need it.

Frequently asked questions about optical fibre and FTTH

1. How does optical fibre carry internet data?

Optical transmitters encode digital information as controlled light signals. The glass core and cladding guide the light along the fibre. At the receiving end, a photodetector converts the light back into electrical signals so network equipment can recover the data.

2. Is fibre broadband faster than copper broadband?

Fibre generally supports higher capacity and longer distances with lower signal loss than traditional copper access technologies. Actual household speed depends on the access design, provider capacity, plan, ONT, router, device and network conditions.

3. What is FTTH?

FTTH means fibre to the home. Optical fibre reaches the customer premises and connects to an ONT or ONU. The ONT commonly provides Ethernet to a router or other customer equipment.

4. What is the difference between an ONT and a router?

An ONT terminates the provider’s optical fibre connection and converts the optical service into customer-facing interfaces such as Ethernet. A router directs traffic between the home network and the provider network and often provides Wi-Fi. Some gateways combine both functions.

5. What is GPON and how is it different from XGS-PON?

GPON is a gigabit-capable passive optical network technology with nominal line rates around 2.488 Gbit/s downstream and 1.244 Gbit/s upstream. XGS-PON is a 10-Gbit/s-class system with nominal 10 Gbit/s line rates in both directions. These are shared PON line rates, not guaranteed speeds for one subscriber.

6. Does a fibre connection use Wi-Fi?

The fibre access link itself uses light in glass, not Wi-Fi radio. At home, an ONT usually connects to a router, which may distribute the internet wirelessly over Wi-Fi or through Ethernet cables.

7. Can a fibre cable be damaged by bending?

Yes. Bending below the cable’s specified minimum bend radius can increase optical loss or damage the fibre. Avoid kinks, crushing and tight loops, and have a technician inspect a cable that appears damaged or causes intermittent service.

8. Why is my fibre internet slow even when the optical signal is normal?

A healthy optical signal does not guarantee that every part of the internet path is fast. Wi-Fi interference, router limits, device hardware, PON congestion, provider aggregation, routing and test-server capacity can all affect speed.

9. What does a red LOS light on an ONT mean?

LOS usually indicates that the ONT is not receiving an acceptable optical signal. A disconnected or damaged drop cable, dirty connector, fibre break, splitter fault or provider-side issue may be responsible. Contact the ISP rather than opening optical equipment.

10. Is fibre internet available during a power cut?

The glass fibre does not need electricity along its passive path, but the ONT and router at home need power. Without a suitable UPS or backup supply, the home connection normally stops working even if the provider’s network remains operational.
