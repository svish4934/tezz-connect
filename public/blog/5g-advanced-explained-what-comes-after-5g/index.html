5G-Advanced Explained: What Comes After Today's 5G Phones?

The arrival of a new 5G phone does not mean the mobile network has stopped changing. The standards behind 5G continue to evolve, adding features for more demanding applications, connected devices, network efficiency and better use of radio resources. This next phase is commonly called 5G-Advanced. It is not a separate sixth-generation network, and it is not simply a promise that every phone will download files twice as fast. It is a collection of carefully standardised improvements that operators, equipment makers and device manufacturers can introduce over time. What a customer actually experiences depends on much more than the label on a handset or a status-bar icon.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

1. What is 5G-Advanced?

5G-Advanced is the name used for the continuing evolution of the 5G system in the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) standards. The first 5G specifications arrived in Release 15. Releases 16 and 17 added further capabilities. Release 18 marks the start of the 5G-Advanced phase, with Release 19 and Release 20 continuing that evolution.

Think of it as a long-running standards programme rather than a single product launch. A release groups technical specifications and enhancements that the mobile industry can implement in networks and devices. A feature becoming part of a standard means that compatible equipment can be designed and tested to support it. It does not mean every operator switches it on at once, every phone supports it, or that every country gets the same feature set at the same time.

3GPP's public release tracker lists Release 18 as frozen in June 2024 and Release 19 as frozen in December 2025. Release 20 remains open and carries both continued 5G-Advanced work and early 6G studies. This distinction is important: a standards release can be complete while commercial rollout, interoperability testing and device availability continue afterwards.

2. Is 5G-Advanced the same as 5.5G or 6G?

Not exactly. 5G-Advanced is the industry name associated with the 3GPP evolution beginning in Release 18. Some vendors and operators use terms such as 5.5G or 5G-A in marketing. Those labels may refer to overlapping technology, but they are not a substitute for checking the exact 3GPP release, feature and deployment.

6G is the next mobile generation and is being studied and standardised separately. Release 20 includes 6G studies alongside further 5G-Advanced work; it does not turn 5G-Advanced into 6G. The first normative 6G specifications belong to a later standards phase. For consumers, a 5G-Advanced-capable phone is still a 5G device, not a 6G phone.

3. What changes in Release 18 and Release 19?

There is no single headline feature that explains all of 5G-Advanced. The releases combine improvements across the radio interface, core network, device categories and network management. Release 18 introduced the 5G-Advanced phase. Release 19 builds on it with further work in areas including AI and machine learning, extended reality, energy efficiency, RedCap, ambient IoT and non-terrestrial networks.

The table below is a guide to the main technology themes, not a checklist of features that every phone or carrier will support.

4. Will 5G-Advanced make downloads much faster?

It can improve performance, but the answer is not a guaranteed speed multiplier. Some enhancements may increase capacity or improve how efficiently a cell uses spectrum. Others focus on uplink, mobility, latency, reliability, device power or network energy use rather than peak download speed.

A mobile connection is shaped by the available spectrum, channel bandwidth, radio conditions, antenna configuration, number of active users, backhaul, core network, server location and device capability. If a phone is connected to a congested cell or has a weak indoor signal, a new standards feature elsewhere in the network may not transform its experience.

Peak laboratory rates are not the same as everyday throughput. The most useful measure for a customer is often consistent performance at the place and time they need it, not a headline maximum measured under ideal conditions. For a practical check, compare download, upload and latency at different times and locations. If results vary sharply between rooms, investigate Wi-Fi or indoor coverage before assuming that the mobile generation is the cause.

5. Why better uplink matters as much as download

People often judge mobile networks by how quickly a video or app downloads. But phones and connected devices also send data back to the network. That uplink carries video calls, live streams, cloud backups, photos, work files, sensor readings and control information.

In crowded places, uplink performance can become a bottleneck. A phone may show a strong signal yet struggle to upload a high-resolution video because the cell has limited uplink resources or many users are transmitting at once. Improvements to uplink coverage, radio scheduling and antenna techniques can help in supported deployments.

This is especially relevant for applications where the device sends a continuous stream of information. A remote inspection camera, a connected vehicle or an industrial machine may need dependable uplink behaviour rather than a very high one-time download speed. The exact benefit still depends on the application, spectrum, device and network configuration.

6. AI in the mobile network: more than a chatbot

5G-Advanced includes work on applying AI and machine learning to parts of the radio network. These techniques can help a network estimate traffic demand, detect unusual conditions, balance load or manage energy. Some standardised use cases focus on AI/ML-assisted mobility optimisation, load balancing and network energy saving.

For example, a network may learn that certain cells become busy at predictable times and prepare resources before congestion peaks. Another model may help identify when a radio configuration is inefficient under a particular traffic pattern. Such tools must be trained, tested and monitored; a model does not automatically understand every local condition.

AI in 5G-Advanced is not a promise that networks will repair every fault by themselves or that a language model will control live radio equipment without supervision. Network operators still need safety policies, measurable performance checks, security and human oversight for high-impact actions. 7. RedCap: 5G for devices that do not need a phone's full capability

Not every connected device needs the speed, antenna complexity or power consumption of a modern smartphone. A wearable, industrial sensor, smart meter or monitoring camera may need reliable connectivity and a moderate data rate, while keeping hardware size, cost and battery use under control.

Reduced Capability (RedCap), also known as NR-Light, was introduced in Release 17 and enhanced in Release 18. It provides a more modest 5G device category for use cases between very low-rate IoT technologies and full-featured 5G devices. Release 18's enhanced RedCap (eRedCap) further targets simpler, lower-data-rate devices.

The practical value is a wider range of device designs. A wearable or sensor can use a 5G ecosystem without carrying every radio feature found in a premium phone. However, RedCap is not automatically the cheapest or best option for every sensor. Wi-Fi, Bluetooth Low Energy, LTE-M, NB-IoT and other technologies may be more appropriate depending on coverage, power, data volume, cost and service requirements.

8. Extended reality and time-sensitive communication

Extended reality (XR) includes augmented reality, virtual reality and mixed-reality experiences. Some applications send large amounts of visual information, while others require low delay between a person's movement and what appears in a headset. If that feedback loop feels slow, the experience can become uncomfortable or difficult to use.

5G-Advanced includes radio and system enhancements intended to support demanding XR and interactive media scenarios. But a good XR experience is not created by the radio network alone. Headset processing, application design, local rendering, edge placement, server performance and the path between them all matter. A 5G-Advanced label cannot guarantee a particular motion-to-photon delay or remove every source of lag.

Industrial applications can have different requirements. A remote expert viewing a machine may need reliable video uplink, while a control system may need predictable latency and carefully engineered safety. These are not interchangeable service levels. The application owner and network provider need to define and test the actual requirement.

9. Positioning: networks that can help answer “where?”

Mobile networks already use radio measurements to support location-related services. 5G-Advanced continues work on more precise positioning and timing capabilities. This could support asset tracking, logistics, industrial automation, navigation inside large facilities and location-aware services.

High accuracy is a goal for specific scenarios, not a universal promise that every phone will pinpoint its position to centimetres. Real-world accuracy depends on the number and placement of network sites, signal conditions, device support, positioning methods and the environment. Indoor reflections, obstructions and limited infrastructure can affect results.

Positioning also raises privacy questions. A system that can locate devices more precisely needs clear rules about consent, access, purpose, data retention and protection. Technical capability should not be confused with permission to track a person.

10. Satellite integration: wider reach, not satellite broadband everywhere

Non-terrestrial network (NTN) work brings satellite connectivity into the broader mobile standards ecosystem. It can help extend service to places where building terrestrial towers is difficult or uneconomic, and it may support selected direct-to-device or IoT use cases.

But satellite-to-phone service has practical limits. The supported phone, satellite constellation, frequency band, operator agreement, regulatory authorisation, sky visibility and service design all matter. Some early services focus on messaging or emergency communication rather than ordinary high-speed mobile broadband. A phone that supports 5G does not automatically connect to a satellite.

5G-Advanced NTN development is therefore best understood as an expansion of the connectivity toolkit. It can complement fibre, terrestrial mobile networks and Wi-Fi; it does not make those systems unnecessary. Our article on undersea internet cables explains another part of the global connectivity system that satellite links may complement during selected disruptions.

11. Network slicing and private 5G: different services on shared infrastructure

Network slicing allows an operator to configure logical network capabilities for different service needs on shared physical infrastructure. A factory, event venue or logistics operator may want connectivity with particular performance, security or management characteristics. A slice can help define and manage those characteristics when the network architecture, spectrum, core and operational systems support it.

It is not a magic reserved lane that guarantees performance regardless of congestion or engineering. The service-level agreement, admission control, radio resources, transport, core network and application all need to work together. Some advanced slicing capabilities depend on 5G Standalone architecture and a suitable end-to-end implementation. A handset showing a 5G icon does not tell you whether a dedicated slice is active.

Private 5G networks can be useful in industrial campuses, ports, mines, warehouses and other controlled environments. Whether they are better than Wi-Fi or wired Ethernet depends on mobility, coverage, interference, device ecosystem, latency, security and total cost. 5G-Advanced adds capabilities to the broader system, but it does not make every enterprise site a candidate for private cellular.

12. Energy efficiency: doing more with less power

Mobile networks consume energy in radio equipment, base stations, cooling, transport and data-centre infrastructure. Traffic changes throughout the day, and the busiest configuration is not always needed at every moment. 5G-Advanced includes work on energy-saving features and AI-assisted methods that can help operators adapt network resources to demand.

For example, a network may use traffic forecasts and measurements to manage selected radio resources during quiet periods. The challenge is to save power without reducing coverage, creating wake-up delays, harming handovers or degrading service for users who still need the cell. Operators must measure both energy use and customer experience.

Energy efficiency is therefore a system-design issue. Better radio hardware, efficient software, cooling, renewable electricity, traffic management and equipment lifecycle decisions all contribute. No single 5G-Advanced feature can by itself make a mobile network carbon-neutral.

13. What does 5G Standalone have to do with it?

5G Standalone (SA) uses a 5G radio access network connected to a 5G Core. Non-Standalone (NSA) deployments use 5G radio together with an existing LTE core architecture. Both can deliver 5G radio service, but the architecture affects which end-to-end capabilities an operator can offer.

Some advanced capabilities—such as more complete network slicing, certain service-based core functions and particular enterprise services—are tied to a 5G Core and a suitable SA deployment. Other radio enhancements can be introduced in stages and should be assessed feature by feature. It is inaccurate to say that every 5G-Advanced feature requires SA, just as it is inaccurate to say that an NSA network can deliver every SA capability.

When comparing operator claims, look for the actual service architecture and supported feature, not only the words “5G” or “5G-A”.

14. What does this mean for India?

India has built a large 5G footprint, but nationwide 5G availability should not be confused with nationwide 5G-Advanced deployment. A Ministry of Communications statement in March 2026 reported that 5G services were available in 99.9% of districts and that 5.23 lakh 5G BTS had been installed as of 28 February 2026. Those figures describe the 5G rollout at that date; they do not establish how many sites had specific Release 18 or Release 19 features enabled.

For Indian users, the arrival of 5G-Advanced capabilities will depend on operator investment, spectrum, network architecture, software upgrades, device support and commercial priorities. Some improvements may first appear in dense urban areas, enterprise deployments or selected bands before becoming more widely available. There is no reason to assume that every city, plan or handset will receive the same feature set at the same time.

India's use cases may include industrial automation, logistics, smart infrastructure, connected healthcare, public safety, agriculture and improved fixed wireless access. Each application needs a real service design and a viable business case. A new standards feature is an enabling tool, not proof that a use case has already been deployed at scale.

15. How can you tell whether your phone supports 5G-Advanced?

Do not rely only on a 5G icon or a marketing phrase. Check the exact phone model and regional variant, supported frequency bands, modem capabilities and the manufacturer's specification for the relevant 3GPP features. Then check whether your mobile operator has deployed the feature in your location and whether your plan and SIM provisioning support the service.

Even a compatible phone cannot use a feature that the local network has not enabled. Conversely, an operator may deploy an enhancement that is invisible to customers because it improves network efficiency rather than adding a new status-bar label. If a vendor advertises “5G-A”, ask which Release 18 or Release 19 capabilities are supported and whether they are available commercially on the network you use.

16. What comes next?

5G-Advanced will continue to evolve through further 3GPP releases. Release 20 is carrying additional 5G work while opening studies for 6G. The next generation will eventually require its own standards, devices, spectrum strategy and deployment plans. Until then, 5G-Advanced is the practical path for improving the existing 5G system.

For users, the most meaningful changes may not always look dramatic. Better uplink, more dependable mobility, support for simpler connected devices, improved network energy management and new enterprise capabilities could matter more than a peak-speed headline. The network is becoming more capable in more dimensions—not simply faster in one.

Frequently asked questions

What is 5G-Advanced in simple words?

It is the next phase in the evolution of 5G standards, beginning with 3GPP Release 18. It adds improvements across performance, efficiency, AI/ML, devices and new use cases.

Is 5G-Advanced the same as 6G?

No. 5G-Advanced continues the 5G system. 6G is the next generation and is being studied and standardised separately.

Is 5G-Advanced faster than normal 5G?

Some features can improve capacity or throughput, but there is no universal speed multiplier. Results depend on spectrum, network deployment, device and local conditions.

Does 5G-Advanced need a new SIM card?

Not automatically. Requirements depend on the operator, service, device and provisioning. Check with the operator before replacing a SIM.

Will my existing 5G phone support 5G-Advanced?

Not necessarily. Support depends on the modem, software, regional model and exact feature. A 5G-capable phone is not automatically compatible with every 5G-Advanced enhancement.

What is the difference between Release 18 and Release 19?

Release 18 introduced the 5G-Advanced phase. Release 19 adds further enhancements in areas such as AI/ML, XR, energy efficiency, RedCap and NTN.

What is RedCap in 5G?

RedCap is a reduced-capability 5G device category designed for connected devices that need less complexity and data capability than a smartphone, such as wearables and industrial sensors.

Does 5G-Advanced include satellite connectivity?

It continues work on non-terrestrial networks, including satellite integration. Availability and supported services depend on satellites, devices, spectrum, operators and regulation.

Does 5G-Advanced require 5G Standalone?

Some end-to-end capabilities depend on a 5G Core and Standalone deployment. Other radio enhancements may be introduced separately. Requirements vary by feature.

Is 5G-Advanced available across India?

India has wide 5G availability, but that does not prove that every operator has deployed every 5G-Advanced feature nationwide. Check specific operator and device support.

What is 5.5G?

It is a term used by some industry participants for advanced 5G capabilities. Check the exact standards release and deployed feature rather than relying on the label alone.

When will 6G replace 5G-Advanced?

There is no single switch-off date. 5G and its advanced releases will continue to operate while 6G standards, devices and networks are developed and deployed.
