The Internet in 2040, 2075 and 2125: Scenarios, Not Predictions

Imagine opening a map, asking a question, joining a video call or sending money in the year 2040. The tools may look different, but the basic need will feel familiar: people want information, connection and services they can trust. Now move the clock to 2075, then 2125. It becomes much harder to describe what the internet will be, because technology, politics, climate, energy, economics and human choices can change in ways that no current forecast can reliably capture. This article does not claim to know the future. It separates developments that are already being researched or standardised from possibilities that depend on uncertain breakthroughs and public decisions. The aim is to build useful scenarios—not make promises about a century that has not happened.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

1. Why a century-long internet forecast would be misleading

Technology forecasts often become less reliable as the time horizon grows. A plan for the next few years can be checked against equipment trials, standards work, investment and regulation. A view of 2040 can be informed by research that is already underway. By 2075, assumptions about energy, materials, computing and society become much less certain. By 2125, even the devices and institutions we take for granted may have changed.

There is a useful distinction between a trend, a target, a scenario and a prediction. A trend is a direction visible in current evidence, such as the growth of AI compute or satellite connectivity. A target is a goal set by a standards body or government, not a guarantee that every network will achieve it. A scenario is a coherent description of one possible future under stated assumptions. A prediction claims that a particular outcome is likely to happen.

For this reason, the dates in this article are thinking tools, not deadlines. The 2040 section stays close to current research. The 2075 section describes conditional possibilities. The 2125 section offers several contrasting futures to show how decisions made now can influence the direction of change. None is assigned a probability.

2. The starting point: the internet in 2026 is still unfinished

It is tempting to imagine the future as a story of faster speeds, smarter devices and more immersive screens. But the most important fact about today's internet may be who cannot use it meaningfully. The International Telecommunication Union estimated that 6.3 billion people—76 per cent of the world's population—were online in 2026, while 2 billion people remained offline. The ITU also reported a large income divide: internet use was estimated at 93 per cent in high-income countries and 32 per cent in low-income countries.

Being technically within mobile coverage is not the same as having affordable, reliable and useful access. People may lack a suitable device, electricity, digital skills, accessible services, local-language content or confidence that their data will be handled responsibly. A connection that drops during a lesson or costs too much to use is not meaningful connectivity.

That is the baseline for every future scenario. If the world builds advanced networks but leaves communities without affordable access, the future internet may become more powerful while opportunity remains unequal. If investment also reaches rural areas, schools, clinics, small businesses and people with disabilities, connectivity can become a broader public resource.

3. The internet around 2040: a plausible extension of work already underway

2040 is close enough for today's research programmes and standards decisions to matter. It is also far enough away for technology generations to change. A reasonable scenario is not that one new network replaces everything, but that several systems work together: fibre, Wi-Fi, mobile networks, satellite links, local edge computing, data centres and connected devices.

6G may be part of the network mix

The ITU calls the next generation of mobile systems IMT-2030, commonly known as 6G. Its framework identifies six usage scenarios: immersive communication; hyper-reliable and low-latency communication; massive communication; ubiquitous connectivity; AI and communication; and integrated sensing and communication. In 2026, ITU working groups had advanced draft technical requirements and evaluation methods, with candidate technology evaluation work still ahead. These are standards-development steps, not proof that a particular 6G feature will be available everywhere by a fixed date.

By 2040, some markets may have mature 6G deployments while others still rely heavily on 5G, 4G, Wi-Fi and fibre. A 6G label will not guarantee the same coverage, speed or latency in every country. Spectrum, device cost, power supply, terrain, backhaul and business models will shape real-world performance.

AI may become part of how networks are operated

Networks already use automation for monitoring, traffic management and fault detection. A 2040 scenario could include more AI systems predicting congestion, coordinating radio resources, managing energy use and helping operators diagnose faults. Some user services may also use AI agents to complete tasks across different applications.

That raises new questions. Who authorised an agent to spend money, share personal information or change a setting? How can a user verify which service is speaking? How does an operator stop an automated system from making a harmful change? Future convenience will depend on identity, permissions, audit trails, security and human control—not just model capability.

Satellite and terrestrial networks may cooperate more closely

Satellite systems can extend coverage to remote areas, ships, aircraft and emergency teams. They may complement mobile networks through direct-to-device services, backhaul or dedicated satellite terminals. The details matter: a satellite text service is not the same as broadband, and neither is identical to a satellite phone. By 2040, hybrid connectivity may be more common, but geography, spectrum, capacity, cost and regulation will still determine what works.

Security will need to adapt

Post-quantum cryptography is a current engineering transition, not a distant science-fiction feature. NIST has standardised post-quantum algorithms, and the IETF published RFC 9958 in June 2026 to help engineers understand the transition's impact on protocols and infrastructure. By 2040, many systems may have migrated, but legacy equipment and long-lived devices could make the change uneven. A future internet will need secure updates, crypto-agility and careful management of old systems.

Digital experiences may become more spatial

Lightweight glasses, voice interfaces, haptic devices, real-time translation and shared 3D workspaces could become useful in some settings. They may help remote maintenance, medical training, design, education and collaboration. Yet screens and keyboards are likely to remain practical for many tasks. The future is more likely to add interaction choices than to make one interface compulsory for everyone.

4. A day online in 2040: one illustrative scenario

Consider a family living in a small Indian city. A student attends a class that mixes a conventional video window with a shared 3D model. The connection moves between home fibre and a mobile network without the student needing to understand the handover. A parent asks a personal digital assistant to compare public transport options, but the assistant must show which services supplied the information and request permission before booking a ticket. A local clinic uses secure remote consultation tools, with a human clinician making the medical decisions.

Later, a storm damages a fibre route. The home connection becomes slower, but mobile coverage and a separate backhaul path keep some essential services available. A public alert arrives through an official channel. The family downloads school material for offline use and checks local authority updates through a radio broadcast when mobile data becomes congested.

Nothing in this example requires a magical invention. It combines technologies and practices that exist or are being developed today. The uncertain part is how widely they will be deployed, how affordable they will become and whether the systems will interoperate safely.

5. The internet around 2075: possibilities that depend on major choices

Looking toward 2075 requires more caution. A 49-year horizon gives time for new materials, computing methods, energy systems, human-computer interfaces and institutions to emerge. It also gives time for technologies to fail commercially, be restricted, or be replaced by approaches that are difficult to imagine today.

Computing could become less visible

One possible future is ambient computing: small, specialised systems embedded in buildings, vehicles, public infrastructure and personal devices. Instead of opening a separate app for every task, people may interact with services through voice, gestures, displays, wearables or context-aware systems. Much of the computation could happen locally, in nearby edge facilities or in distant data centres, depending on privacy, latency, cost and power.

This does not mean that every room must be full of sensors. Some people may prefer fewer connected objects, physical controls and devices that work without cloud accounts. Privacy rules, energy use, repairability and user choice could limit where ambient systems are installed.

Quantum networks may serve specialised purposes

Research into quantum communication includes quantum key distribution, entanglement distribution, quantum repeaters and links between quantum processors. These technologies could support specialised scientific, security or distributed-computing applications if major engineering challenges are solved. They should not be confused with a forecast that ordinary internet traffic will move onto a quantum internet.

ITU technical work on quantum networks describes how conventional digital networks continue to provide essential control, signalling, synchronisation and error-correction communication for quantum systems. In other words, quantum networking may complement classical infrastructure rather than replace the internet protocol networks used by people every day.

Machines may exchange more information with other machines

Connected infrastructure could coordinate transport, energy, water, agriculture and manufacturing. Robots and autonomous systems may share information with public networks and local computing systems. That could improve efficiency and safety, but it would also create new dependencies. A failure in identity, timing, software updates or communications could affect physical systems. Strong isolation, fallback controls and human accountability would remain essential.

Access and ownership could follow different models

By 2075, people might access connectivity through public infrastructure, commercial subscriptions, community networks, satellite services or new cooperative models. Some services could be highly personalised; others may be deliberately simple and open. Whether people can move their data, switch providers and use independent tools will depend on standards, competition, law and social choices.

6. The internet in 2125: three contrasting scenarios

A century is too distant for a single forecast. Instead, consider three scenarios. They are not predictions, and the real world could combine elements of all three.

Scenario A: The open and interoperable internet

In this future, international standards and open protocols remain strong. Devices and services from different providers can communicate. People can move between platforms without losing their identity, data or access to essential services. Community networks and public investment help connect remote areas. AI systems operate with clear permissions, security and accountability. Privacy and freedom of expression are protected through laws and technical design.

The network may use technologies that do not exist today, but the defining feature is continuity of the internet's open architecture: different networks can interconnect without one company or government controlling every layer. Innovation remains possible because people can build new services on common foundations.

Scenario B: The fragmented digital world

In this future, geopolitical competition, incompatible regulations, proprietary platforms and national security restrictions create separate digital ecosystems. Some regions may have strong domestic networks but limited interoperability with others. Identity systems, cloud platforms, AI agents and data rules may not work across borders. Access to information and digital services could depend heavily on where a person lives.

Fragmentation does not require the physical internet to disappear. Fibre cables, satellites and radio networks may still exist, while the services, rules and gateways layered on top become harder to connect. The result could be a world where communication is technically possible but costly, restricted or difficult to trust.

Scenario C: The resilient and resource-aware internet

In this future, climate adaptation, energy and water constraints influence every infrastructure decision. Networks use more efficient equipment, repairable devices, circular materials and diverse energy sources. Local computing supports essential services when long-distance connectivity is unavailable. Public agencies, communities and operators coordinate disaster recovery. Digital services are designed to work at different bandwidths and on older devices, rather than assuming constant high-speed access.

Some services may be less visually elaborate than today's immersive applications, because communities prioritise reliability, accessibility and lower resource use. Progress is measured not only by peak speed, but by whether the network remains useful during heat, storms, power disruptions and supply-chain shocks.

7. What might stay familiar even as technology changes?

Even if radios, devices and computing systems change dramatically, some needs may remain remarkably stable. People will want to communicate, learn, earn, create, organise, exchange value and access public services. They will need to know who or what they are interacting with, whether information is authentic, and how their personal data is used.

The internet's layered design may also remain useful. A global network can connect many independently operated networks through shared protocols. The physical links may change from copper and fibre to new optical, wireless or satellite systems, but interoperability remains valuable. The Internet Society argues that decisions which fragment the open global internet can undermine the ability of networks and services to connect efficiently.

Not every future service needs to be permanently online. Offline-first design, local storage, peer-to-peer communication and local public infrastructure can make essential tools more dependable. A resilient internet should allow people to disconnect safely, recover from outages and retain control over their information.

8. The constraints that technology cannot simply wish away

Future networks will be shaped by physical limits and public choices. Spectrum is finite and shared. Fibre routes require land, rights of way and maintenance. Satellites need launch capacity, spectrum coordination, ground infrastructure and safe orbital practices. Data centres need electricity, cooling, water management, chips and skilled workers. Devices require minerals, manufacturing and end-of-life systems.

Energy and environmental impact will remain central. More efficient processors can reduce energy per computation, but overall demand may still grow if society runs far more workloads. Water use can vary by cooling design and location. Hardware manufacturing and replacement create impacts that do not appear on a monthly electricity bill. Future services will need transparent measurement rather than vague claims that a technology is automatically sustainable.

Cybersecurity and trust may be even more important as digital services become connected to physical infrastructure. A compromised system could affect transport, healthcare, finance or energy. Long-lived devices need secure updates; AI systems need meaningful oversight; and critical services need tested fallback modes. A fast network is not a good network if people cannot rely on it.

Finally, access is a social and economic issue. A technically available service may remain out of reach because of price, disability barriers, language, skills, electricity or a lack of local relevance. Universal connectivity requires more than towers and cables.

9. India's role in shaping the future internet

India has a significant opportunity to contribute to next-generation communications through research, manufacturing, standards participation and deployment. The Bharat 6G Vision sets an ambition for India to be a contributor to 6G design, development and deployment by 2030. Government updates in June 2026 reported 136 research and development projects under the Telecom Technology Development Fund, with sanctioned funding of nearly ₹543 crore across areas including 6G, quantum communication, satellite and non-terrestrial networks, optical systems and telecom security.

These figures describe research support and projects, not proof that all technologies are ready for commercial use. Research outcomes depend on prototypes, testing, spectrum policy, standards, manufacturing capability, investment and the availability of affordable devices.

India's future internet will also depend on work that is less visible than a new radio generation: extending fibre and reliable power, maintaining rural and remote networks, improving affordability, building digital skills, supporting local-language services, protecting privacy and securing critical infrastructure. A 6G laboratory can be important, but so can a repair team restoring a fibre cut after a flood.

India can shape the global future by contributing interoperable standards, practical engineering, open research, affordable equipment and solutions designed for varied geography and income levels. The aim should be to build technology that works for people, not only technology that looks advanced in a demonstration.

10. Decisions made now will shape the choices available later

Long-range scenarios are useful because they reveal decisions that cannot be postponed indefinitely. Some infrastructure lasts for decades. Standards, spectrum allocations, buildings, fibre corridors, data-centre locations and device ecosystems create path dependencies. Once deployed at scale, they can be expensive to change.

Several choices matter across all three horizons:

  • Keep networks interoperable: Support open standards and practical ways for independent networks and services to connect.
  • Make access meaningful: Treat affordability, skills, accessibility, language and reliability as core connectivity goals.
  • Build security into the lifecycle: Plan for post-quantum migration, software updates, identity protection and secure recovery.
  • Protect human agency: Give people understandable controls over AI agents, personal data, automated decisions and connected devices.
  • Design for disruption: Use diverse routes, local fallback, tested recovery plans and multiple communication channels.
  • Measure environmental impact: Track electricity, water, emissions, materials and equipment lifetimes with transparent boundaries.
  • Preserve room for innovation: Avoid locking essential services into closed platforms that prevent new providers and community solutions from emerging.
  • Include people in governance: Technology decisions should involve public institutions, engineers, businesses, researchers and communities affected by the systems.

None of these decisions guarantees a perfect future. They make it more likely that future generations inherit choices rather than constraints.

11. How to read future technology claims without being misled

When a company announces a future network, device or AI system, ask what stage it has reached. Is it a research paper, laboratory demonstration, prototype, standard, field trial, commercial launch or widely available service? Those are different milestones.

Check who set the performance target and what it measures. A peak data rate in a controlled test is not the same as an everyday user experience. A government vision document describes an ambition. A standards framework sets common direction. A commercial product announcement describes a supplier's offering. None alone proves broad availability or affordability.

Look for independent testing, transparent methods, real deployment data, security review, energy and water reporting, device support and regulatory approval. Be careful with exact dates for technologies that depend on many organisations and countries. The farther the claim reaches into the future, the more clearly its assumptions should be stated.

A useful future article should leave readers with better questions, not a list of guaranteed inventions.

12. The question for 2125 is not only what the internet can do

By 2125, today's familiar devices may be museum pieces. Some network technologies may have disappeared, and others may have become so ordinary that people rarely notice them. But the central question will still matter: does the communications system help people participate in society, share knowledge, build opportunity and solve common problems?

One future could connect almost everyone through open, secure and resilient infrastructure. Another could deliver extraordinary computing power while dividing people into incompatible digital territories. A third could put reliability, sustainability and local control ahead of constant expansion. Each is shaped by choices about standards, investment, governance, rights and access.

We cannot know which future will arrive. We can decide whether to build systems that are more open, secure, inclusive, repairable and resilient. That is a more useful ambition than trying to predict the exact shape of the internet one hundred years from now.

Frequently asked questions

1. What will the internet look like in 2040?

No one can know exactly. A plausible scenario includes wider use of AI-assisted services, more advanced mobile networks, satellite-terrestrial connectivity, stronger cryptographic migration and more connected devices. Availability will vary by country and community.

2. Will 6G be available everywhere by 2030?

No such guarantee exists. IMT-2030 is an active standards and evaluation process. Commercial deployment, coverage, devices, spectrum and affordability will differ across markets and over time.

3. What is the difference between a 6G target and real-world performance?

A target is a technical requirement or goal assessed under defined conditions. Everyday performance depends on spectrum, network design, distance, interference, backhaul, device capability, traffic and local deployment.

4. Will AI replace websites and apps?

AI agents may complete more tasks across services, but websites, apps, APIs and human-controlled interfaces may continue to coexist. Trust, permissions, accessibility and user choice will influence how people interact with digital services.

5. Will satellite internet replace fibre and mobile networks?

It is more likely to complement terrestrial networks. Fibre provides high-capacity fixed links, mobile networks provide local wireless access, and satellites can extend coverage or provide alternate paths. Each has different capacity, cost and deployment constraints.

6. Will there be a quantum internet by 2075?

Quantum networks are an active research and standardisation area, but their future scale and applications are uncertain. They are expected to complement conventional networks for specialised functions rather than automatically replace today's internet.

7. Will quantum computers make today's internet insecure?

A sufficiently capable cryptographically relevant quantum computer could threaten some widely used public-key algorithms. The security transition is already underway through post-quantum cryptography standards and engineering work. The timing of any such computer remains uncertain.

8. What could the internet look like in 2125?

It is too far away for a reliable prediction. Possible scenarios include an open global network, a fragmented set of regional digital systems, or a highly resilient and resource-aware infrastructure. Actual outcomes may combine elements of these futures.

9. Will everyone in the world be connected by 2040?

That is a goal, not a certainty. Connectivity depends on infrastructure, affordability, electricity, suitable devices, skills, accessibility, language and local relevance—not coverage alone.

10. Could the internet become fragmented?

Yes. Incompatible technical rules, restrictions, proprietary platforms and geopolitical tensions could make networks and services harder to interoperate. Open standards and cross-border cooperation can help reduce this risk.

11. Will future internet services use less energy?

They may become more efficient per unit of computing or data, but total energy demand could still rise if usage grows faster. Sustainability will depend on electricity sources, cooling, hardware lifetimes, network design and transparent measurement.

12. What should India focus on for the future internet?

India can invest in research and standards while also improving affordable access, fibre and power reliability, local-language services, digital skills, privacy, cybersecurity, disaster resilience and domestic technology capability.
