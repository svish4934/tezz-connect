Post-Quantum Cryptography: How the Internet Is Preparing for Quantum Computers

A future quantum computer could undermine some of the public-key cryptography that helps secure websites, apps and digital identity. The response is already taking shape—but it is a long, careful migration, not an overnight switch.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

When you open a banking website, sign in to an email account or send a private message, cryptography works quietly in the background. It helps devices establish shared secrets, checks that information has not been altered and verifies that a service is connected to the right identity.

Some of the public-key mathematics used for these tasks could be broken by a sufficiently powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computer. That machine does not exist today in a form known to threaten internet cryptography at scale. But replacing cryptography across browsers, phones, servers, certificates, payment systems and embedded devices takes years. Waiting until a threat is demonstrated would leave too little time.

This is why standards bodies and technology providers are preparing for post-quantum cryptography (PQC): cryptographic algorithms designed to run on ordinary computers while resisting attacks from both classical and quantum computers. This article reflects primary-source information available on 22 November 2026.

1. What exactly is the quantum threat?

Today's public-key cryptography uses mathematical problems that are believed to be difficult for conventional computers. RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring large integers. Elliptic-curve systems rely on discrete-logarithm problems. These techniques are used in key establishment and digital signatures across many internet protocols.

A sufficiently capable quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve the mathematical problems behind widely used RSA and elliptic-curve systems much more efficiently than a classical computer. That would affect important parts of secure connection setup and identity verification.

It does not mean a quantum computer can automatically read every encrypted file, guess every password or make all existing encryption useless. Symmetric encryption and cryptographic hash functions face a different quantum threat profile. The engineering response depends on the algorithm and its role; it is not accurate to say that all encryption is equally broken.

There is also no dependable public date for when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will be available. Estimates vary, and technical obstacles remain. The uncertainty is a reason to plan carefully—not a reason to claim that current banking or HTTPS traffic is already being decrypted by quantum machines.

2. Why security teams worry about “harvest now, decrypt later”

Imagine an organisation records an encrypted communication today. The attacker cannot read it with current equipment, but stores the captured data. If a powerful quantum computer becomes available years later, the attacker may try to decrypt the older recording if it used a vulnerable key-establishment method.

This is often called harvest now, decrypt later. It matters most when information remains sensitive for a long time: confidential business plans, government records, intellectual property, long-lived identity data or private communications that should remain private for many years.

The risk is different from stealing a password today. It is about the future confidentiality of data captured now. It also explains why migration cannot wait until a quantum computer is publicly demonstrated: the data may already have been collected.

Digital signatures have a different concern. An attacker cannot normally store a signature today and later use quantum computing to reveal the signed message's confidentiality. Instead, a future quantum-capable attacker could threaten the trust placed in vulnerable signature algorithms—for example, by forging signatures or undermining certificate and software-signing systems. Those systems also need a transition, even though the “harvest now” issue primarily concerns confidentiality.

3. Post-quantum cryptography does not mean a quantum internet

The name can be confusing. Post-quantum cryptography is not quantum communication, quantum key distribution or a network of quantum computers. PQC algorithms are mathematical methods implemented in conventional software and hardware. A laptop, phone or server can use them without containing a quantum processor.

Quantum networking research—covered separately in our future of the internet pillar article—looks at distributing quantum states between specialised devices. PQC solves a different problem: protecting cryptographic operations on the ordinary internet against future quantum attacks.

For most websites and apps, the likely transition will happen inside operating systems, browsers, server software, cryptographic libraries, network protocols and certificate infrastructure. Users may notice software updates or compatibility changes, but they will not need to buy a “quantum internet” router.

4. The three NIST standards to know

In August 2024, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published three final post-quantum cryptography standards. They are important reference points for the wider technology industry. They do different jobs, so they should not be described as three interchangeable encryption methods.

StandardAlgorithmWhat it does
FIPS 203ML-KEMKey encapsulation: lets two parties establish a shared secret over a public channel. That secret can then be used with symmetric cryptography.
FIPS 204ML-DSADigital signatures: helps verify who signed data and detect unauthorised changes.
FIPS 205SLH-DSADigital signatures based on hash functions, providing a different design approach from ML-DSA.

ML-KEM is based on the Module Learning with Errors problem. NIST specifies ML-KEM-512, ML-KEM-768 and ML-KEM-1024 parameter sets. A key-encapsulation mechanism does not directly encrypt a whole website session; it helps the parties establish a shared key, which is then used by symmetric algorithms for the actual data protection.

ML-DSA is a lattice-based digital-signature standard. SLH-DSA is a stateless hash-based signature standard. Signature algorithms are used in places such as software updates, certificates and other systems that need to establish authenticity and integrity. Which algorithm is suitable depends on the application, implementation constraints and applicable security requirements.

NIST has also published errata and planning notes for some standards. Implementers should check the current standard pages and updates rather than treating a 2024 PDF as permanently frozen.

5. What changes when a website uses HTTPS?

HTTPS uses Transport Layer Security (TLS) to protect a connection between a browser and a website. In a simplified connection setup, the browser and server authenticate the service and agree on shared key material. The session then uses symmetric cryptography to protect the data travelling in both directions.

Public-key cryptography plays important roles in that process, but it is not the same thing as the bulk encryption used for every page, image or download. A PQC migration therefore needs to update the vulnerable public-key parts while preserving the complete security properties of the protocol.

One approach being deployed and evaluated is hybrid key establishment. In a hybrid design, a conventional key-establishment method and a post-quantum method contribute to the session secret. Properly designed combinations can provide a safety margin during transition: if one component later proves weaker than expected, the other may still protect the result. Hybrid operation can also add computational cost and larger handshake messages, so protocol designers must test performance, compatibility and reliability.

The exact algorithms and combinations depend on the protocol version, implementation and current standards guidance. A website displaying a padlock does not tell a visitor which key-establishment method was negotiated. That detail is normally handled by the browser and server software.

6. Why the migration takes so long

Changing one algorithm in one server is relatively straightforward. Changing cryptography across the internet is not. Organisations often do not have a complete list of where cryptography is used. It may be hidden in a cloud service, a hardware security module, a mobile application, a router, a payment terminal, an old database, a certificate authority or a supplier's software.

Some equipment cannot be updated easily. A device may have a long service life, limited memory or processing capacity, or a vendor that no longer provides support. Larger keys and signatures can affect network packet sizes, handshake performance, storage, bandwidth and constrained devices. Systems also need to interoperate: a secure new algorithm is not useful if two endpoints cannot agree on it.

NIST's final October 2026 report, NISTIR 8547, sets out a transition approach for moving away from quantum-vulnerable algorithms toward post-quantum key-establishment and signature standards. It stresses that migration is complex and that different use cases need different priorities. The report also discusses hybrid approaches and the importance of understanding an organisation's cryptographic supply chain.

In the United States, federal migration milestones include 2030 for key establishment in high-value assets and high-impact systems, 2031 for digital signatures in those systems, and a broader 2035 target. These are US federal policy timelines; they are not a global deadline imposed on every website or a statement of Indian regulatory requirements.

7. What does this mean for India and everyday users?

For a person using UPI, net banking, email, social media or a shopping app in India, the transition will mostly be handled by service providers, operating-system vendors, browser developers, cloud platforms and the companies that maintain payment and identity infrastructure.

It would be premature to claim that every Indian bank, telecom operator or government service has completed a PQC migration. Publicly available global standards do not prove the implementation status of a particular Indian service. That needs to be checked through the organisation's own technical disclosures or regulator guidance.

For now, sensible user habits remain useful:

  • Keep your phone, computer, browser and applications updated.
  • Use supported devices and software; stop relying on products that no longer receive security updates.
  • Install banking and payment apps from their official sources and protect accounts with strong authentication.
  • Do not install a tool that promises to “make your phone quantum-proof” unless it comes from a trusted provider and has a clear, independently verifiable purpose.
  • Do not disable HTTPS warnings or certificate checks to make an old application work.

There is no general need for a household to replace its Wi-Fi router or change its broadband plan simply because PQC standards exist. The important changes occur in cryptographic software and services, not in the speed tier of a home internet connection.

8. What organisations should do before the deadline

For businesses, universities, service providers and public institutions, the first step is visibility. You cannot replace cryptography you do not know you are using. A practical programme usually begins with a cryptographic inventory: where algorithms, keys, certificates and cryptographic modules appear, who owns them, what data they protect and how long that data needs to remain confidential.

Next, prioritise systems by exposure and consequence. Long-lived sensitive information may make key-establishment migration urgent. Software signing, identity systems and certificate infrastructure need their own plans because signature migration involves different dependencies and coordination.

Organisations should ask suppliers about supported PQC standards, hybrid options, update paths, interoperability testing, performance on constrained devices, certificate and key-management plans, and how cryptographic agility will be maintained. A product label saying “quantum safe” is not a substitute for an implementation statement and a tested migration plan.

Finally, test in stages. Measure handshake size and latency, CPU and memory use, failure rates, compatibility with older clients and behaviour under real network conditions. Keep rollback and incident-response plans. Cryptographic migration is a systems-engineering project, not just a library upgrade.

9. What is likely to happen between now and 2035?

The following is a way to understand the direction of travel, not a prediction that every organisation will follow the same calendar.

  • 2026–2027: Organisations use the final NIST transition guidance to identify vulnerable cryptography, classify sensitive data and test PQC-capable products. Protocol and vendor support continues to evolve.
  • 2028–2030: Migration activity is likely to become more visible in high-value systems, cloud services, identity infrastructure and long-lived data protection. US federal policy sets earlier milestones for designated high-value and high-impact systems.
  • 2031–2035: More systems are expected to complete signature and key-establishment transitions. NIST's broader federal target is 2035, with the detailed treatment of individual algorithms defined in its standards and transition guidance.
  • Beyond 2035: Legacy equipment and long-lived systems may still need special handling. Cryptographic agility—the ability to replace algorithms without rebuilding an entire product—will remain important as standards and threat assessments evolve.

10. The practical takeaway

Quantum computing is a serious long-term consideration for public-key cryptography, but it is not a reason to panic about today's internet connection. NIST's post-quantum standards are published, migration guidance is available and the work is moving from research into implementation.

The transition will be largely invisible to most people when it is done well. Browsers, servers, apps and network services will gradually learn to use new cryptographic methods. The real challenge is coordinating the change across a vast ecosystem without breaking compatibility or weakening security along the way.

For readers, the best immediate action is to keep software current and rely on reputable providers. For organisations, the work starts with knowing where cryptography lives and planning its replacement before the data or systems become exposed.

Frequently asked questions

Can quantum computers hack my bank account today?

There is no public evidence that a cryptographically relevant quantum computer currently exists that can break the public-key cryptography protecting ordinary banking traffic at scale. Keep your software updated and follow normal account-security practices.

Does post-quantum cryptography use a quantum computer?

No. PQC algorithms run on conventional computers. They are designed to resist attacks from both classical computers and future quantum computers.

Will quantum computers break all encryption?

No. The best-known threat from Shor's algorithm targets certain public-key systems such as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. Symmetric encryption and hash functions have a different risk profile and are not all broken in the same way.

What are ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA?

They are the algorithms specified in NIST's first three final PQC standards: ML-KEM establishes shared secret keys, while ML-DSA and SLH-DSA are digital-signature algorithms.

Will I need a new phone or Wi-Fi router?

Not simply because PQC standards have been published. Compatibility will depend on future software, hardware and service support. Keep devices updated and replace unsupported equipment for ordinary security reasons.

What is the difference between post-quantum cryptography and quantum key distribution?

PQC uses mathematical algorithms on ordinary computers. Quantum key distribution uses quantum physical systems to distribute keys. They are different technologies and are not interchangeable.

When will the internet become quantum-safe?

There is no single worldwide switch date. Standards, products and organisations will migrate at different speeds. NIST's October 2026 report gives transition guidance and US federal milestones, but it does not establish a global completion date for every service.
