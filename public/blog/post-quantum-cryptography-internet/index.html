Post-Quantum Cryptography: How the Internet Is Preparing for Quantum Computers

A future quantum computer could undermine some of the public-key cryptography that helps secure websites, apps and digital identity. The response is already taking shape—but it is a long, careful migration, not an overnight switch.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

When you open a banking website, sign in to an email account or send a private message, cryptography works quietly in the background. It helps devices establish shared secrets, checks that information has not been altered and verifies that a service is connected to the right identity.

Some of the public-key mathematics used for these tasks could be broken by a sufficiently powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computer. That machine does not exist today in a form known to threaten internet cryptography at scale. But replacing cryptography across browsers, phones, servers, certificates, payment systems and embedded devices takes years. Waiting until a threat is demonstrated would leave too little time.

This is why standards bodies and technology providers are preparing for post-quantum cryptography (PQC): cryptographic algorithms designed to run on ordinary computers while resisting attacks from both classical and quantum computers. This article reflects primary-source information available on 22 November 2026.

1. What exactly is the quantum threat?

Today's public-key cryptography uses mathematical problems that are believed to be difficult for conventional computers. RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring large integers. Elliptic-curve systems rely on discrete-logarithm problems. These techniques are used in key establishment and digital signatures across many internet protocols.

A sufficiently capable quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve the mathematical problems behind widely used RSA and elliptic-curve systems much more efficiently than a classical computer. That would affect important parts of secure connection setup and identity verification.

It does not mean a quantum computer can automatically read every encrypted file, guess every password or make all existing encryption useless. Symmetric encryption and cryptographic hash functions face a different quantum threat profile. The engineering response depends on the algorithm and its role; it is not accurate to say that all encryption is equally broken.

There is also no dependable public date for when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will be available. Estimates vary, and technical obstacles remain. The uncertainty is a reason to plan carefully—not a reason to claim that current banking or HTTPS traffic is already being decrypted by quantum machines.

2. Why security teams worry about “harvest now, decrypt later”

Imagine an organisation records an encrypted communication today. The attacker cannot read it with current equipment, but stores the captured data. If a powerful quantum computer becomes available years later, the attacker may try to decrypt the older recording if it used a vulnerable key-establishment method.

This is often called harvest now, decrypt later. It matters most when information remains sensitive for a long time: confidential business plans, government records, intellectual property, long-lived identity data or private communications that should remain private for many years.

The risk is different from stealing a password today. It is about the future confidentiality of data captured now. It also explains why migration cannot wait until a quantum computer is publicly demonstrated: the data may already have been collected.

Digital signatures have a different concern. An attacker cannot normally store a signature today and later use quantum computing to reveal the signed message's confidentiality. Instead, a future quantum-capable attacker could threaten the trust placed in vulnerable signature algorithms—for example, by forging signatures or undermining certificate and software-signing systems. Those systems also need a transition, even though the “harvest now” issue primarily concerns confidentiality.

3. Post-quantum cryptography does not mean a quantum internet

The name can be confusing. Post-quantum cryptography is not quantum communication, quantum key distribution or a network of quantum computers. PQC algorithms are mathematical methods implemented in conventional software and hardware. A laptop, phone or server can use them without containing a quantum processor.

Quantum networking research—covered separately in our future of the internet pillar article—looks at distributing quantum states between specialised devices. PQC solves a different problem: protecting cryptographic operations on the ordinary internet against future quantum attacks.

For most websites and apps, the likely transition will happen inside operating systems, browsers, server software, cryptographic libraries, network protocols and certificate infrastructure. Users may notice software updates or compatibility changes, but they will not need to buy a “quantum internet” router.

4. The three NIST standards to know

In August 2024, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published three final post-quantum cryptography standards. They are important reference points for the wider technology industry. They do different jobs, so they should not be described as three interchangeable encryption methods.

StandardAlgorithmWhat it does
FIPS 203ML-KEMKey encapsulation: lets two parties establish a shared secret over a public channel. That secret can then be used with symmetric cryptography.
FIPS 204ML-DSADigital signatures: helps verify who signed data and detect unauthorised changes.
FIPS 205SLH-DSADigital signatures based on hash functions, providing a different design approach from ML-DSA.

ML-KEM is based on the Module Learning with Errors problem. NIST specifies ML-KEM-512, ML-KEM-768 and ML-KEM-1024 parameter sets. A key-encapsulation mechanism does not directly encrypt a whole website session; it helps the parties establish a shared key, which is then used by symmetric algorithms for the actual data protection.

ML-DSA is a lattice-based digital-signature standard. SLH-DSA is a stateless hash-based signature standard. Signature algorithms are used in places such as software updates, certificates and other systems that need to establish authenticity and integrity. Which algorithm is suitable depends on the application, implementation constraints and applicable security requirements.

NIST has also published errata and planning notes for some standards. Implementers should check the current standard pages and updates rather than treating a 2024 PDF as permanently frozen.

5. What changes when a website uses HTTPS?

HTTPS uses Transport Layer Security (TLS) to protect a connection between a browser and a website. In a simplified connection setup, the browser and server authenticate the service and agree on shared key material. The session then uses symmetric cryptography to protect the data travelling in both directions.

Public-key cryptography plays important roles in that process, but it is not the same thing as the bulk encryption used for every page, image or download. A PQC migration therefore needs to update the vulnerable public-key parts while preserving the complete security properties of the protocol.

One approach being deployed and evaluated is hybrid key establishment. In a hybrid design, a conventional key-establishment method and a post-quantum method contribute to the session secret. Properly designed combinations can provide a safety margin during transition: if one component later proves weaker than expected, the other may still protect the result. Hybrid operation can also add computational cost and larger handshake messages, so protocol designers must test performance, compatibility and reliability.

The exact algorithms and combinations depend on the protocol version, implementation and current standards guidance. A website displaying a padlock does not tell a visitor which key-establishment method was negotiated. That detail is normally handled by the browser and server software.

6. Why the migration takes so long

Changing one algorithm in one server is relatively straightforward. Changing cryptography across the internet is not. Organisations often do not have a complete list of where cryptography is used. It may be hidden in a cloud service, a hardware security module, a mobile application, a router, a payment terminal, an old database, a certificate authority or a supplier's software.

Some equipment cannot be updated easily. A device may have a long service life, limited memory or processing capacity, or a vendor that no longer provides support. Larger keys and signatures can affect network packet sizes, handshake performance, storage, bandwidth and constrained devices. Systems also need to interoperate: a secure new algorithm is not useful if two endpoints cannot agree on it.

NIST's final October 2026 report, NISTIR 8547, sets out a transition approach for moving away from quantum-vulnerable algorithms toward post-quantum key-establishment and signature standards. It stresses that migration is complex and that different use cases need different priorities. The report also discusses hybrid approaches and the importance of understanding an organisation's cryptographic supply chain.

In the United States, federal migration milestones include 2030 for key establishment in high-value assets and high-impact systems, 2031 for digital signatures in those systems, and a broader 2035 target. These are US federal policy timelines; they are not a global deadline imposed on every website or a statement of Indian regulatory requirements.

7. What does this mean for India and everyday users?

For a person using UPI, net banking, email, social media or a shopping app in India, the transition will mostly be handled by service providers, operating-system vendors, browser developers, cloud platforms and the companies that maintain payment and identity infrastructure.

It would be premature to claim that every Indian bank, telecom operator or government service has completed a PQC migration. Publicly available global standards do not prove the implementation status of a particular Indian service. That needs to be checked through the organisation's own technical disclosures or regulator guidance.

For now, sensible user habits remain useful:

  • Keep your phone, computer, browser and applications updated.
  • Use supported devices and software; stop relying on products that no longer receive security updates.
  • Install banking and payment apps from their official sources and protect accounts with strong authentication.
  • Do not install a tool that promises to “make your phone quantum-proof” unless it comes from a trusted provider and has a clear, independently verifiable purpose.
  • Do not disable HTTPS warnings or certificate checks to make an old application work.

There is no general need for a household to replace its Wi-Fi router or change its broadband plan simply because PQC standards exist. The important changes occur in cryptographic software and services, not in the speed tier of a home internet connection.

8. What organisations should do before the deadline

For businesses, universities, service providers and public institutions, the first step is visibility. You cannot replace cryptography you do not know you are using. A practical programme usually begins with a cryptographic inventory: where algorithms, keys, certificates and cryptographic modules appear, who owns them, what data they protect and how long that data needs to remain confidential.

Next, prioritise systems by exposure and consequence. Long-lived sensitive information may make key-establishment migration urgent. Software signing, identity systems and certificate infrastructure need their own plans because signature migration involves different dependencies and coordination.

Organisations should ask suppliers about supported PQC standards, hybrid options, update paths, interoperability testing, performance on constrained devices, certificate and key-management plans, and how cryptographic agility will be maintained. A product label saying “quantum safe” is not a substitute for an implementation statement and a tested migration plan.

Finally, test in stages. Measure handshake size and latency, CPU and memory use, failure rates, compatibility with older clients and behaviour under real network conditions. Keep rollback and incident-response plans. Cryptographic migration is a systems-engineering project, not just a library upgrade.

9. What is likely to happen between now and 2035?

The following is a way to understand the direction of travel, not a prediction that every organisation will follow the same calendar.

  • 2026–2027: Organisations use the final NIST transition guidance to identify vulnerable cryptography, classify sensitive data and test PQC-capable products. Protocol and vendor support continues to evolve.
  • 2028–2030: Migration activity is likely to become more visible in high-value systems, cloud services, identity infrastructure and long-lived data protection. US federal policy sets earlier milestones for designated high-value and high-impact systems.
  • 2031–2035: More systems are expected to complete signature and key-establishment transitions. NIST's broader federal target is 2035, with the detailed treatment of individual algorithms defined in its standards and transition guidance.
  • Beyond 2035: Legacy equipment and long-lived systems may still need special handling. Cryptographic agility—the ability to replace algorithms without rebuilding an entire product—will remain important as standards and threat assessments evolve.

10. The practical takeaway

Quantum computing is a serious long-term consideration for public-key cryptography, but it is not a reason to panic about today's internet connection. NIST's post-quantum standards are published, migration guidance is available and the work is moving from research into implementation.

The transition will be largely invisible to most people when it is done well. Browsers, servers, apps and network services will gradually learn to use new cryptographic methods. The real challenge is coordinating the change across a vast ecosystem without breaking compatibility or weakening security along the way.

For readers, the best immediate action is to keep software current and rely on reputable providers. For organisations, the work starts with knowing where cryptography lives and planning its replacement before the data or systems become exposed.

11. Start with a threat model, not an algorithm shopping list

A useful PQC programme begins by asking what must remain confidential, authentic or available, for how long, and what systems make that protection possible. Selecting an algorithm before answering those questions can create expensive work in the wrong order. A public website that serves general information has a different exposure profile from a research institution holding unpublished research, a hospital retaining patient records, a bank operating long-lived identity infrastructure or a manufacturer supporting industrial equipment for twenty years.

Separate three security objectives. Confidentiality asks whether an attacker could read captured information later. Authentication and integrity ask whether a system can still prove who created a message, software package or certificate and whether it was changed. Availability asks whether a migration or compatibility failure could interrupt a service. PQC planning must cover all three, but the urgency and engineering path may differ.

For confidentiality, estimate the required secrecy lifetime of the information and the time an adversary might retain it. A document that becomes public next month is not equivalent to a medical record that must remain private for decades. For signatures, map the period during which a signature must remain trustworthy and the systems that validate it. For availability, identify devices that cannot be patched remotely, services with strict uptime requirements and clients that may not support larger handshakes or new algorithms.

Do not assume every asset is equally exposed. Classify information and systems by sensitivity, lifetime, external exposure, business impact, updateability and supplier dependency. That creates a defensible prioritisation method rather than a race to label every product “quantum ready”.

12. A practical cryptographic inventory: what to look for

Cryptographic discovery is often harder than the eventual software update. A company may know that its customer portal uses TLS but not know which library performs the handshake, which certificate authority issues its certificates, whether a load balancer terminates TLS, how service-to-service connections are protected or whether a third-party monitoring agent contains its own cryptographic stack.

Build an inventory that records at least the following fields:

Inventory fieldQuestion to answerWhy it matters
System and ownerWhich application, device or service uses cryptography, and who is accountable?Creates a route for decisions, testing and remediation.
PurposeIs it key establishment, encryption, signing, authentication, integrity or key storage?Different functions need different PQC replacements.
Algorithm and parametersWhich protocol, algorithm, key size and implementation version are active?Identifies quantum-vulnerable dependencies and obsolete configurations.
Data lifetimeHow long must protected information remain confidential or signatures remain trusted?Helps prioritise harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure and long-lived trust.
DependenciesWhich vendors, libraries, hardware security modules, certificates and partner systems are involved?Migration may depend on a supplier or an entire ecosystem.
Update pathCan the component be patched, reconfigured or replaced, and how long will that take?Highlights legacy equipment that needs early planning.
Test evidenceHas the proposed change been tested for interoperability, performance and recovery?Prevents an algorithm upgrade from becoming an outage.

Use more than a source-code search. Review TLS termination points, VPN gateways, SSH services, code-signing pipelines, certificate authorities, identity providers, database encryption, backups, device firmware, hardware security modules and managed services. Ask suppliers for a cryptographic bill of materials or equivalent documentation where available. A software bill of materials can help identify components, but it does not automatically reveal every algorithm, key use or configuration; treat it as one input, not the complete inventory.

Record uncertainty honestly. If a supplier cannot yet identify the cryptographic library inside a device, mark that dependency as unknown, assign an owner and request a dated response. An inventory with explicit unknowns is more useful than a spreadsheet that silently assumes everything is safe.

13. Which systems should be migrated first?

Prioritisation should combine the sensitivity of protected data with the time needed to migrate the system. A practical scoring exercise can rate each asset from low to high across five dimensions: confidentiality lifetime, external exposure, consequence of compromise, difficulty of replacement and supplier readiness. The score is a planning aid, not a mathematical proof of risk.

System or use caseTypical PQC concernPlanning priority to consider
Long-lived confidential communicationsRecorded traffic may be retained for future decryption attempts.High when secrecy must last many years.
Public-facing TLS and VPN gatewaysKey establishment, protocol compatibility and large handshake messages.High for exposed services carrying sensitive data.
Software and firmware signingLong-term trust in update authenticity and code provenance.High where devices remain deployed for years.
Certificate authorities and identity systemsTrust chains and signature verification have broad downstream impact.High because changes require ecosystem coordination.
Short-lived low-sensitivity dataLower confidentiality lifetime may reduce immediate urgency.Assess proportionately; do not ignore shared infrastructure.
Unsupported embedded devicesLimited memory, processing capacity and no reliable update mechanism.Start early because replacement cycles can be long.

Do not read this table as a universal ranking. A VPN carrying sensitive long-term research may be urgent; a public website with no confidential user traffic may have a different risk. A code-signing system used to update millions of deployed devices may deserve early attention even if it is not directly exposed to the public internet. The organisation's data, architecture and operational constraints determine the order.

Shared infrastructure deserves special treatment. A single certificate service, identity provider, software-signing key or cryptographic library can affect many applications. Migrating such a dependency can unlock progress across a portfolio, but it also creates a concentrated change risk that needs careful staging.

14. How hybrid TLS key establishment works in practice

Hybrid key establishment is one of the most visible examples of the transition entering internet protocols. Traditional ephemeral Diffie–Hellman key exchange has a long deployment history and is efficient, but its underlying public-key mathematics is vulnerable to a sufficiently capable quantum computer. ML-KEM is designed to provide a post-quantum key-establishment component. A hybrid group combines both contributions into one negotiated method.

For TLS 1.3, IETF RFC 10024 (August 2026) specifies three post-quantum/traditional hybrid key agreement mechanisms: X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768 and SecP384r1MLKEM1024. They pair ML-KEM with an elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman exchange. RFC 9954 describes the general hybrid construction framework. These are protocol specifications; their existence does not mean every browser, server, CDN, enterprise proxy or mobile application supports every group.

At a high level, the client and server negotiate a supported group, exchange the required public values, derive a combined secret according to the protocol construction and use the TLS key schedule to produce traffic keys. The browser still validates the server certificate and the TLS handshake. Key establishment and digital signatures are separate cryptographic jobs: deploying a hybrid key exchange does not by itself replace RSA or elliptic-curve signatures used for authentication.

Hybrid messages can be larger than traditional handshakes. This may interact with packet fragmentation, middleboxes, older network equipment, constrained clients and latency-sensitive connections. Teams should measure handshake success rates and tail latency across real access networks, not only a lab with modern equipment. If a deployment has older clients, test fallback behaviour carefully; an insecure fallback must not become the easiest path through a compatibility problem.

For a service owner, a sensible sequence is to inventory TLS termination points, confirm support from the operating system and cryptographic library vendors, test the exact protocol group in staging, monitor negotiation and handshake failures, then roll out to a limited share of traffic before expanding. Keep the ability to disable a faulty configuration without reverting unrelated security controls.

15. Digital signatures need their own migration plan

Key establishment protects the creation of shared secrets. Digital signatures answer a different question: did the holder of a signing key approve this data, and has the data remained unchanged? Signatures are used in public-key certificates, software updates, firmware, signed documents, package repositories, code repositories, secure boot and many identity systems.

ML-DSA and SLH-DSA are not drop-in replacements that can be substituted without considering every verifier. A signature can be larger, verification can have different performance characteristics, and certificate formats, protocols, hardware tokens, secure boot implementations and trust stores may have constraints. A device that accepts only a fixed set of signature algorithms may reject an otherwise valid update if its firmware cannot be changed first.

Map the full signing chain. Identify who creates the signature, where the private key is protected, how the public key is distributed, which clients verify it, how revocation or key rollover works and what happens if a signing key is compromised. Include offline systems and devices that may remain disconnected for long periods. A migration that updates the signing service but leaves deployed verifiers unable to recognise the new signature can strand devices.

Plan a transition period in which old and new formats may need to coexist under explicit policy. Avoid uncontrolled dual-signing that creates ambiguous trust. Define which algorithms are accepted, for which asset classes and until what date; record the reason for exceptions; and make sure the verifier's policy cannot be weakened by an attacker. For high-value firmware, test recovery and authenticated rollback protection before changing production signing.

16. Performance, bandwidth and user experience: what should be measured?

PQC can change the size and computational cost of cryptographic operations. The impact depends on the algorithm, parameter set, protocol, implementation, hardware and network path. It is misleading to claim that post-quantum encryption will automatically make the internet slow—or that it has no performance cost at all.

For TLS and VPNs, measure handshake completion time, CPU use, memory use, bytes exchanged during setup, packet fragmentation, connection failure rate and the 95th or 99th percentile latency. For signatures, measure signing and verification time, signature size, certificate-chain size and storage requirements. For embedded systems, include battery consumption, flash memory, boot time and the ability to complete an update over a weak connection.

MeasurementTest approachWhat a regression could indicate
Handshake success rateCompare supported client and server combinations.Unsupported groups, middlebox interference or implementation defects.
Connection setup latencyMeasure median and tail latency across local, mobile and international paths.Larger messages, retransmissions or extra processing.
Network bytes during setupCapture handshake traffic in a controlled test environment.Certificate or key-exchange size changes affecting constrained links.
CPU and memoryProfile server, client and gateway under representative concurrency.Capacity planning or denial-of-service exposure.
Signature verificationBenchmark the actual application and hardware.Slow boot, package installation or request validation.
Recovery and compatibilityTest old clients, interrupted updates and rollback procedures.Operational risk that a simple benchmark would miss.

Use a baseline captured before the change. Compare like with like: same device class, software version, network path, load and test duration. A speed-test result is not a PQC benchmark. It measures an end-to-end internet path and cannot identify whether a cryptographic handshake caused a delay without a controlled protocol-level test.

17. Common migration mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Assuming the standards list is the deployment plan. Standards define algorithms and requirements; organisations still need inventory, architecture, vendor coordination, testing and operational policy.
  • Replacing only TLS key exchange. Signatures, code signing, certificates, SSH, VPNs, backups, device firmware and internal protocols may have separate dependencies.
  • Ignoring the verifier. A new signing algorithm is useful only when every required verifier can process it safely.
  • Assuming hybrid means risk-free. Hybrid constructions can reduce dependence on one algorithm family, but implementation errors, weak randomness, bad key management and vulnerable endpoints remain risks.
  • Turning off certificate validation to solve compatibility. This removes an essential security check and is not an acceptable migration workaround.
  • Buying a product based on a marketing badge. Ask which standard, parameter set, protocol, implementation and validated configuration are supported.
  • Leaving unsupported equipment until last. Devices with long replacement cycles may need the earliest planning even if their migration happens later.
  • Failing to monitor after rollout. Track negotiation rates, handshake failures, latency, support incidents and policy exceptions after deployment.

A good programme has a named owner, an approved target architecture, supplier milestones, test evidence, exception handling, rollback criteria and a review date. Treat cryptographic agility as a design requirement: systems should be able to change algorithms through supported configuration or software updates rather than requiring an emergency rebuild.

18. An actionable 90-day PQC readiness plan

A 90-day plan will not complete a large organisation's migration. It can establish governance, expose unknowns and create a prioritised roadmap. Smaller organisations can scale the activities down; critical infrastructure providers should use a more formal risk and assurance process.

PeriodActionsDeliverable
Days 1–15Appoint an accountable owner; define confidentiality and authenticity priorities; identify regulated or high-impact systems.Scope, decision owners and initial risk statement.
Days 16–30Inventory external TLS, VPN, identity, signing, certificates, SSH, backup and embedded-device dependencies.Cryptographic asset register with unknowns and owners.

For an individual or small business, the equivalent first step is simpler: keep supported devices updated, ask critical SaaS and hosting providers about their PQC roadmap, retain a record of unsupported systems and avoid untrusted “quantum security” downloads. Most households do not need to configure cryptographic algorithms manually.

19. What Indian organisations should ask suppliers

India's organisations often depend on global cloud platforms, software vendors, telecom networks, payment processors and locally deployed equipment. A useful supplier conversation should be specific rather than asking only whether a product is “quantum safe”. Ask which NIST standards and IETF protocol specifications are supported; whether the implementation is production-ready or experimental; which versions and parameter sets are available; how keys and certificates are managed; and what happens to customers using older devices.

For banks, payment service providers, hospitals, universities, IT exporters and government contractors, include the cryptographic transition in vendor-risk reviews and long-term data-protection planning. Ask whether the provider has inventoried subcontractors and managed services, whether the roadmap covers both key establishment and signatures, and how customers will receive evidence of changes. Clarify data residency and contractual obligations separately: PQC does not replace privacy, cybersecurity or sector-specific compliance requirements.

Do not attribute US federal milestones to Indian regulators. NISTIR 8547 is a NIST transition report and the dates it describes for federal systems are US policy context. Indian organisations should follow applicable Indian laws, sectoral regulator directions, procurement conditions and their own risk obligations. Where a regulator or government agency publishes a PQC-specific direction, use that primary document and its current version.

20. A simple checklist for readers

  • Keep operating systems, browsers, routers and applications within their supported update period.
  • Use reputable banking, payment and cloud services; install apps only from official sources.
  • Do not disable certificate warnings or install unknown certificates to bypass an application error.
  • Do not purchase a new phone, laptop or router solely because it is advertised as “quantum-proof”.
  • If you run a website, ask your hosting, CDN and TLS provider about supported hybrid key establishment and their migration roadmap.
  • If you manage business data, identify how long sensitive information must remain confidential and where cryptography is used.
  • If you procure software or connected devices, ask how cryptographic updates will be delivered over the product's full support life.

The main lesson is preparation. Post-quantum cryptography is a significant internet infrastructure transition, but it is not an emergency setting that consumers must switch on. Standards are available, protocols are being specified and deployments are progressing. The organisations that map their dependencies, prioritise long-lived data and test changes early will be in a much better position than those that wait for a dramatic headline.

Frequently asked questions

Can a quantum computer hack my bank account today?

There is no public evidence that a cryptographically relevant quantum computer can currently break ordinary banking traffic at scale. Keep your devices and banking apps updated and use normal account-security protections.

Does post-quantum cryptography run on a quantum computer?

No. PQC algorithms run on conventional computers. They are designed to resist attacks from classical computers and future quantum computers.

Will quantum computers break all encryption?

No. Shor's algorithm threatens important public-key systems such as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. Symmetric encryption and hash functions have a different risk profile and are not all broken in the same way.

What are ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA?

They are algorithms in NIST's first three final PQC standards. ML-KEM establishes shared secrets; ML-DSA and SLH-DSA are digital-signature schemes.

What is the difference between encryption and a digital signature?

Encryption protects confidentiality by limiting who can read data. A digital signature helps establish authenticity and integrity by allowing a verifier to check who signed data and whether it changed.

What does “harvest now, decrypt later” mean?

An attacker stores encrypted information today and hopes to decrypt it in the future with a sufficiently capable quantum computer. It is most relevant to information that must remain confidential for many years.

What is hybrid post-quantum key exchange?

It combines a traditional key-establishment method with a post-quantum method, deriving a session secret from both contributions. The goal is to provide a transition path while maintaining security if one component remains sound.

Does a hybrid TLS handshake mean a website's certificate is post-quantum?

No. Hybrid key establishment and digital signatures are separate functions. A TLS connection may use a post-quantum hybrid key exchange while certificate authentication still uses a traditional signature algorithm.

Will I need a new phone, laptop or Wi-Fi router?

Not simply because PQC standards have been published. Support will arrive through software, hardware and service updates. Replace unsupported devices for ordinary security reasons and follow vendor guidance.

Will post-quantum cryptography make internet speed slower?

Some algorithms and handshakes use larger messages or more processing, but the real impact depends on implementation and network conditions. It should be measured with controlled protocol tests rather than assumed.

What is the difference between PQC and quantum key distribution?

PQC uses mathematical algorithms on ordinary computers. Quantum key distribution uses quantum physical systems to distribute keys. They are different technologies with different deployment requirements.

When will the whole internet become quantum-safe?

There is no single global switch date. Standards, vendors and organisations will migrate at different speeds. NISTIR 8547 describes US federal transition guidance, not a worldwide completion deadline.

What should a small business do first?

List its important services and suppliers, identify where TLS, VPNs, certificates and code signing are used, ask providers for their PQC roadmap and keep all software supported and updated.

Does NIST's 2035 target apply to Indian businesses?

No. NIST's dates are part of US federal transition policy and guidance. Indian organisations should follow applicable Indian laws, sectoral regulator directions, contracts and their own risk assessments.
