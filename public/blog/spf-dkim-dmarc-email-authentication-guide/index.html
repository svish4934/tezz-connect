SPF, DKIM and DMARC Explained: How Email Authentication Protects Your Domain

An email can display your company name and address even when it was not sent by your organisation. SPF, DKIM and DMARC give receiving mail systems ways to evaluate that claim. This practical guide explains how the three standards work together, where their DNS records belong, what alignment means, how to roll out a policy safely and why authentication is not the same as guaranteed inbox delivery.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Why email needs authentication

Email was designed to move messages between independent systems. Its openness helped it become a universal communication tool, but the visible sender identity is not, by itself, proof of who operated the sending server. A criminal can attempt to send a message that appears to come from a bank, an employer, a supplier or a family member. This technique is commonly called email spoofing. It can support phishing, invoice fraud, credential theft, malware delivery and brand impersonation.

Imagine receiving a message that appears to come from accounts@example.com asking you to pay an urgent invoice to a new bank account. The display name and visible From address may look familiar. Without authentication checks, a recipient has limited technical evidence about whether the sending system was authorised by the domain owner. Email authentication adds signals that receiving systems can evaluate before deciding how to handle a message.

Three standards are central to domain-based email authentication: Sender Policy Framework (SPF), DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM), and Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance (DMARC). They solve related but different problems. SPF checks the sending infrastructure against a published policy. DKIM checks a cryptographic signature attached to the message. DMARC connects those results to the visible From domain, defines alignment rules and lets a domain owner publish a handling policy.

These mechanisms are not a complete anti-phishing solution. They do not prove that a message is honest, that a sender's account has not been compromised, or that a link is safe. They help establish whether a message is authorised to use a domain identity and whether the message's signed content has changed in transit. Good security combines authentication with account protection, filtering, user awareness and monitoring.

The three standards at a glance

SPF, DKIM and DMARC are often discussed as a single package, but each evaluates a different part of an email. Understanding that distinction makes configuration and troubleshooting much easier.

  • SPF: Does the server connecting to the recipient have permission under the SPF policy for the envelope-sender domain?
  • DKIM: Does the message contain a valid signature from a domain that published the corresponding public key, and has the signed content remained intact?
  • DMARC: Does at least one of SPF or DKIM both pass and align with the domain in the visible From address, and what policy should receivers apply when neither aligned method passes?

DMARC does not replace SPF or DKIM. It uses their results and compares the authenticated domains with the domain a recipient sees. This is important because an attacker could otherwise authenticate a domain they control while placing a different organisation's address in the visible From field. Alignment makes the relationship between the authentication result and the claimed identity explicit.

SPF: authorising the systems that send your email

Sender Policy Framework is published in DNS as a TXT record. It identifies which sending hosts or services are authorised to send mail using a particular domain in the SMTP envelope identity. The recipient's mail server checks the connecting IP address against the SPF policy for the relevant domain. The standard is defined in IETF RFC 7208.

A simplified SPF record might look like this:

example.com. TXT "v=spf1 ip4:192.0.2.10 include:mail.example-provider.net -all"

This example is illustrative, not a record to copy into a real domain. The version marker identifies SPF. The ip4 mechanism authorises a specific IPv4 address. The include mechanism asks the receiver to evaluate another domain's SPF policy. The final all mechanism matches any sender not already matched; the minus qualifier indicates a hard fail. Real policies must use the exact mechanisms and values supplied by every legitimate email provider.

SPF is evaluated against the SMTP envelope sender, which is not necessarily the same as the address displayed to the person reading the message. The envelope sender is used during mail transport and is commonly visible in message headers as Return-Path after delivery. DMARC later checks whether this authenticated domain aligns with the visible From domain.

SPF has operational limits. A policy can trigger a DNS lookup limit, and nested includes can make a record unexpectedly complex. A domain should publish one SPF policy record at a given owner name, not several independent SPF TXT records. If multiple services send mail for the same domain, their authorised mechanisms must be combined into the single policy. Exceeding the SPF lookup limit can result in a PermError, so adding every provider's include statement without reviewing the total is unsafe.

Do not add an SPF record simply because a vendor's setup wizard suggests one. First confirm that the service actually sends messages using your domain, identify the envelope domain it uses, and follow its current instructions. Some services use a dedicated bounce subdomain. Others use a custom return-path domain. Your DNS record must match the domain that is actually evaluated.

DKIM: signing messages with cryptographic keys

DomainKeys Identified Mail attaches a digital signature to an outgoing message. The sending system uses a private key to create the signature, and the receiving system retrieves the corresponding public key from DNS. If verification succeeds, the receiver has evidence that the signed portions of the message have not changed since signing and that the signer controlled the key associated with the signing domain.

A DKIM signature is added in a message header named DKIM-Signature. It contains fields such as the signing domain (d=), selector (s=), algorithm, signed header list and body hash. The selector tells the receiver where to find the public key. For example, a signature with d=example.com and s=mail2026 points to a DNS name similar to:

mail2026._domainkey.example.com

The TXT record at that name contains the public key and related parameters. The private key stays with the sending system and should never be published in DNS, pasted into a support ticket or shared with an unauthorised person. The public key is intended to be discoverable; the private key is the secret that makes the signature trustworthy.

DKIM can be more resilient than SPF when messages are forwarded. Forwarding changes the server that connects to the recipient, so SPF may fail. A DKIM signature can remain valid if the forwarder does not alter the signed parts of the message. However, mailing lists and gateways sometimes modify subjects, footers, MIME content or other signed elements, which can break a signature. A DKIM pass also does not automatically mean DMARC passes: the signing domain must align with the visible From domain.

Selectors support key rotation and multiple sending systems. An organisation might use different selectors for a marketing platform, a help-desk service and its primary mail provider. This separation can make it easier to replace one provider's key without changing every other sender. Keep an inventory of selectors, ownership and retirement dates. When a provider is removed, follow its guidance for safely retiring old DNS keys.

The core DKIM specification is IETF RFC 6376. Providers may offer newer key algorithms or signing options, so use their current supported configuration rather than assuming every service supports the same choices.

DMARC: connecting authentication to the visible From address

DMARC builds on SPF and DKIM. It checks whether at least one authentication method passes and whether the authenticated domain aligns with the domain in the visible From header. It also lets a domain owner publish a policy describing how receivers should handle messages that fail DMARC, and where aggregate reports should be sent.

DMARC is published as a TXT record at the special DNS name _dmarc. A basic monitoring example is:

_dmarc.example.com. TXT "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com"

The v tag identifies the protocol version. The p tag sets the requested policy for the domain. The rua tag provides an address for aggregate reports. This example is only a starting illustration: the reporting mailbox must exist, be monitored or processed securely, and the domain owner must understand the privacy and volume implications of receiving reports.

There are three familiar policy choices. p=none asks receivers to take no DMARC-specific enforcement action and is useful for observation. p=quarantine asks receivers to treat failing messages as suspicious, often by placing them in spam or junk. p=reject asks receivers to reject messages that fail DMARC. Receiver implementations and local policies still matter; a published policy is not a guarantee that every provider will handle every message identically.

DMARC also supports a subdomain policy through sp, percentage-based rollout through pct in specifications and implementations that support it, and alignment modes through adkim and aspf. Do not add tags from copied examples without understanding their effect. The current DMARC standard was updated in 2026 through the RFC 9989 series, including aggregate and failure reporting specifications. Many systems and guides still refer to the earlier RFC 7489, so administrators should check current implementation support as they deploy.

What DMARC alignment actually means

Alignment is one of the most important concepts in email authentication. A message can pass SPF for a domain controlled by a sending provider and pass DKIM for that provider's own domain, yet still claim to be from yourcompany.com in the visible From header. DMARC asks whether the successful SPF or DKIM identity is related to the visible From domain according to the alignment mode.

In relaxed alignment, domains that share the same organisational domain can align. For example, a visible From address at news.example.com may align with an authenticated domain at example.com, depending on the public suffix calculation and exact domain relationship. In strict alignment, the domains must match exactly. Strict alignment can provide tighter identity matching but may require more careful configuration across subdomains and third-party senders.

DMARC passes when at least one of the two paths succeeds with alignment: SPF must pass and its authenticated domain must align, or DKIM must pass and its signing domain must align. Both do not have to pass for DMARC to pass, although configuring both correctly improves resilience and visibility. For bulk senders, mailbox providers may separately require both SPF and DKIM to be configured even though DMARC's pass logic accepts one aligned method.

This is why a simple green SPF result in a DNS checker does not prove that your email setup is complete. You need to inspect a real delivered message and check the envelope domain, DKIM signing domain, visible From domain and DMARC alignment result.

How the receiver evaluates an incoming message

Consider a message sent by a business application using the visible address billing@example.com. The recipient's mail system receives the SMTP connection and evaluates the sending IP against the SPF policy for the envelope sender. It then checks whether the message has a DKIM signature and retrieves the public key using the selector and signing domain. After those checks, it evaluates DMARC by comparing the successful authentication domains with example.com.

If SPF passes and aligns, DMARC can pass even if DKIM fails. If DKIM passes and aligns, DMARC can pass even if SPF fails. If neither path both passes and aligns, DMARC fails and the receiver considers the domain's published policy. Other filtering systems may still apply reputation, malware, spam and user-specific decisions.

After evaluation, the receiver may add an Authentication-Results header. This header can report values such as spf=pass, dkim=pass and dmarc=pass, along with the domains that were evaluated. When troubleshooting, inspect the result added by the receiving provider rather than trusting a header inserted by an untrusted sender earlier in the message path.

How to deploy authentication without breaking legitimate email

The safest rollout begins with discovery, not with a strict policy. List every system that sends messages using your domain: employee mail, customer support, billing, password resets, website forms, marketing campaigns, CRM tools, payroll, monitoring alerts, invoicing and outsourced agencies. Include less obvious systems such as printers, scanners, website hosting platforms and legacy applications. A sender that is forgotten during deployment may fail when the policy becomes strict.

Step 1: inventory sending services. Record the provider, purpose, From domain, envelope-sender domain, DKIM signing domain, owner and whether the service is still active. Ask each vendor for its current SPF, DKIM and DMARC alignment instructions. Avoid relying on old setup notes because vendors change infrastructure and selectors.

Step 2: configure SPF carefully. Combine the authorised mechanisms into one policy for the evaluated domain. Check nested includes and DNS lookup counts. Remove obsolete senders only after confirming they are no longer used. Do not publish several SPF records at the same name.

Step 3: enable DKIM for each sender. Publish each provider's selector record and confirm the service is signing messages. Prefer separate selectors for independent platforms when supported. Protect private keys, plan rotation and document who controls each sending identity.

Step 4: publish DMARC in monitoring mode. A p=none policy allows the domain owner to collect aggregate reports without asking receivers to quarantine or reject failures. Confirm that the reporting address works and that reports can be parsed. Reports can contain detailed sending infrastructure information, so restrict access and use a reputable reporting processor if needed.

Step 5: review reports and test real mail. Identify legitimate senders that fail SPF, DKIM or alignment. Fix their configuration and check whether unknown sources represent spoofing, forgotten systems or misconfigured services. Test different message types, including password resets, invoices, newsletters, replies, forwarded messages and messages sent from subdomains.

Step 6: move policy gradually. Once legitimate traffic consistently authenticates, consider a staged move to quarantine and later reject. Review reports after each change. A strict policy is valuable only when the organisation understands its sending ecosystem. If a business cannot identify a failing sender, it should investigate before enforcing a policy that could block customer or operational messages.

Reading DMARC aggregate reports

Aggregate reports, often called RUA reports, summarise email authentication observed by receiving providers over a reporting period. They commonly include the reporting organisation, date range, source IP addresses, message counts, policy information, SPF results, DKIM results and alignment outcomes. They are usually delivered as XML attachments or compressed XML files.

Reports help answer practical questions: Which services send mail for the domain? Are expected providers passing? Are unknown IP addresses attempting to use the domain? Did a vendor change its sending infrastructure? Are messages passing SPF but failing alignment because the envelope domain is different? Is DKIM passing under a vendor domain that does not align with the visible From address?

Do not assume every unfamiliar source IP is an attacker. It may be a forwarding service, a third-party sender, a legitimate relay or a service whose provider uses shared infrastructure. Investigate the source, compare it with your inventory and contact the vendor when the evidence is unclear. Aggregate reports are evidence for analysis, not a complete record of every individual email.

Failure or forensic reporting has additional privacy and operational considerations and is not consistently supported by all receivers. Avoid collecting message samples or personal data without a clear legal and security basis. For most organisations, aggregate reporting is the practical starting point.

Common SPF, DKIM and DMARC mistakes

  • Publishing multiple SPF records: Merge authorised senders into one SPF policy at the relevant owner name.
  • Exceeding SPF DNS lookup limits: Review nested includes and remove obsolete mechanisms; do not blindly flatten records without understanding maintenance and provider changes.
  • Using the wrong SPF domain: The policy must exist for the envelope identity evaluated by the receiver, which may be a bounce subdomain rather than the visible From domain.
  • Publishing a DKIM key under the wrong selector: Match the selector and signing domain in the message's DKIM-Signature header.
  • Publishing a private key: Only the public key belongs in DNS. Keep the private signing key protected by the mail provider or controlled key-management system.
  • Forgetting third-party platforms: Marketing, ticketing, billing and website services may send as your domain and need their own authentication configuration.
  • Enforcing reject too early: Start by observing real traffic, fix legitimate failures and move to enforcement only when the sender inventory is complete.
  • Confusing authentication with reputation: SPF, DKIM and DMARC do not guarantee that a message is wanted or will reach the inbox.
  • Ignoring subdomains: Decide whether subdomains send mail and whether they need explicit DMARC policies or inherited behaviour.
  • Failing to monitor changes: New vendors, marketing tools and domain migrations can introduce authentication failures months after the initial setup.

Email forwarding, mailing lists and indirect mail flows

Forwarding is a common reason that SPF and DKIM results differ from expectations. When a recipient forwards a message, the final receiving server sees the forwarder's IP address. That address is usually not authorised by the original sender's SPF policy, so SPF can fail. Some forwarding services use Sender Rewriting Scheme (SRS) to rewrite the envelope sender and improve SPF behaviour, but this does not automatically solve every DMARC alignment scenario.

DKIM may survive forwarding because the signature is attached to the message, not to the network path. If the forwarder leaves the signed headers and body intact, the recipient can still verify the signature. If a mailing list adds a subject prefix, modifies the body or changes signed headers, verification may fail. A sender should test realistic forwarding and list workflows rather than assuming that all intermediaries behave identically.

ARC, or Authenticated Received Chain, is a related mechanism that allows intermediaries to preserve authentication assessments across a chain of forwarding or modification. It can provide receivers with additional context, but it does not replace SPF, DKIM or DMARC and does not force a receiver to trust a particular intermediary. ARC trust decisions remain receiver-specific.

What current mailbox-provider requirements mean

Mailbox providers have increased their focus on authentication, especially for high-volume senders. Google's published sender guidelines distinguish requirements for all senders from additional requirements for senders who send more than 5,000 messages per day to personal Gmail accounts. Its guidance calls for SPF or DKIM for all senders and SPF, DKIM and DMARC for bulk senders, along with other requirements such as valid DNS, TLS for mail transport, low spam rates and one-click unsubscribe for applicable marketing messages.

These requirements are provider policies and can change. They should not be treated as a universal law that every receiving mail server follows in exactly the same way. Check the current guidance for Gmail, Yahoo, Microsoft and any other important recipient ecosystem before planning a campaign or migration. Authentication is one part of deliverability; sender reputation, complaint rates, consent, list hygiene and message quality remain important.

For a small organisation, the practical lesson is straightforward: configure SPF and DKIM for every legitimate sending system, publish DMARC, monitor reports and keep the records accurate. Do not wait until a provider rejects messages or a spoofing incident damages trust.

Authentication does not guarantee inbox placement

A message can pass SPF, DKIM and DMARC and still land in spam. Authentication tells the receiver that the domain identity has passed certain checks; it does not prove that the recipient asked for the message, that the content is useful or that the sender has a good reputation. Providers also evaluate sending patterns, recipient engagement, complaint rates, bounce rates, suspicious links, attachments and many other signals.

Likewise, a message that fails one authentication method is not automatically malicious. Forwarding, mailing lists, configuration errors and third-party senders can cause failures. DMARC alignment provides a more meaningful domain-level decision, but the receiver's overall filtering system still makes the final delivery decision.

For this reason, measure authentication and deliverability separately. Monitor SPF/DKIM/DMARC results, but also watch bounce classifications, complaint rates, domain reputation tools, user reports and transactional mail delivery. Never try to solve a reputation or consent problem by repeatedly changing DNS records.

A practical checklist for domain owners

Before declaring an email domain protected, confirm the following:

  • Every service that sends using the domain has been identified and assigned an owner.
  • There is only one SPF policy record at each relevant SPF owner name.
  • The SPF policy authorises only legitimate sending infrastructure and remains within lookup limits.
  • Each sending service has DKIM enabled and its public selector record resolves correctly.
  • Private DKIM keys are protected and key rotation is documented.
  • A DMARC TXT record exists at _dmarc for the organisational domain and any required subdomain policy is understood.
  • Aggregate reports are received, parsed and reviewed by an accountable person or service.
  • Real messages show expected SPF, DKIM and DMARC outcomes in recipient-side Authentication-Results headers.
  • At least one aligned authentication path consistently passes for each legitimate sender.
  • Policy enforcement is increased only after failures are understood and corrected.
  • New vendors and sending systems must complete authentication checks before being allowed to use the organisation's From domain.

Frequently asked questions

1. What are SPF, DKIM and DMARC?

SPF identifies which sending systems are authorised for a domain, DKIM uses a cryptographic signature to verify a message, and DMARC checks alignment with the visible From domain and publishes a policy for failed authentication.

2. Do I need SPF, DKIM and DMARC for a small business?

Yes. Even a small organisation benefits from configuring all three for every domain it uses to send email. Requirements vary by receiving provider and sending volume, but the combined controls provide stronger protection and useful visibility.

3. Can I publish more than one SPF record?

A domain should publish one SPF policy record for a given owner name. Combine the authorised senders into that policy rather than creating multiple SPF TXT records, which can cause a PermError.

4. What is a DKIM selector?

A DKIM selector is a label chosen by the sending system that identifies the public key published in DNS. It appears in the DKIM-Signature header and helps receivers find the correct key.

5. What does DMARC alignment mean?

Alignment means the authenticated domain used by SPF or DKIM corresponds to the domain shown in the message's visible From address, according to the selected relaxed or strict alignment mode.

6. Should I start DMARC with p=reject?

Usually not before inventorying legitimate senders and reviewing reports. Many domain owners begin with p=none, fix authentication and alignment, then move gradually to quarantine and reject when evidence supports enforcement.

7. Does passing SPF, DKIM and DMARC guarantee inbox placement?

No. Authentication helps establish domain identity and policy, but inbox placement also depends on reputation, recipient engagement, message content, sending behaviour, provider rules and complaint rates.

8. What happens to SPF when an email is forwarded?

Forwarding often changes the connecting server, so SPF may fail because the forwarder is not authorised by the original sender's SPF record. DKIM can survive forwarding if the signed content is not modified, and DMARC can pass through aligned DKIM.

9. Where do I publish SPF, DKIM and DMARC records?

They are published in the authoritative DNS zone for the sending domain. SPF and DMARC use TXT records at defined names, while DKIM public keys are published under a selector and the _domainkey subdomain.

10. How can I check whether an email passed authentication?

Inspect the received message's full headers and find the receiving provider's Authentication-Results field. It normally reports SPF, DKIM and DMARC outcomes, but you should also review the domains involved and whether alignment succeeded.
