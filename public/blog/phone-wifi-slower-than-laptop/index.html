Why Is Wi-Fi Slower on Your Phone Than on Your Laptop?

Phones and laptops can perform differently on the same Wi-Fi network because their wireless hardware and software are not identical.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Why can a phone be slower than a laptop on the same Wi-Fi?

It is a common and confusing home-network problem: a laptop opens websites quickly and downloads files at a high rate, while a phone connected to what appears to be the same Wi-Fi network feels sluggish. The Wi-Fi symbol may show full bars on both devices, yet a speed test on the phone reports a much lower result. This does not automatically mean that the phone is defective, that the broadband plan is under-delivering, or that the router needs replacing. A phone and a laptop are two different wireless clients. They can have different radio designs, antenna arrangements, supported Wi-Fi features, software policies, physical placement and background workloads. Any one of those differences can affect the connection.

The phrase “same Wi-Fi” also needs careful interpretation. Two devices can display the same network name while connecting on different frequency bands, channels, access points or mesh nodes. They may negotiate different link rates with the router. They may also use different routes to a test server if an app, VPN, private DNS service or security filter is involved. A useful diagnosis therefore separates the local wireless link from the broadband connection and from the test application. This guide explains the likely causes and gives a repeatable method to identify the one that matters in your home.

Short answer: a laptop can be faster because it has a more capable Wi-Fi adapter or antenna arrangement, is connected to a better band, has a stronger radio link, or is less affected by phone-specific power, software and background activity. Compare both devices in the same place, on the same band and with the same test method before drawing a conclusion.

1. Phone and laptop Wi-Fi hardware are not identical

Wi-Fi performance depends on more than the Wi-Fi generation printed on a product box. The wireless client and the router negotiate a connection using the capabilities they have in common. Relevant characteristics include supported frequency bands, channel widths, modulation methods, spatial streams, antenna design, transmit power, receive sensitivity and the quality of the radio implementation. A laptop may have room for antennas positioned around its display or chassis. A phone has to fit its antennas into a compact enclosure alongside batteries, cameras, cellular radios and other components. That physical difference can influence reception and transmission, although it does not mean every laptop is faster than every phone.

One useful term is spatial stream. Modern Wi-Fi can use multiple transmit and receive paths to move data. A device that supports more spatial streams may have a higher theoretical link capacity under suitable conditions. The actual number varies by model and band. A Wi-Fi 6 label alone does not tell you that two devices have the same number of streams, channel width or radio performance. A high-end phone can outperform an older laptop, while an entry-level phone may have more modest wireless capabilities than a recent notebook.

Channel width is another difference. Wider channels can carry more data in clean radio conditions, but they also use more spectrum and may be less practical in a congested environment. A laptop adapter might support a wider channel than a phone, or the router may negotiate different channel widths with each client. Modulation also changes with signal quality. When a device receives a clean signal, it may use a more efficient modulation and coding rate. When signal quality falls, it may step down to a more robust but slower mode. The result is that two clients in the same room can achieve different throughput even when both are connected to the same access point.

Do not compare devices only by their advertised maximum Wi-Fi speed. Manufacturer figures often describe theoretical peak capabilities under specific combinations of bands, streams and channel widths. Real-world performance is lower and depends on the router, distance, interference, network traffic, client implementation and the internet service beyond the router. A useful comparison is the actual performance you can reproduce at home, not a headline number from a specification sheet.

2. The phone may be using 2.4 GHz while the laptop uses 5 GHz

Many home routers broadcast more than one Wi-Fi frequency band. The 2.4 GHz band generally travels farther and can pass through obstacles more effectively, but it has fewer non-overlapping channels and is often crowded by neighbouring networks and household devices. The 5 GHz band can offer higher throughput and more channel capacity, especially near the router, but its signal usually weakens more quickly through walls and floors. Some newer equipment also supports 6 GHz, which can provide additional spectrum for compatible Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 devices. Both the router and the client must support a band before it can be used.

A router may combine these bands under one network name using band steering. The router and device make decisions about which band to use, and the choice is not always the one that produces the highest speed at that exact moment. Your phone may remain on 2.4 GHz because it prioritises a stable connection or because it is at the edge of 5 GHz coverage. Your laptop, positioned closer to the access point or with a different antenna design, may connect to 5 GHz. Both devices can show the same Wi-Fi network name even though their radio links are different.

To test this possibility, open the Wi-Fi connection details on each device and look for the frequency or band. Android menus vary by manufacturer and operating-system version; some show frequency, link speed or network details after you tap the connected network. On Windows, open the Wi-Fi network properties and inspect the network band and link information. If your router offers separate names for 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, connect both devices to the same band for a controlled test. Do not assume the 5 GHz band will always win: at a greater distance or through several walls, 2.4 GHz may provide a more stable connection.

For 6 GHz, compatibility is especially important. A router advertising Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 does not make an older phone capable of using 6 GHz. The phone must include a compatible radio and the region's regulatory rules must permit the relevant operation. If a laptop supports a newer band and the phone does not, the laptop may have access to more spectrum. That is a capability difference, not proof that the phone's connection is broken.

3. Antenna placement, grip and phone cases can affect reception

A laptop is often used open on a desk, with its antennas in a relatively predictable position. A phone moves constantly. You may hold it in one hand, cover part of its edge with your palm, place it flat on a surface, keep it in a pocket or use it in a room separated from the router by your body. The human body absorbs and blocks some radio energy. The effect depends on the phone's antenna locations, orientation, frequency band and the direction of the access point. Turning around or changing the way you hold the phone can sometimes change reception slightly.

Cases and accessories are also worth considering, but they should not be blamed without a test. A normal case is not automatically a Wi-Fi problem. However, metal components, magnetic mounts, thick accessories or damaged cases can affect radio behaviour in some designs. If the phone became slower after a case or accessory was added, compare performance with the accessory removed. Repeat the test in the same location and orientation. A single speed-test result is not enough to establish cause.

Placement matters for the router too. A laptop on a desk may have a clearer path to a router positioned in the same room, while a phone is used behind a person, near a floor, beside a television or inside a room with multiple walls in between. Compare both devices at the same height and position, then repeat at the locations where you normally use them. This distinguishes a device-specific difference from a coverage problem in a particular part of the home.

4. Signal bars do not tell the whole story

The Wi-Fi icon is a rough indication of signal strength, not a complete measurement of connection quality. It does not normally tell you the available internet throughput, interference level, channel utilisation, retransmission rate, latency under load or the performance of the ISP. Two devices can display similar bars while experiencing different radio conditions. A strong signal can still be slow if the channel is crowded. A weaker signal can sometimes perform well if the channel is clean and the link remains stable.

Interference and congestion are separate but related issues. Interference can come from overlapping Wi-Fi networks, nearby electronics and other radio activity. Congestion can occur when many devices share airtime on the same access point or channel. Wi-Fi is a shared medium: clients take turns transmitting according to the rules of the network. A laptop running a large cloud backup, a television streaming high-resolution video and several phones downloading updates can all compete for airtime. The phone may be slower during busy periods even if its own signal strength has not changed.

Try a comparison when the household network is quiet and another when several devices are active. If the gap appears mainly during busy periods, the issue may be airtime contention, router capacity or competing traffic rather than a permanent limitation in the phone. If the phone remains consistently slower in quiet conditions, investigate its band, link rate, software and hardware next.

5. Battery and power-management behaviour can change performance

Phones are designed to balance responsiveness, heat and battery life. Their operating systems and manufacturers may adjust background activity, network behaviour or application work to conserve energy. These policies vary by model and software version. A battery-saving mode is not guaranteed to reduce every Wi-Fi speed test, and it is not correct to assume that all phones deliberately throttle Wi-Fi whenever the screen is off. Still, power-management settings are a reasonable variable to check if the phone performs differently when battery saver is enabled, when the battery is very low or when the device becomes warm.

For a fair test, charge the phone to a comfortable level, let it return to a normal temperature and temporarily turn off battery saver if it is active. Keep the screen awake during the test and avoid running a large number of applications. Do not disable safety or thermal protections. If the phone is hot, allow it to cool naturally before testing again. Heat can affect the performance of many electronic components, and a phone may change its behaviour to manage temperature. Never place a phone in a refrigerator or use other extreme cooling methods.

Compare results with the phone's normal settings restored after the controlled test. If the speed difference only appears with a particular power mode, decide whether the trade-off is acceptable for everyday use. The goal is not to maximise one speed-test number at the expense of battery life; it is to understand whether a setting is changing the experience you care about.

6. Background apps, VPNs and network filters can distort the comparison

A phone can be doing work that is invisible on the screen. Photo synchronisation, app updates, messaging attachments, operating-system downloads, cloud drives and media backups can use bandwidth while you are testing. A laptop may also have background traffic, but the two devices rarely have identical workloads. Before comparing speeds, pause large downloads and uploads on both devices. Close streaming sessions and wait for pending updates to finish. Avoid force-stopping essential system processes or changing multiple settings at once.

A VPN can change the path that traffic takes. The speed test may travel through a VPN server before reaching its measurement server, adding distance, processing and possible congestion. Security applications, filtering services, parental-control software and custom DNS configurations can also affect access to particular services or the way a test is reached. If you use a VPN, compare results with it on and off only when it is safe and appropriate to do so. Follow your workplace or organisation's security policy; do not disable a required corporate VPN or security control merely to improve a speed test.

Private DNS and ordinary DNS should not be confused with raw Wi-Fi throughput. DNS helps translate a domain name into an address. A slow DNS response can make the start of a website feel delayed, but it does not normally explain a sustained low download rate once a large transfer is underway. Similarly, a browser extension or content blocker may affect page loading without reducing the actual Wi-Fi link capacity. Test both a speed measurement and a few normal tasks, such as opening a new website and downloading a known file from a trusted service.

Android's Data Saver is designed to reduce data use by restricting background activity for apps under relevant conditions. It is useful for conserving data, but it is not a universal Wi-Fi speed limiter. Its behaviour depends on network classification, Android version and app implementation. If an application behaves differently with Data Saver enabled, check the setting and app-specific allowances rather than assuming the router is at fault.

7. The browser or speed-test method may be the real difference

A phone browser and a laptop browser are not necessarily running the same test code under identical conditions. They may use different browser versions, CPU resources, connection settings, server selection, parallel connections or measurement techniques. A native speed-test app may behave differently from a website opened in a mobile browser. The test server itself can also be busy or geographically distant. That means a lower phone result is not always a direct measurement of the phone's Wi-Fi radio.

For a meaningful comparison, use the same reputable test service and, where possible, the same selected server. Run the tests close together in time. Avoid comparing a phone result taken during peak evening use with a laptop result taken early in the morning. Run at least three tests on each device and compare the median rather than selecting the single highest number. Record download, upload and latency separately. If the test supports jitter or packet loss, record those too, but understand that each metric describes a different aspect of connection quality.

Do not run both speed tests simultaneously. They will compete for the same broadband connection and wireless airtime, making the comparison less useful. Test one device, wait for the transfer to finish, then test the other. Alternate the order across several rounds: phone first, laptop first, then phone first again. This reduces the chance that a changing network condition always favours the same device.

8. A controlled phone-versus-laptop Wi-Fi test

The following procedure is designed to isolate variables without requiring paid tools. You can use your existing router settings and a reputable browser-based speed test. Do not reset the router or phone before collecting a baseline. Changing settings first can hide the original cause.

  1. Choose a quiet test period. Pause large downloads, cloud backups, game updates and streaming on both devices and other household equipment where practical. Tell other users that you are running a short test so their activity does not unexpectedly change the result.
  2. Use one physical location. Place the phone and laptop close to each other, at roughly the same height and orientation. Keep them away from the router's immediate surface and do not stack one device on top of the other. If possible, use a table with a clear path to the access point.
  3. Check the network name and band. Confirm that both devices are connected to the intended home network. Inspect the frequency band on each device or in the router's client list. If the devices are on different bands, repeat with both on the same band when your router allows it.
  4. Check for VPNs and unusual traffic. Note whether either device is using a VPN, proxy, security filter or data-management app. Pause optional large transfers. Do not disable required security controls.
  5. Use the same test service and server. If the service lets you choose a server, select the same one on both devices. If it selects automatically and does not expose the server, record that limitation and avoid treating the numbers as a perfect hardware comparison.
  6. Test sequentially. Run one test on the phone, wait for completion, then run one on the laptop. Repeat for at least three rounds, alternating which device goes first. Keep a note of the time and results.
  7. Compare more than download speed. Record upload, idle latency and, if available, loaded latency, jitter and packet loss. A phone may have a similar download result but much worse latency under load, which can affect calls and gaming.
  8. Repeat near the router. If the gap is large in the usual room, repeat the same process near the router. If the gap disappears nearby, distance, walls, band selection or local interference may be responsible.
  9. Repeat on another Wi-Fi network. If practical, test the phone and laptop on another trusted network. If the phone is slow on multiple networks while the laptop is not, the phone's settings, software or hardware deserve closer attention.
  10. Change one variable at a time. Update software, forget and rejoin the network, test another band or remove an accessory one at a time. Repeat the same test after each change so you can identify what actually helped.

9. How to check the phone itself

Android phones

Android settings differ across brands such as Google Pixel, Samsung, OnePlus, Xiaomi, Motorola and other manufacturers. Menu names and available diagnostics can also change with the Android version. Start with the basics: make sure Wi-Fi is on, confirm Airplane mode is off, restart the phone and check whether the problem occurs on more than one network. Google's Pixel support guidance recommends comparing with another device and testing the phone on another network to help separate a phone problem from a network or internet problem. Those are useful general diagnostic ideas, although exact menu steps may differ on a non-Pixel phone.

Open the connected network's details and inspect any available frequency, link speed or signal information. If your phone consistently joins 2.4 GHz while the laptop joins 5 GHz, try the 5 GHz network when you are close enough for a stable signal. If the phone supports only 2.4 GHz, it cannot be forced to use 5 GHz through a setting. Check the exact model specification rather than relying on the product family name.

Install system updates from the phone manufacturer and update the browser or speed-test app. If the problem began immediately after installing a network, VPN or security app, test whether that app is involved. Google's troubleshooting guidance describes using safe mode on supported Pixel devices to check whether a downloaded app is causing a connection issue. Safe-mode behaviour varies by manufacturer, so follow your device maker's instructions. If Wi-Fi works normally in safe mode, investigate recently installed apps one by one.

Forgetting and reconnecting to the Wi-Fi network can help if the saved profile is damaged or contains outdated settings. Before doing so, make sure you know the Wi-Fi password. A full network-settings reset is more disruptive: it can remove saved Wi-Fi networks and other network configurations. Use it only after simpler steps fail and after checking the manufacturer's instructions. If the phone is managed by an employer or school, consult the administrator first.

iPhone and iPad

On an iPhone, confirm that Wi-Fi is enabled and that Airplane Mode is off. Check whether the problem happens only on one network or on multiple trusted networks. Apple's support guidance recommends checking the router, ensuring the device is within range, keeping router firmware current and trying another frequency band when the router provides separate bands. It also recommends checking whether VPN or security software is interfering when connection problems persist.

Do not jump straight to Reset Network Settings. Apple notes that this removes saved network configurations and other network-related settings. Record any information you may need to reconnect, and use the reset only after simpler troubleshooting has failed. If the phone connects to Wi-Fi but cannot access the internet while other devices work normally, test another network and contact Apple or the network administrator if the issue follows the device.

Windows laptops

The laptop should be checked too. A laptop can appear faster because it is using a different band or adapter, but it can also have driver or configuration problems that make a comparison misleading. In Windows, inspect the Wi-Fi network properties and the adapter's supported radio types. Microsoft provides troubleshooting guidance for changing frequency bands, comparing another device on the same network and checking whether the wireless adapter supports the router's frequency. Update Wi-Fi drivers through the laptop manufacturer or the approved Windows update channel rather than installing random driver packages.

10. When router settings are worth checking

Only move to router changes after you have recorded the baseline and checked the devices. If both devices slow down in the same room, the router, coverage or broadband connection may be involved. If only the phone is slow in the same conditions, focus first on the phone. If the phone is slow only on one band, check the router's band configuration, channel congestion and coverage. If the phone is slow only on one access point in a mesh system, investigate roaming and node placement.

Check whether the router has separate 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz network names. A temporary separate name can make testing easier, but it is not necessary to keep separate names permanently. Some routers use band steering and a single network name for convenience. If you change a channel, channel width or security mode, record the original setting and change only one item at a time. Incorrect settings can disconnect older devices or reduce compatibility. If the router belongs to your ISP, ask the provider before changing advanced settings.

Update router firmware using the manufacturer's official process. Firmware updates can address reliability, compatibility and security issues, but they are not guaranteed to increase speed. Avoid factory-resetting the router as an early troubleshooting step because it may erase ISP configuration, Wi-Fi names, passwords and custom settings. If you use a mesh network, test near the primary router first, then near each satellite. A satellite with a weak backhaul connection can provide a strong local Wi-Fi signal while still delivering poor internet performance to the client.

11. What the results can tell you

Phone slow on every Wi-Fi network: if the phone is consistently slower than another device on several independent networks, check the phone's supported bands, software updates, VPN and security apps, case or accessories, and any recent physical damage. If the problem persists after basic troubleshooting, contact the phone manufacturer or an authorised service provider. A hardware fault is possible, but it should not be assumed before checking software and test conditions.

Phone slow only at home: if the phone performs normally elsewhere, investigate the home router, band steering, channel congestion, mesh roaming, access-control rules and saved network profile. The issue may be a compatibility or configuration interaction between that phone and that router rather than a fault in either device.

Phone slow only in one room: this pattern points towards coverage, obstruction or interference. Test near the router and in the problem room. Consider moving the router to a more central, open position or improving coverage with a properly placed access point or mesh node. Do not buy a new router until you know that coverage is the limiting factor.

Speed test is low but normal apps work well: the test server, browser, VPN route or measurement method may be influencing the result. Try a second reputable test and compare real tasks. A single test does not define the full quality of a connection.

Download is fast but video calls or games lag: investigate latency, jitter, packet loss and loaded latency, not only download throughput. Video calls and interactive games are sensitive to delay variation and packet loss. A connection with a high download number can still feel poor when the network is busy.

12. Common mistakes to avoid

Do not assume full Wi-Fi bars mean full internet speed. Bars do not reveal all the factors that determine throughput. Do not compare results taken at different times, in different rooms or on different test servers and call the difference a device limitation. Do not run simultaneous speed tests. Do not repeatedly reset the router, phone and network settings without recording what changed. If the results improve, you will not know which action helped.

Do not install unknown “Wi-Fi booster” applications that promise to increase radio power or unlock hidden speed. An app cannot change the physical antenna design or make unsupported frequency bands appear. Some apps simply display connection details or run a speed test; others may show intrusive advertising or request unnecessary permissions. Use built-in settings and trusted diagnostic tools first.

Do not change advanced router security settings just to chase a higher speed number. Disabling encryption or weakening security is not an acceptable performance fix. Keep modern security enabled where supported and use the router manufacturer's documentation. If an older device cannot connect using the current security mode, seek a secure compatibility option from the manufacturer rather than leaving the network open.

Do not conclude that a phone is faulty from one disappointing test. Wireless performance varies naturally. Repeat the test, record the conditions and compare medians. If the phone is consistently slow across several networks, at close range, with updated software and without unusual traffic, then a service check is more reasonable.

13. Choosing the right fix without wasting money

If the phone was simply connecting to 2.4 GHz while the laptop used 5 GHz, a new broadband plan is unlikely to solve the difference. If the phone has older Wi-Fi hardware, a faster router may not make it perform like a newer laptop. If both devices slow down behind the same wall, moving the router or improving coverage may be more useful than replacing the phone. If the phone is slow only while a VPN is active, investigate the VPN route or service rather than buying networking equipment.

When considering a router upgrade, check the devices you actually own. A Wi-Fi 7 router cannot give a Wi-Fi 5 phone Wi-Fi 7 capabilities. Newer routers can still improve network capacity, coverage and management for multiple devices, but the benefit depends on client support, placement and configuration. Consider the broadband plan, wired backhaul options, number of rooms, wall construction and the location of heavy-use devices. A well-placed mid-range access point can sometimes be more useful than a high-end router hidden in a corner.

If the phone is older and its supported Wi-Fi features are limited, you may decide that its performance is adequate for messaging, browsing and standard video. A faster laptop does not mean every device needs to reach the same throughput. Match the solution to the task: stable calls, responsive browsing, reliable uploads or large downloads may matter more than the maximum download number.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my phone slower than my laptop on the same Wi-Fi?

The devices may have different Wi-Fi radios, antennas, supported bands, channel widths or spatial streams. They may also connect to different bands or access points, run different background tasks or use different VPN and test settings. Compare them in the same place, on the same band and with the same test service before deciding that the phone is the cause.

Does a laptop always have better Wi-Fi than a phone?

No. Performance depends on the exact models, radio design, supported Wi-Fi features, software, placement and network conditions. A modern phone can outperform an older laptop. Compare device specifications and repeatable real-world results rather than assuming one category is always faster.

Can a phone case make Wi-Fi slower?

Some accessories can affect radio performance depending on their materials and design, but an ordinary case is not automatically a problem. If the slowdown began after adding a case, compare results with it removed while keeping all other conditions the same. Avoid drawing conclusions from a single test.

Should I connect my phone to 5 GHz instead of 2.4 GHz?

Try 5 GHz when the phone and router support it and the phone is reasonably close to the access point. It often offers more throughput, but 2.4 GHz can provide better range through walls. The best band depends on distance, interference and the actual task. Test both bands where available.

Does battery saver reduce Wi-Fi speed?

It can influence device behaviour on some models and software versions, but there is no universal rule that battery saver always reduces Wi-Fi throughput. Compare with battery saver on and off while the phone is cool and the test conditions are unchanged. Restore your preferred setting after testing.

Can Data Saver make Wi-Fi slow?

Android Data Saver is designed to limit data use and background activity for apps in supported situations. It is not a general Wi-Fi radio speed control. If one app behaves differently, check its background-data permissions and the network's metered status. Menu names vary by Android version and manufacturer.

Why does my phone show full Wi-Fi bars but a low speed?

Signal bars are only a simplified indicator. They do not show channel congestion, interference, retransmissions, available airtime, latency under load or the speed of the internet connection beyond the router. A strong signal can still share a busy channel or use a slower negotiated link.

Will buying a new router make my phone faster?

Only if the router or coverage is a meaningful bottleneck and the phone can use the relevant features. A new router cannot add unsupported Wi-Fi bands or spatial streams to a phone. Test near the existing router, compare bands and inspect the phone's supported capabilities before spending money.

How many speed tests should I run?

Run at least three sequential tests per device under consistent conditions and compare the median. Alternate which device goes first, use the same test service and server where possible, and avoid simultaneous tests. Record the band, location, time and whether a VPN or background transfer was active.

When should I contact the phone manufacturer?

Contact the manufacturer if the phone remains substantially slower across multiple trusted Wi-Fi networks, at close range, after software updates and basic troubleshooting, especially if the problem began after physical damage or a repair. Share the model, operating-system version and repeatable test conditions rather than only one speed-test screenshot.
