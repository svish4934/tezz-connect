Greener Internet: The Energy Behind Data Centres, Networks and Streaming

A video call, a map search, a streamed film or an AI answer can feel weightless: tap a screen and information appears. Behind that moment are phones and laptops, radio sites, fibre routes, routers, storage systems and data centres running around the clock. They use electricity, generate heat, require cooling and depend on equipment that must be mined, manufactured, transported and eventually replaced. The internet is not one machine, and it does not have one fixed environmental footprint. Its impact depends on where a service runs, how much hardware it needs, which electricity powers it, how efficiently it is used and what is included in the calculation. This guide follows a digital request through that physical system, explains what current research can and cannot tell us, and shows how providers and everyday users can make more credible choices.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

1. Is the internet really using energy?

Yes. The internet is a global system of physical infrastructure. A request from your phone may travel over Wi-Fi to a home router, through a fibre access network, across an internet service provider's equipment and into a data centre or another network. On mobile, the first hop may be a radio connection to a nearby base station. The service may also rely on caching servers, content delivery networks, undersea cables, cloud platforms and the device itself.

Each part needs power. Network equipment converts electricity into radio signals, optical signals and data processing. Servers run applications and store information. Cooling systems remove heat. Backup batteries and generators support resilience. Your phone or television also consumes electricity while receiving, decoding and displaying content.

That does not mean every tap triggers every part of the global internet. Content may be cached close to you, a service may use a local data centre, or a request may be handled by a small nearby system. The route and workload vary. A short message, a live video stream, a cloud backup and an AI model request are different activities, so assigning them one universal energy number would be misleading.

The useful starting point is to ask: which equipment is included, what task is being measured, where and when did it run, and what energy source powered it?

2. Where does the electricity go?

It helps to separate the internet into connected layers. They overlap in real life, but each has different efficiency opportunities.

LayerWhat uses energyWhat changes the footprint
Data centresServers, accelerators, storage, memory, power conversion, cooling and lighting.Workload, server utilisation, chip efficiency, cooling design, facility age and electricity mix.
Fixed networksFibre access equipment, optical amplifiers, routers, switches, cable landing stations and customer premises equipment.Network architecture, traffic levels, equipment generations, idle power and replacement cycles.
Mobile networksRadio base stations, antennas, baseband processing, backhaul, core networks and site cooling.Coverage design, traffic, site density, radio technology, sleep modes, power supply and backup systems.
End-user devicesPhones, computers, televisions, streaming boxes, smart-home devices and home routers.Screen size and brightness, device age, workload, standby power, battery charging and useful life.

Electricity used directly by a data centre is only one part of the total. A complete assessment may also count the electricity used to generate that power, the emissions from manufacturing hardware, construction materials and the electricity consumed by networks and user devices. Different studies draw their boundaries differently. That is one reason published totals sometimes appear to disagree.

3. Data centres: the engine rooms behind digital services

A data centre is a building or group of buildings designed to house computing and storage equipment with reliable power, networking, physical security and heat removal. Cloud services, websites, banking systems, video platforms, enterprise software and AI workloads may all run in these facilities.

Demand is growing, particularly as organisations adopt cloud services and AI. The International Energy Agency's 2026 outlook estimates that global data-centre electricity consumption was about 485 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2025 and could reach around 950 TWh in 2030. The estimate is a scenario-based outlook, not a guaranteed outcome. The IEA identifies AI as a major driver, while also recognising demand from other digital services and the constraints involved in building power and data-centre capacity.

Why does AI matter? Training and running large models can require substantial accelerator capacity, memory, networking and cooling. Some AI workloads are concentrated in high-density facilities. But not every data centre is an AI data centre, and not every AI request has the same computational cost. Model size, hardware, prompt length, output length, caching, batching, utilisation and service design all affect energy use. Public estimates for a single AI prompt often use different assumptions and should not be treated as universal facts.

More computing capacity does not automatically mean the same percentage increase in electricity consumption. New chips can perform more work per unit of energy; software can use hardware more effectively; and operators can improve cooling and power systems. Yet efficiency gains can be outweighed if the total number and intensity of workloads grow faster. This is the rebound challenge: cheaper or more efficient computing can encourage more use.

4. What is PUE—and what does it leave out?

Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is a common data-centre efficiency metric. It is calculated by dividing the total energy entering a data-centre facility by the energy used by its IT equipment over the same period.

If a facility uses 1.5 units of total electricity for every 1 unit consumed by its IT equipment, its PUE is 1.5. A value closer to 1 means less overhead energy is used outside the IT load. It is useful for tracking a facility over time and comparing similar facilities when the measurement boundaries are consistent.

But PUE is not a complete sustainability score. It does not tell you whether the servers are doing useful work, whether the electricity is low-carbon, how much water is consumed, how much hardware was manufactured, or whether equipment is being replaced too quickly. A facility can have an excellent PUE and still use a large amount of electricity because it operates a very large IT load.

Uptime Institute's 2025 global survey reported a weighted average annual PUE of 1.54 among respondents, with the headline average having changed little over several years. That is a survey result, not a universal value for every data centre. Facility size, climate, age, workload and measurement practice all matter. A single PUE figure should never be used to rank providers without understanding those differences.

Other metrics help complete the picture. Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE) tracks water use against IT energy under a defined method. Carbon Usage Effectiveness (CUE) relates carbon emissions to IT energy. Renewable energy matching, waste heat reuse, equipment utilisation and hardware lifecycle data can add further context. The ITU's 2026 Recommendation L.1312 explicitly promotes a multi-dimensional view covering energy, water and other resources, greenhouse-gas emissions and wider environmental impacts.

5. Cooling: the heat behind the cloud

Computers turn electrical energy into computation and heat. That heat must be carried away so processors, memory and power systems remain within their operating limits. Traditional facilities often move air through server racks and use chillers, cooling towers or other heat-rejection systems. The best design depends on climate, building layout, water availability, equipment density and reliability requirements.

AI and high-performance computing can create much higher rack power densities than older general-purpose workloads. Air cooling remains useful in many facilities, but direct-to-chip liquid cooling, rear-door heat exchangers, immersion systems and closed-loop designs are increasingly considered where heat loads justify them. Liquid cooling does not automatically mean zero water use: systems differ, and heat still has to be rejected somewhere. A closed loop can reduce or avoid some forms of on-site water consumption, while the electricity used to run pumps and external heat-rejection equipment still matters. The IEA also highlights a wider design trade-off: some air-cooled configurations use less water but more electricity, while water-cooled configurations can use less electricity while consuming more water. Climate, local water stress and the full cooling design therefore matter more than a technology label alone.

Water is a local issue as much as a global one. A facility in a water-stressed area can create a different community impact from one in a water-abundant region, even if their annual totals look similar. It is important to distinguish water withdrawal—the amount taken from a source—from water consumption, the portion not returned to the same watershed in a usable form. Reports should also state whether they include water used to generate electricity and manufacture chips, not just water used inside the building.

The IEA's 2026 World Energy Outlook estimates that water consumption associated with data centres reached 885 billion litres in 2025 when the wider electricity and supply-chain footprint is included; about 240 billion litres was directly consumed within data centres. These are global estimates with defined accounting boundaries, not a claim that every facility uses water in the same way. The distinction between direct and indirect water is essential when interpreting the headline.

6. India's data-centre growth: energy and water choices matter now

India is building more digital infrastructure to support payments, government services, cloud applications, streaming, enterprise systems and AI. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology reported in August 2026 that installed data-centre capacity had grown from about 375 megawatts (MW) in 2020 to around 1,575 MW. The same government update cited a Central Electricity Authority estimate of data-centre power demand of about 17 gigawatts (GW) by 2031–32.

These figures describe different things. Installed capacity is a measure of the sector's data-centre capacity; projected power demand is an estimate of future electricity requirements. They should not be compared as if they were the same unit or the same type of measurement. The projection depends on future construction, utilisation, technology and the definition of what is included.

Independent research adds another layer. A February 2026 study by the Council on Energy, Environment and Water (CEEW) and SYSTEMIQ reports estimates of around 0.5 per cent of national electricity consumption and approximately 150 billion litres of annual water use for India's data centres. Its detailed section identifies the underlying estimates as 2024 figures and says both could more than double by 2030. These are study estimates, not a government meter reading for every facility. The report highlights the importance of location, electricity sourcing and cooling choices as the sector expands.

Mumbai and Navi Mumbai are among India's major data-centre locations. That makes the issue especially relevant to the wider Mumbai region: power availability, grid planning, water stress, land use, heat and resilience all influence where new facilities can operate responsibly. A data centre's local impact cannot be judged only by the number of jobs or the amount of investment it attracts.

Government updates describe measures such as renewable-energy access, building-efficiency standards and advanced cooling options including direct-to-chip liquid cooling, adiabatic cooling, immersion cooling and closed-loop systems. These technologies can help, but their real-world benefit depends on design and operation. A credible sustainability report should disclose actual electricity use, water withdrawals and consumption, cooling method, power sourcing and performance over time.

7. Mobile networks and the energy cost of staying connected

Mobile networks use electricity at radio sites, baseband units, transmission links, core-network facilities and supporting buildings. A site may consume power even when traffic is low because it must remain ready to provide coverage, mobility and emergency connectivity. In hot climates, cooling and backup power add to the engineering challenge.

Traffic growth does not translate directly into the same growth in electricity use. Operators can move more data with newer radio equipment, better antennas, efficient power amplifiers, fibre backhaul and software controls. They can also place equipment into lower-power states when demand is low, provided coverage and service quality remain acceptable.

The GSMA's Mobile Net Zero 2026 report found that, among 18 operators reporting the relevant metric, energy intensity of data transmission fell by an average of 15 per cent per year between 2019 and 2025. That is an average intensity trend in the reporting sample, not proof that total network electricity fell by 15 per cent annually. The same report warns that network densification, more antenna elements and growth in fixed wireless access can increase total electricity demand even as each unit of data becomes more efficient.

Fixed networks have their own trade-offs. Fibre can be more energy-efficient per unit of data than some older access technologies, but the whole system still includes optical equipment, routers, customer premises devices and the electricity mix. Retiring legacy equipment can save energy, but replacement also has a material footprint. Operators need to consider both operational efficiency and the lifecycle cost of new hardware.

8. Streaming: is higher video quality always worse?

Streaming uses energy in the device, home network or mobile connection, content delivery network and origin data centre. The amount varies with video resolution, codec, screen size, viewing duration, network path, server utilisation and how close cached content is to the viewer.

Higher resolution can require more bits and more processing, but the relationship is not a simple fixed multiplier. Modern codecs can deliver better visual quality at lower bitrates than older formats. A large television may use more electricity than a phone, while a phone's smaller screen and lower resolution may reduce the display and decoding load. A Wi-Fi router or set-top box also draws power while it is operating.

There is no reliable universal statement such as “one hour of streaming uses exactly this much electricity” that applies to every person and service. A useful estimate needs to state the device, resolution, bitrate, network technology, viewing duration, data-centre assumptions and electricity mix. If a report gives a single number without a method, treat it as an illustration rather than a personal bill.

Practical choices are straightforward: choose the resolution that suits the screen and viewing distance, avoid leaving streams running when nobody is watching, use efficient devices, and keep equipment for its useful life. There is no need to turn every video into a climate calculation. The bigger gains often come from efficient infrastructure and sensible product design at scale.

9. The hidden footprint: manufacturing, minerals and e-waste

Electricity during operation is only part of the footprint. Servers, GPUs, phones, laptops, displays, batteries, routers and fibre equipment require materials, factories, transport and packaging. Semiconductor manufacturing uses energy, ultra-pure water and specialised chemicals. Mining and processing metals can affect land, water and ecosystems. Buildings also require steel, concrete, glass and other materials.

These impacts are often called embodied emissions or embodied environmental impacts. They happen before a device is switched on and may be significant, particularly for equipment that is replaced frequently. A more energy-efficient new device is not automatically the greener choice if manufacturing it creates a large footprint and the old device still works well. The answer depends on the efficiency improvement, product lifespan, repairability, electricity mix and end-of-life treatment.

Extending useful life can reduce the need to manufacture replacements. Repairing a phone, upgrading memory or storage where possible, replacing a battery, refurbishing servers and reusing network equipment can all help. Recycling is important, but it is not a substitute for reducing unnecessary production: collection and recovery rates vary, and some materials are difficult to reclaim at high quality.

Responsible operators should disclose hardware refresh policies, repair and reuse programmes, e-waste handling and supplier expectations. Consumers can use devices for longer when security updates and battery health allow, and should send retired electronics to authorised collection and recycling channels rather than informal disposal.

10. What makes a digital service genuinely greener?

A greener service is not defined by a leaf symbol, a renewable-energy press release or a claim that it runs in the cloud. It requires a clear boundary, a useful unit of work and measurements that can be checked. The Green Software Foundation's Software Carbon Intensity (SCI) specification, standardised as ISO/IEC 21031:2024, is one approach. It expresses emissions per functional unit, such as per user, transaction or API call, and includes both operational energy emissions and an allocation for hardware embodied emissions.

This way of thinking matters because a total can rise while a service becomes more efficient. If a platform doubles its users but cuts emissions per transaction, the intensity improves while total emissions may still increase. Both measures are useful: total impact shows the scale of the system; intensity shows how efficiently it delivers a defined service.

Teams can improve a service by removing unnecessary computation, reducing redundant data transfer, caching frequently requested content, using efficient data formats, improving server utilisation, choosing right-sized hardware and avoiding idle resources. Developers can measure energy and performance for real workloads rather than optimising only for code size or benchmark scores.

Carbon-aware computing can schedule flexible tasks when and where electricity is cleaner, where practical. But shifting a workload is not automatically a reduction if it merely moves demand to another grid, increases data transfer or creates new infrastructure. Location and time matter, and the method should disclose how electricity carbon intensity was calculated.

Renewable-energy claims also need care. Buying renewable certificates or signing a power purchase agreement can support clean-energy investment, but annual matching does not necessarily mean a facility is powered by renewable electricity every hour. Stronger reporting distinguishes physical supply, contractual instruments, hourly matching, additional generation and residual grid emissions. A provider should explain exactly what its “100% renewable” statement means.

11. What should customers ask cloud and internet providers?

Businesses buying cloud, hosting, connectivity or managed services can ask for evidence rather than relying on a broad sustainability promise. Useful questions include:

  • What is included? Does the footprint cover data centres only, or also networks, customer devices, manufacturing and supply chains?
  • Which locations are used? Where is the workload processed, and what electricity mix applies to those locations?
  • How much energy is used? Are total electricity and energy intensity both disclosed, with a clear functional unit?
  • What about water? Are withdrawal and consumption reported separately, with direct cooling distinguished from indirect water used for electricity?
  • How is renewable power counted? Is the claim based on annual certificates, contracts, actual supply or hourly matching?
  • How efficient is the service? Are PUE, WUE, CUE, server utilisation and workload efficiency reported with boundaries and dates?
  • What happens to equipment? Are reuse, repair, refurbishment, recycling and product-life targets measured?
  • Can the figures be checked? Is there a published methodology, reporting period and independent assurance?

For smaller businesses, the simplest approach is to ask for the provider's latest sustainability report and check whether its claims match the service and region being purchased. Avoid comparing two providers using one headline metric if their boundaries are different.

12. What can everyday internet users do?

Individual habits matter, but they should be proportionate. The internet enables useful services such as remote work, online learning, digital payments and access to information. The aim is not to avoid being online; it is to reduce waste and encourage better products and infrastructure.

  • Keep devices longer: Repair batteries and screens where practical, install security updates and avoid replacing a working device only for a small specification upgrade.
  • Choose fit-for-purpose hardware: A device that meets your needs without unnecessary processing power can reduce purchase cost and material demand.
  • Turn off what you do not use: Shut down spare screens, set-top boxes and unused equipment when convenient, while keeping essential security and connectivity functions available.
  • Use sensible streaming settings: Select a resolution suited to the screen and avoid unattended playback. Do not assume that the highest setting always provides a visible benefit.
  • Use efficient home networking: Replace faulty or obsolete equipment when there is a genuine need, and choose appropriately sized routers and mesh systems rather than adding hardware without a coverage problem.
  • Recycle electronics responsibly: Use authorised e-waste collection or manufacturer take-back programmes.
  • Support transparent providers: Ask internet, cloud and device companies to publish comparable energy, water and lifecycle information.

One user's decision is small compared with a national network or data-centre build-out. But millions of purchasing choices influence product design, replacement cycles and demand. The most important changes still require operators, manufacturers, regulators and energy providers to measure and improve the infrastructure itself.

13. The greener internet of 2030 and beyond

The next decade will bring more AI services, connected devices, high-capacity networks, cloud computing and digital public infrastructure. That growth will increase demand for electricity, materials and cooling. It will also create opportunities: more efficient chips, liquid cooling, better power management, renewable electricity, grid flexibility, heat reuse, fibre upgrades, circular hardware and software that performs useful work with less computation.

There is no single technology that makes the internet sustainable. A new chip may improve performance per watt but encourage larger workloads. A water-saving cooling system may need more electricity. A renewable contract may reduce reported emissions without changing the electricity available at a particular hour. A newer network may carry each gigabyte more efficiently while total traffic and site count rise.

The right question is not simply whether a technology is “green”. It is whether the complete system delivers more useful digital service with lower total environmental impact—and whether that improvement is measured transparently across energy, water, emissions, materials and waste.

A greener internet is therefore an engineering and governance challenge as much as a consumer choice. The strongest progress will come from transparent data, efficient infrastructure, cleaner electricity, longer-lived equipment and honest reporting about what is—and is not—being counted.

Frequently asked questions

1. Does using the internet consume electricity?

Yes. Data centres, fibre and mobile networks, routers and end-user devices all use electricity to process, transport, store and display information.

2. How much electricity do data centres use worldwide?

The IEA's 2026 outlook estimates about 485 TWh in 2025 and around 950 TWh by 2030. The 2030 figure is a projection, and estimates depend on definitions, workloads and future infrastructure growth.

3. Does AI use more energy than ordinary websites?

Some AI workloads, especially large-model training and inference on accelerated hardware, can be energy-intensive. But there is no single energy cost for an AI prompt; model, hardware, output, utilisation and service design all matter.

4. What is PUE in a data centre?

Power Usage Effectiveness is total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy. It measures facility overhead but does not show carbon intensity, water use, hardware manufacturing or how much useful work servers perform.

5. Do data centres consume a lot of water?

Some facilities use water directly for cooling, while additional water may be associated with electricity generation and hardware manufacturing. The amount depends on location, cooling design and accounting boundaries.

6. Is liquid cooling always better for the environment?

Not automatically. It can manage high-density heat loads efficiently, but pumps, heat rejection, water use and the complete facility design still need to be considered.

7. Is streaming video bad for the environment?

Streaming has an energy footprint across devices, networks and data centres, but its impact varies with screen, resolution, codec, network route, duration and electricity mix. There is no single universal figure for every hour of viewing.

8. Does 5G use more electricity than 4G?

5G can move data more efficiently per unit of traffic, but total network electricity depends on coverage, site density, traffic, equipment and the retirement of older systems. Efficiency per gigabyte and total electricity are different measures.

9. What is the environmental impact of a smartphone?

It includes electricity used while charging and operating, plus materials, semiconductor and battery manufacturing, transport, repair and end-of-life treatment. Manufacturing can be a significant part of a device's lifecycle footprint.

10. What is Software Carbon Intensity?

SCI is a methodology for measuring software emissions per defined unit of work. It considers operational energy emissions and an allocation for the embodied emissions of the hardware used.

11. Is renewable-energy matching the same as 24/7 clean electricity?

No. Annual renewable-energy matching can differ from the physical electricity mix at each hour. Providers should explain whether claims use annual certificates, contracts, hourly matching or actual supply.

12. What is the biggest thing users can do to reduce their digital footprint?

Keep devices useful for longer, repair where practical, avoid unnecessary upgrades and choose sensible settings. At system scale, transparent infrastructure efficiency and cleaner electricity are essential.
