Ethernet Cable Categories Explained: Cat5e vs Cat6 vs Cat6A vs Cat8

Compare Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6A and Cat8 Ethernet cables by speed, distance, bandwidth, PoE and installation needs. Learn how to choose and test the right cable.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 27 November 2026 · Updated 27 November 2026

Choosing an Ethernet cable can look deceptively simple. Product listings show labels such as Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6A, Cat7 and Cat8, often beside impressive numbers such as 1,000 MHz, 10 Gbps or 40 Gbps. Those figures describe different things. A cable category is a performance specification for the cabling component; an Ethernet speed is a capability of a complete link; and the speed delivered by an internet connection is limited by the slowest relevant part of the path. Buying the cable with the largest number on its package does not automatically make a home or office network faster.

This guide explains the common copper Ethernet categories, what their ratings mean, how distance changes the answer, and how to select and verify a cable for a real installation. It is written for home users, small offices, installers and anyone comparing cable labels before buying. The central rule is straightforward: match the cable to the Ethernet ports, required distance, installation environment and future needs, then test the finished link.

1. What does Cat mean on an Ethernet cable?

Cat is short for category. It identifies a set of electrical performance requirements for balanced twisted-pair cabling and its connecting hardware. A category is not simply a marketing name or a direct speed rating. The requirements include characteristics that affect how reliably signals can travel through the cable, including insertion loss, return loss and crosstalk between pairs.

Ethernet over copper uses pairs of insulated conductors twisted together. The twists help reduce interference and unwanted coupling. The number and geometry of twists, conductor dimensions, insulation, separators, cable construction, connectors and installation practices all contribute to performance. A cable marked Cat6 does not guarantee a Cat6 channel if it is terminated with unsuitable jacks, damaged during installation or connected through lower-rated components.

Category ratings are associated with frequency ranges. Cat5e is specified to 100 MHz, Cat6 to 250 MHz, Cat6A to 500 MHz and Cat8 to 2,000 MHz. These are cabling bandwidth specifications, not the same unit as data throughput. MHz describes signal frequency; Mbps and Gbps describe bits transferred per second. A 500 MHz cable is not a 500 Mbps cable, and a higher MHz figure does not directly translate into an internet speed.

For a permanent building installation, the complete channel matters: fixed cable, patch panels, outlets, connectors and patch cords. A short pre-made patch cable is one component of a link. The category printed on one piece cannot compensate for a weak connector or poor termination elsewhere.

2. Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6A and Cat8 at a glance

The following is a practical summary of common balanced copper Ethernet cabling. Distances refer to standards-based channel use, not a promise that any cable sold online will achieve the rate. The exact application standard, cable quality, alien crosstalk environment, connectors and installation all matter.

Cat5e is still useful. If both devices have 1 Gigabit Ethernet ports and the installed channel is compliant, replacing a working Cat5e cable with Cat8 will not make the link negotiate above 1 Gbps. Cat6 adds frequency headroom and may be a sensible choice for new general-purpose runs, but it should not be sold as guaranteed 10 Gbps across 100 metres. Cat6A is the usual copper choice when a new 10GBASE-T channel must cover the full 100-metre structured-cabling distance. Cat8 is designed for a different, shorter-reach environment.

Do not interpret the table as a substitute for the applicable standards or a project specification. A professional installation should specify the required category or ISO/IEC class and the Ethernet applications it must support.

3. Cat5e: a practical choice for gigabit networks

Cat5e is an enhanced version of the earlier Cat5 category. It is specified to 100 MHz and is widely used for 1000BASE-T Gigabit Ethernet over a channel up to 100 metres when the cable and installation meet the relevant requirements. That makes it adequate for many routers, desktop computers, televisions, printers, access points and broadband connections whose local Ethernet ports operate at 1 Gbps or below.

Cat5e should not be dismissed as obsolete simply because newer labels exist. A sound, properly terminated Cat5e installation can continue to serve a household or small office well. IEEE 802.3bz also introduced 2.5GBASE-T and 5GBASE-T to help use existing cabling for multi-gigabit access. A compliant Cat5e channel is relevant to 2.5GBASE-T, but 5 Gbps operation should not be assumed for every Cat5e run. Check the exact application, cable condition and installation guidance.

When troubleshooting an older cable, inspect the ends for broken clips, bent contacts, corrosion, crushed sections or tight bends. A cable can pass a basic continuity test and still fail to deliver the expected high-frequency performance. If the link repeatedly negotiates at 100 Mbps rather than 1 Gbps, a damaged pair, poor termination or a device-port limitation may be responsible. Gigabit Ethernet uses all four pairs, so a cable with a pair fault may fall back to a lower rate.

For a short patch lead between a router and a gigabit computer, a certified Cat5e cable from a reputable manufacturer can be entirely reasonable. There is no technical benefit in paying for a much higher category if the devices and application do not use it.

4. Cat6: more headroom, but not universal 10 Gbps

Cat6 is specified to 250 MHz and supports 1000BASE-T over a channel up to 100 metres. Compared with Cat5e, it has tighter performance requirements and can provide more margin against interference and crosstalk when correctly installed. This makes it a common option for new office and home structured cabling where gigabit is the immediate requirement and some additional headroom is useful.

Cat6 is often advertised as a 10 Gbps cable. The important qualification is distance and environment. 10GBASE-T can operate over certain Cat6 channels at shorter lengths, commonly up to around 55 metres, but alien crosstalk from neighbouring cables and the actual quality of the installation influence whether the link meets requirements. A long bundle of cables in a crowded pathway is not equivalent to a short, isolated patch lead.

For 2.5GBASE-T and 5GBASE-T, Cat6 can be useful in existing installations. IEEE 802.3bz was developed to provide intermediate multi-gigabit speeds over installed Cat5e and Cat6 cabling. That does not mean every old cable will perform identically. If a network upgrade depends on 5 Gbps or 10 Gbps, test the installed channel to the relevant requirements instead of relying on its jacket printing.

Cat6 may be a good value when the channel is relatively short, the equipment is gigabit or multi-gigabit, and the installation is not being designed around guaranteed 10 Gbps at 100 metres. If the cable will be concealed inside walls for many years and full-distance 10 Gbps is a design requirement, consider Cat6A instead.

5. Cat6A: the straightforward copper option for 10 Gbps to 100 metres

Cat6A, sometimes written Category 6A or Augmented Category 6, is specified to 500 MHz. It was developed to support 10GBASE-T across a channel up to 100 metres. It also has more demanding alien-crosstalk performance requirements than Cat6. This makes it a common choice for new structured-cabling installations that need a full-length 10-gigabit copper path.

Cat6A can be a sensible option for high-performance office networks, server rooms, Wi-Fi access-point uplinks, workstations with 10 GbE adapters and building cabling expected to remain in place for years. It can also provide useful margin for Power over Ethernet deployments, although the correct cable gauge, bundle design, temperature rating and PoE standard still matter. Category alone does not determine how much power a particular installation can safely deliver.

There are trade-offs. Cat6A cable can be thicker, less flexible and more difficult to route through small conduits than Cat6 or Cat5e. Its bend radius, pathway fill, termination space and patch-panel compatibility should be considered before installation. Shielded versions may require compatible connectors and correct bonding practices. An unnecessarily large cable can make a poorly planned installation harder rather than better.

Cat6A does not make a 1 Gbps router port become 10 Gbps. Both ends must support 10GBASE-T, and intermediate switches, patch panels and other components must be suitable for the required channel. If the internet plan is 500 Mbps, Cat6A will not turn it into 10 Gbps; its value is in the local network capability and the useful life of the installed cabling.

6. Cat8: high speed over a much shorter channel

Cat8 is a high-performance copper category associated with 25GBASE-T and 40GBASE-T applications over channels up to 30 metres. It is aimed mainly at short links in data-centre and equipment-room environments, such as connections between switches and servers or between racks where the application and equipment are designed for it.

The 30-metre limit is a critical part of the Cat8 story. A product page that highlights 40 Gbps but hides the reach can lead a buyer to the wrong conclusion. Cat8 is not a universal upgrade for a home. Most consumer routers, laptops, televisions and game consoles have 1 Gbps or 2.5 Gbps ports; some higher-end devices have 10 GbE, but ordinary home equipment does not negotiate 25 or 40 Gbps just because a Cat8 cable is inserted.

Cat8 systems use stricter construction and shielding requirements, and their connectors and installation must match the relevant standard. A generic cable labelled Cat8 is not proof that it has been tested to a recognised component or channel specification. Check the manufacturer's technical data and, for infrastructure, require documented certification.

For a typical home, Cat8 is usually unnecessary. If the goal is a 10 Gbps link across a long building run, Cat6A is generally the more relevant category. If the goal is a short 25 or 40 Gbps data-centre connection, the network designer should select the appropriate Cat8 system or another supported media type based on equipment, topology, power, distance and lifecycle costs.

7. What about Cat7 and Cat7A?

Cat7 and Cat7A appear in online listings and are often presented as automatic upgrades over Cat6A. The standards landscape is more nuanced. Category 7 and 7A are part of ISO/IEC structured-cabling specifications, commonly associated with Class F and Class FA channels and shielded constructions. They are not TIA-recognised categories in the same way as Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6A and Cat8.

Some Cat7 product listings use familiar RJ45-style connectors, while other standards-based systems have used different connector interfaces. This matters because the connector system, patch panels, outlets and channel must be compatible. A cable marked Cat7 with ordinary modular plugs should not be assumed to deliver a standards-compliant Class F channel merely because the jacket says Cat7.

For many buyers, Cat6A is a simpler and more widely recognised route to 10GBASE-T over 100 metres. If a project specifically requires ISO/IEC Class F or FA, the designer should specify the complete compatible system rather than mixing parts based on marketing labels. Ask for the applicable standard, connector type, test report and warranty conditions.

Be cautious of claims such as Cat7e or Cat8.2 when the seller does not identify the exact standard and channel requirements. A category name without a traceable specification and credible test evidence is not a useful engineering guarantee.

8. Ethernet cable speed depends on the whole link

A wired Ethernet connection negotiates a speed supported by both link partners and the physical channel. If a 10 GbE computer connects to a 1 GbE router port, the link will operate at no more than 1 Gbps. If a switch between them has only gigabit ports, it is another limit. The cable category cannot override the port capabilities.

Think of the path as a chain: network adapter, patch lead, wall outlet, in-wall cable, patch panel, switch, router and the destination device. Every relevant segment must support the intended application. A single damaged connector or low-rated patch lead can undermine an otherwise capable installation. Auto-negotiation can also select a lower speed when the physical channel has errors or when the devices share only a lower mode.

Internet speed is a separate question. A 2.5 Gbps local Ethernet link does not mean an internet plan delivers 2.5 Gbps. The service tier, provider network, modem or optical terminal, router forwarding capacity, server, congestion and measurement method affect internet throughput. Local file transfers between two computers on the same switch can be faster than the internet connection, but only if both endpoints and the network path support it.

Use the link status on both endpoints or the switch to see the negotiated rate. For an internet speed test, connect a capable computer directly to a suitable router or gateway port, disable other heavy traffic, and confirm that the device itself can process the expected rate. A cable replacement is only one possible fix when a test result is low.

9. Cable length, bends, routing and interference

The familiar 100-metre limit for common structured copper Ethernet is a channel limit, not permission to run any cable for 100 metres in any conditions. A structured cabling channel normally includes the permanent link plus patch cords at both ends. The permitted component lengths and channel design should follow the applicable standard. Higher-speed applications may have shorter reach limits, as Cat8 illustrates.

Do not tightly coil excess cable, crush it under furniture, staple through it or pull it around sharp corners. Respect the manufacturer's minimum bend radius and pulling tension. Excessive force can deform the pair geometry and change electrical performance even when the outer jacket looks intact. Keep data cabling separated from sources of electrical interference according to local electrical and cabling rules, and avoid sharing pathways with power wiring unless the installation method is specifically permitted.

Parallel runs near electrical equipment, motors, fluorescent lighting ballasts or other noisy sources may need careful pathway planning. Shielded cable is not a magic solution. Shielding must be selected for the environment and installed with compatible hardware and bonding practices. Poorly implemented shielding can introduce problems rather than remove them. Many ordinary home and office installations use unshielded twisted pair successfully.

Outdoor cable requires suitable environmental protection, including UV resistance, moisture management and appropriate entry protection. Direct burial requires cable specifically rated for that purpose and local code compliance. Do not route indoor-only cable outdoors simply because it is inside a plastic conduit; moisture can still accumulate.

10. Power over Ethernet: data cable also carries electrical power

Power over Ethernet (PoE) allows compatible network equipment to send electrical power and data over the same twisted-pair cable. It is used for wireless access points, IP cameras, VoIP phones, access-control devices and other networked equipment. This can reduce the need for a separate power outlet near every device, but it adds design considerations to the cabling system.

PoE capability is not determined by the category label alone. The power sourcing equipment, such as a PoE switch or injector, must support the required IEEE PoE type; the powered device must accept it; and the cabling system must be suitable for the current and heat involved. Cable conductor size, conductor material, bundle size, ambient temperature, pathway ventilation and connector quality can all matter, especially with higher-power PoE.

Prefer solid-copper conductors for permanent building cabling and follow the system designer's specification. Avoid copper-clad aluminium (CCA) cable for installations where standards compliance, PoE performance and long-term reliability are required. CCA has different electrical resistance characteristics from solid copper and should not be treated as an equivalent substitute just because a listing claims the same category.

For a PoE access point or camera, check the device's power requirement, the switch's per-port and total power budget, the maximum cable route, and the cable manufacturer's specifications. A switch may advertise a high total PoE budget but still have a per-port limit. If several powered devices share a cable bundle, follow the applicable thermal guidance and installation requirements.

11. UTP, FTP, STP and shielded cable labels

Ethernet cable listings use several shield descriptions. UTP generally means unshielded twisted pair. Other designations describe foil or braid around individual pairs, around all pairs, or both. Common shorthand includes F/UTP, U/FTP, S/FTP and SF/UTP. The first part describes an overall screen; the part after the slash describes how the pairs are screened.

Shielding can be useful in electrically noisy industrial environments or where the installation design calls for it. It is not automatically required in every home or office. A shielded channel must be built with compatible shielded connectors, outlets, patch panels and appropriate bonding or earthing practices. Mixing shielded and unshielded parts without a design can compromise the intended performance and create confusing fault conditions.

For ordinary residential wiring, a reputable unshielded Cat5e, Cat6 or Cat6A system can be a sensible choice when installed away from interference sources and within the relevant rules. For industrial or specialist installations, follow the cabling designer's requirements and local electrical codes. Do not choose shielded cable solely because a seller uses words such as premium or professional.

Also look beyond shielding. Check conductor material, gauge, solid versus stranded construction, jacket rating, temperature rating, category certification and the intended use. Solid conductors are common for fixed horizontal cabling; stranded conductors are common for flexible patch cords. They serve different roles and should be terminated with compatible hardware.

12. How to choose the right cable for your situation

Start by identifying the ports at both ends. Look for the Ethernet speed printed in the device specifications: 100 Mbps, 1 Gbps, 2.5 Gbps, 5 Gbps or 10 Gbps. If the router, switch and computer are all gigabit, a compliant Cat5e or Cat6 channel is sufficient for a 1 Gbps link. Buying a 40 Gbps cable will not change the negotiated rate.

Next, measure the actual cable route, not the straight-line distance between devices. Include the route through walls, ceilings, conduits and equipment cabinets, plus patch cords. If a new in-wall run must support 10 Gbps across a channel up to 100 metres, Cat6A is the straightforward copper choice. If the run is short and the target is 1 Gbps or 2.5 Gbps, Cat5e or Cat6 may be appropriate depending on existing infrastructure and project requirements.

Then consider whether the cable will carry PoE, pass through a hot or electrically noisy area, be installed outdoors, or remain inaccessible for many years. These factors may justify a different cable construction, conductor gauge, jacket rating or category. Check the local building and fire-safety requirements before installing cable inside walls or ceilings. Plenum, riser, low-smoke and outdoor ratings are not interchangeable.

Finally, compare evidence rather than adjectives. Look for a clearly identified manufacturer, full category marking, conductor material, cable gauge, applicable standards, traceable test documentation and a return or warranty policy. Be wary of unusually cheap cable that claims a very high category but provides no technical data. A credible product specification is more valuable than a large speed number on the box.

13. How to test an Ethernet cable properly

A basic cable tester can identify some common faults, including open conductors, shorts, reversed pairs and split pairs. It is a useful first step for patch cables and simple troubleshooting. A continuity tester does not certify that a channel meets Cat6A or another high-frequency performance category. The cable may have all eight conductors connected and still fail because of excessive insertion loss, return loss or crosstalk.

For a permanent installation, use a tester appropriate to the required category or class. Certification testing checks multiple parameters defined by the selected standard. These can include wire map, length, propagation delay, delay skew, insertion loss, return loss, near-end crosstalk and other measurements. The selected test limit matters: a Cat5e pass does not prove Cat6A compliance.

Ask the installer for the test report for each permanent link, with the cable identifier, selected limit, result and date. Retain the report with the building or network documentation. If a link fails, the result can help locate a termination or cable problem. Re-terminating a poorly dressed pair, replacing a damaged patch lead or correcting a pathway issue may solve the fault, but retest after the repair.

For a home user, start with simple isolation: replace the patch cable with a known-good one, test another router or switch port, inspect the connectors, and check the negotiated link rate. If the problem remains on a concealed cable run, a professional cable test may be needed. Avoid repeatedly bending or pulling a cable to make a link work temporarily; that can worsen damage.

14. Common Ethernet cable buying myths

Myth: Cat8 always gives faster internet. Reality: the Ethernet ports and internet service set the practical ceiling. Cat8's higher application rates are intended for supported short links, not as a speed upgrade for gigabit devices.

Myth: MHz equals Mbps. Reality: MHz is a frequency rating for the cabling component. It is not a direct throughput figure and cannot be converted into internet speed by a simple one-to-one formula.

Myth: Cat6 supports 10 Gbps over 100 metres. Reality: Cat6 may support 10GBASE-T over shorter qualified channels, but Cat6A is the usual choice for 10 Gbps to 100 metres.

Myth: shielded cable is always better. Reality: shielding needs compatible components and correct bonding. A properly installed unshielded system is suitable for many environments.

Myth: a cable tester beep proves the cable is high category. Reality: continuity checks only basic electrical connections. Certification testing evaluates the performance parameters required by the selected category.

Myth: a cable labelled Cat7 or Cat8 must be certified. Reality: a printed label is not a test report. Ask for traceable specifications and evidence that the cable and complete channel meet the claimed standard.

15. A practical Ethernet cable checklist

  • Identify the maximum Ethernet speed supported by both endpoint ports and any intermediate switch.
  • Measure the full route and include patch cords when checking the channel distance.
  • Choose Cat5e or Cat6 for suitable gigabit links; use Cat6A when a new 10GBASE-T channel must reach 100 metres.
  • Use Cat8 only when the short-reach 25/40 Gbps application and equipment actually require it.
  • For PoE, verify the switch or injector, powered device, cable conductor material and thermal installation conditions.
  • Check solid versus stranded construction, conductor gauge, jacket rating and indoor/outdoor suitability.
  • Use shielded cable only when the environment and complete installation design call for it.
  • Keep cables within bend and pulling limits and away from unsuitable sources of interference.
  • Use reputable products with clear technical documentation; avoid unsupported speed claims and CCA for standards-based permanent cabling.
  • Test the completed channel to the required category or class and retain the results.

The best cable is not the one with the biggest category number. It is the one that meets the required application, distance, environment and installation standard at a sensible lifecycle cost. A correctly installed and tested Cat5e link can outperform a poorly terminated cable marketed as Cat8. Choose from the network outward: define the link speed, measure the route, specify the cabling system, and verify the finished work.

Frequently asked questions

Is Cat6 faster than Cat5e?

Cat6 has a higher specified bandwidth and more performance headroom, but both Cat5e and Cat6 support 1 Gigabit Ethernet over a compliant channel up to 100 metres. The connected ports and network standard determine the negotiated link speed.

Can Cat5e support 2.5 Gigabit Ethernet?

A compliant Cat5e channel can support 2.5GBASE-T up to 100 metres under the applicable IEEE requirements. Actual results still depend on cable condition, connectors, installation and the equipment at both ends.

Can Cat6 run 10 Gigabit Ethernet?

Cat6 can support 10GBASE-T on shorter qualified channels, commonly up to about 55 metres depending on alien crosstalk and installation conditions. For a new 10 Gigabit copper run up to 100 metres, Cat6A is the safer standards-based choice.

Is Cat6A worth buying for a home?

Cat6A is useful for new in-wall runs when you want 10 Gigabit capability over the full structured-cabling distance or need additional headroom for demanding access points and PoE. For short ordinary gigabit connections, Cat5e or Cat6 may already be sufficient.

What is the maximum Ethernet cable length?

For common balanced copper Ethernet structured cabling, the familiar channel limit is 100 metres, including the permitted patch-cord allowance. Some higher-speed applications, such as 25GBASE-T and 40GBASE-T over Cat8, have a shorter 30-metre channel limit.

Does a higher MHz rating mean faster internet?

No. MHz describes the frequency range for which a cabling category is specified. It is not the internet speed. The Ethernet ports, link standard, cable quality, network equipment and internet plan all affect throughput.

What is the difference between UTP and shielded Ethernet cable?

UTP has no overall conductive shield, while shielded designs add foil or braid around pairs or the cable. Shielding can help in suitable electrically noisy environments, but it must be installed and bonded correctly. It is not automatically better for every home.

Can Ethernet cable carry power to a device?

Yes. Power over Ethernet sends data and electrical power over compatible twisted-pair cabling to devices such as access points, cameras and phones. The switch or injector, powered device, cable construction, bundle temperature and applicable PoE standard must all be compatible.

Are flat Ethernet cables as good as round cables?

A flat cable is not automatically unsuitable, but shape alone does not prove category compliance. Check the manufacturer's documented category, conductor material, gauge, construction and test claims. Avoid assuming a thin or flat cable meets a high-category rating.

How do I know if an Ethernet cable is faulty?

Check link negotiation, try a known-good cable and port, inspect connectors, and run a wire-map test. For installed structured cabling, a certification test against the required category or class is more meaningful than a simple continuity check.
