Is Public Wi-Fi Safe? Security Risks, VPNs, Fake Hotspots and Practical Protection

Learn how public Wi-Fi risks work, what HTTPS and VPNs protect, how to spot fake hotspots, and how to use hotel, airport and café networks more safely.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 27 November 2026 · Updated 27 November 2026

Public Wi-Fi makes it easy to get online while travelling or working away from home. The convenience can hide an important detail: a shared hotspot is a network you do not control. That does not mean every public connection is dangerous, or that strangers can automatically read every message. It means you should understand what the network can see, what modern encryption protects, and which practical steps reduce avoidable exposure.

The short answer: public Wi-Fi can be useful, but treat it as an untrusted network

Public Wi-Fi is available in airports, hotels, cafés, libraries, conference centres, transport hubs and many other shared spaces. It is convenient when mobile coverage is weak or a device needs a larger connection, but the person operating the hotspot may not be the person who owns the building, and the network may be shared by hundreds of strangers.

Connecting to a public hotspot does not automatically mean that someone can read every message or password you send. Modern websites and apps commonly use HTTPS and other encrypted protocols. Those protections are important. At the same time, a shared network can expose devices to fake access points, misleading sign-in pages, insecure local services, traffic manipulation attempts and privacy risks. The actual risk depends on the network configuration, the device and the services being used.

A practical approach is to use mobile data or a personal hotspot for sensitive tasks when that is convenient. If public Wi-Fi is needed, verify the network name with the venue, keep device software current, use encrypted websites and apps, avoid accepting unexpected certificates or installing unknown profiles, and use an organisation-approved VPN for work access. These steps reduce risk; no single setting makes an unknown network completely safe.

What happens when you join a public hotspot?

When a device joins Wi-Fi, it associates with a nearby access point and negotiates the wireless security method, if one is required. It then typically obtains network settings such as an IP address, default gateway and DNS resolver. The gateway forwards traffic toward the internet. A venue may also use a captive portal: a web page that asks the visitor to accept terms, enter a room number, provide a voucher or complete another access step before general internet access is allowed.

On an open network without link-layer encryption, nearby people with suitable equipment may be able to observe some local wireless traffic. Modern application encryption makes the contents of most correctly configured HTTPS sessions unreadable to a passive observer, but metadata and connection patterns may still be visible. The hotspot operator can also observe information such as the device's network activity and the destinations it connects to, subject to the protocols and privacy protections in use.

A password on the Wi-Fi network is not the same as a private connection. In a shared password network, many guests may know the same passphrase. Depending on the security mode and implementation, that password may not prevent other guests from attempting local network discovery or attacks. Enterprise authentication and modern encryption can provide stronger separation, but a user cannot infer the security design just from a printed password at reception.

The main public Wi-Fi risks

An evil twin is a fake access point designed to look like a legitimate network. An attacker may copy the venue's network name, use a similar name, or position a stronger signal near the real hotspot. A device configured to join familiar networks automatically may connect without the user noticing. The fake network can then attempt to redirect users, present a counterfeit portal or monitor traffic that is not protected by end-to-end encryption.

Traffic interception and manipulation are also concerns. An attacker who controls or can influence the network path may attempt to redirect insecure connections, interfere with DNS responses, or exploit services that trust the local network. Correctly implemented HTTPS makes it much harder to read or modify the contents of a protected web session, but it does not make every app, protocol or device service safe.

Local network exposure matters too. Some devices share files, printers, media libraries or remote-control services with other devices on the same network. If those services are reachable and insufficiently protected, a nearby user may be able to probe them. Public networks should be treated as untrusted: turn off unnecessary sharing, use a public-network firewall profile where available, and avoid exposing administrative interfaces.

Captive portal impersonation can be used to collect credentials or payment details. A real venue portal may ask for limited information, but a page that unexpectedly requests an email password, banking login, identity document or card security code deserves suspicion. Confirm unusual requests with staff rather than entering sensitive credentials into a page simply because it appeared after connecting.

Privacy is another issue. A hotspot operator may collect device identifiers, connection times, bandwidth usage and browsing-related metadata. Other users may see some local network information depending on isolation settings. A VPN can reduce what the local network can learn about internet destinations, but the VPN provider becomes an important party in the trust relationship.

What HTTPS protects — and what it does not

HTTPS uses Transport Layer Security (TLS) to protect data exchanged between a browser or app and the service it is communicating with. When the certificate is valid and the app correctly verifies it, people operating the Wi-Fi network generally cannot read or silently alter the protected page contents. This is why users should prefer HTTPS sites and apps that use modern encrypted connections.

HTTPS does not hide everything. The network still needs enough information to route packets, and some metadata such as IP addresses, connection timing and traffic volume can remain observable. DNS lookups may also be visible unless the device or application uses an encrypted DNS method. Depending on the protocol and configuration, the hostname may be exposed during connection setup even when page contents are encrypted.

HTTPS also cannot protect a user from voluntarily entering a password into a convincing fake website. A phishing page can have a valid HTTPS certificate for its own deceptive domain. The padlock or secure-connection indicator means the connection to that domain is encrypted; it does not certify that the site is genuine or trustworthy. Check the exact domain before signing in, especially when a portal redirects to a payment or identity page.

Keep browser and operating-system updates current because they contain fixes for certificate handling, protocol vulnerabilities and other security issues. Do not bypass certificate warnings on public Wi-Fi. A warning may have a benign explanation in a managed workplace environment, but on an unknown hotspot it is a reason to stop and investigate rather than click through.

Do you need a VPN on public Wi-Fi?

A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between a device and a VPN endpoint. When configured correctly, it can protect traffic from being read by other people on the local Wi-Fi network and can reduce the hotspot operator's visibility into the destinations and DNS activity carried inside the tunnel. For employees accessing company systems, use the VPN and security controls supplied by the organisation.

A VPN is not a magic shield. It does not make phishing pages legitimate, prevent malware already on a device, secure weak passwords, or stop a user from sharing information with a malicious service. It also does not necessarily protect traffic sent before the tunnel is active, traffic excluded by split tunnelling, or tethered-device traffic that the phone does not route through its VPN. The exact behaviour depends on the operating system and VPN configuration.

Choosing a consumer VPN requires trust. The provider may be able to see connection metadata and, depending on the service and protocol, some traffic information. Read the provider's privacy policy, understand its business model, check independent security assessments where available, and avoid unknown apps that promise free unlimited protection without a credible explanation of how they operate. A poorly chosen VPN can replace one privacy concern with another.

For ordinary browsing, HTTPS already provides strong protection for the contents of most modern web sessions. A VPN can add protection against local network observation and may be required by an employer, but it does not make every public Wi-Fi activity risk-free. Use it as one layer in a broader security approach rather than as a reason to ignore warnings or connect to suspicious networks.

Captive portals: how to sign in without making risky assumptions

A captive portal is the sign-in or terms page shown by some public hotspots before internet access is granted. The device may detect that it has limited connectivity and open a browser or a small captive-portal assistant. This is normal behaviour in hotels, airports and other guest networks, but attackers can imitate the process.

Before entering details, confirm the network name with staff or official venue signage. Be cautious if the portal address looks unrelated to the venue, uses a misspelled brand name, or requests credentials that have nothing to do with providing Wi-Fi access. A hotel may reasonably ask for a room number and surname; it should not need the password to your primary email account.

Some VPN configurations block the portal from opening because the portal must be reached before the VPN tunnel is established. If the device is managed by an employer, follow its approved process rather than disabling security controls. Some platforms provide a captive-portal assistant that handles this initial connection. After authentication, confirm that the VPN reconnects and that normal traffic is protected.

Do not install a certificate, configuration profile, app or browser extension just to obtain guest Wi-Fi unless the venue and your organisation have a verified reason and trusted distribution process. A portal that demands a root certificate or asks you to change device security settings is unusual for ordinary guest access. Stop and ask venue staff or use mobile data instead.

How to recognise a fake or suspicious hotspot

Look for network names that differ slightly from the official name. Attackers may add words such as “Free”, “Guest”, “_WiFi” or a location number. A familiar name is not proof of legitimacy because network names can be copied. Ask a member of staff for the exact SSID and whether the venue uses more than one network.

Be suspicious of repeated portal pop-ups, unexpected redirects, pages that request unrelated passwords, or warnings about certificates and secure connections. A network that claims to require an app download or a special security update before browsing deserves extra scrutiny. Do not install software from a portal merely because the page says it is mandatory.

Turn off automatic joining for public networks you no longer use. Remove old hotel, airport and café networks from the saved list when there is no reason to reconnect. Automatic connection is convenient, but it can cause a device to join a similarly named network in another location. Use the device's setting to ask before joining unknown networks.

Signal strength is not a reliable security indicator. A fake access point may deliberately transmit a stronger signal than the legitimate one. Likewise, a network with a professional-looking portal may still be unsafe. Verification through the venue's official channel is more useful than judging the hotspot by its name, signal bars or appearance.

A safer public Wi-Fi checklist before you connect

Update your operating system, browser and important apps before travelling. Enable automatic security updates where practical. Use a device screen lock and make sure lost-device features are configured. Back up important data so that a lost or compromised device does not become a single point of failure.

Verify the network name with the venue. Prefer a network that uses modern security and authenticated access when available. If the network is open, remember that open does not mean trusted. Avoid joining networks that appear to imitate the venue or that demand unusual software installation.

Set the connection profile to Public on operating systems that provide network profiles. This generally applies stricter discovery and sharing behaviour than a Private or Home profile. Turn off file sharing, printer sharing, nearby sharing and remote administration when they are not needed. Keep the device firewall enabled.

Use HTTPS websites and official apps. Check the domain before entering passwords, payment details or personal information. Use a password manager and multi-factor authentication, preferably phishing-resistant methods for important accounts. A password manager can also help by refusing to autofill credentials on a lookalike domain.

Use an organisation-approved VPN for work access. For personal VPN use, choose a reputable provider and understand its privacy practices. Do not ignore certificate warnings, accept unexpected security prompts or install unknown profiles. If the network behaves strangely, disconnect and switch to mobile data.

Safer use on laptops, phones and tablets

On laptops, set the network to Public when prompted and leave the firewall enabled. Disable network discovery and file sharing on untrusted networks. Avoid exposing remote desktop, development servers, network shares or local admin tools to the public interface. If you need to transfer a file, use a trusted cloud service or a secure, authenticated method rather than an open shared folder.

On phones and tablets, turn off automatic connection to open hotspots if the operating system offers that setting. Review saved networks periodically and remove those you no longer need. Keep Bluetooth and nearby sharing features disabled when not in use, especially if you are in a crowded place. Bluetooth is a separate radio technology, but reducing unnecessary discoverability can limit unwanted connection requests.

Use official app stores and keep apps updated. Avoid installing a profile or app supplied by a public Wi-Fi portal unless it is clearly verified and genuinely necessary. Check which apps have access to local network discovery, location and nearby-device permissions. Public Wi-Fi does not require a device to grant broad permissions to unrelated apps.

Be careful with unattended devices. Physical theft can bypass many network protections if a laptop is left unlocked or a phone is visible on a café table. Lock the screen when stepping away, keep devices with you, and avoid leaving sensitive information displayed where strangers can read it.

Public Wi-Fi for banking, shopping and work

Banking and payment apps should use encrypted connections and additional authentication, but the safest choice for high-impact transactions is often a trusted mobile connection or a known private network. If you must use public Wi-Fi, verify the bank's official app or exact website domain, do not follow login links from unexpected messages, and never disclose one-time passwords or authentication approvals to another person.

Online shopping has similar considerations. Check that the site is genuine, use a trusted payment method, and avoid saving payment details on a shared or borrowed device. HTTPS protects the connection to the site but does not protect against a fake merchant, compromised account or fraudulent checkout page.

For work, follow the employer's remote-access policy. Use company-managed devices and the organisation's VPN, zero-trust access service or approved remote desktop. Do not move confidential files to personal storage simply because the company network is inconvenient. A public hotspot is an unmanaged environment, so company controls and device configuration are important.

If you handle regulated or highly sensitive information, your organisation may prohibit public Wi-Fi entirely or require specific safeguards. Follow those rules even when a hotspot appears secure. A personal VPN is not a substitute for the employer's approved security architecture or incident-reporting process.

Public Wi-Fi, mobile data and personal hotspots compared

Mobile data connects through a cellular provider rather than a shared local Wi-Fi access point. It can be a sensible alternative for sensitive browsing when coverage is good and the data plan permits it. Cellular networks have their own security and privacy considerations, so mobile data should not be treated as invulnerable, but it avoids joining an unknown local wireless network.

A personal hotspot uses a phone or dedicated mobile router to share a cellular connection over Wi-Fi or USB. It gives the owner more control over who can join and allows the hotspot to use a unique password and modern security settings. Use a strong password, restrict access to known devices and turn the hotspot off when it is no longer needed.

Public Wi-Fi may be more practical when cellular coverage is poor, roaming charges are high or the venue provides a managed connection. In those cases, verify the network and apply the safeguards described above. The best choice depends on security requirements, data costs, signal availability, battery life and the trustworthiness of the service.

Enhanced Open, WPA3 and Passpoint: what newer Wi-Fi security features mean

Wi-Fi Enhanced Open uses Opportunistic Wireless Encryption (OWE) to encrypt traffic on open networks without requiring each visitor to enter a shared password. It improves privacy compared with a completely unencrypted open hotspot, but it does not authenticate the user to the network in the way enterprise credentials do. A fake access point can still imitate a public network name, so users must verify the hotspot.

WPA3-Personal improves authentication and protection for personal networks compared with older shared-key methods. WPA3-Enterprise uses enterprise authentication and encryption features for managed environments. Whether a visitor benefits from these standards depends on the access point and device support. Older devices may use transition modes, and the actual configuration should be checked rather than inferred from a product label.

Passpoint, also known as Hotspot 2.0, helps compatible devices discover and authenticate to participating Wi-Fi networks using provisioned credentials. It can make access more seamless and can use enterprise authentication and encryption. It is not the same as a random open hotspot, and it depends on the operator, device and credential profile supporting the service.

These standards improve the security and usability of Wi-Fi when implemented correctly, but they do not eliminate phishing, compromised devices, malicious portals or unsafe applications. Technology can strengthen the connection; users still need to verify the network and protect their accounts.

What to do if you think you connected to a fake hotspot

Disconnect from the network and turn off automatic joining for that SSID. Switch to mobile data or another verified connection. Do not continue using the suspicious portal or install anything it offered. If the device displayed a certificate warning or unexpected security prompt, record what happened without accepting the prompt.

If you entered a password on a page that may have been fake, change it from a trusted connection using the official service website or app. Change the password anywhere else it was reused. Review active sessions and sign out unfamiliar devices. Enable multi-factor authentication and revoke suspicious app permissions or tokens where the service provides that option.

If you entered payment information, contact the card issuer or bank through its official channel and review transactions. If the device installed an unknown app, certificate or configuration profile, seek help from the device vendor or your organisation's IT team. Removing the suspicious item may require more than deleting a browser history entry.

For a work device or business account, report the incident promptly to the organisation's security or IT team. Provide the network name, venue, approximate time, portal address and any prompts you saw. Early reporting can help security teams assess exposure and take action before an account or device is misused.

Frequently asked questions

Is public Wi-Fi safe for online banking?

A modern banking app or HTTPS website encrypts its connection, but a fake hotspot or phishing page can still deceive users. For sensitive transactions, prefer mobile data or a trusted network when practical, verify the official app or domain, and use multi-factor authentication.

Can someone see my passwords on public Wi-Fi?

A passive observer generally cannot read passwords sent through correctly configured HTTPS apps and websites. Risks remain from fake login pages, insecure apps, malware, certificate-warning bypasses and services that do not encrypt traffic properly.

Should I always use a VPN on public Wi-Fi?

A trusted VPN can protect traffic between your device and the VPN endpoint from local network observation. It is useful for work access and some privacy needs, but it does not prevent phishing, malware or unsafe behaviour. Use an organisation-approved VPN for work.

What is an evil twin Wi-Fi network?

An evil twin is a fake access point that imitates a legitimate hotspot, often by copying its network name. A device that joins it may send traffic through an attacker-controlled connection. Verify the exact network name with the venue.

Is password-protected public Wi-Fi secure?

Not necessarily. A shared password may allow many guests to join and does not prove that devices are isolated or that the network uses modern encryption. Ask about the network's security and still treat it as untrusted.

Is HTTPS enough without a VPN?

HTTPS protects the contents of most modern web sessions when certificates are valid and verified. It does not hide all metadata or protect against fake websites, insecure apps or device compromise. A VPN adds a separate encrypted tunnel to its endpoint.

What should I do if a public Wi-Fi login page asks for my email password?

Do not enter it. A venue may need a room number, voucher or terms acceptance, but it should not need your primary email password. Confirm the portal with staff and use mobile data if the request remains suspicious.

Can public Wi-Fi infect my phone or laptop?

Simply joining a network does not automatically mean a device will be infected. Risk can arise from vulnerable software, exposed services, malicious downloads, deceptive portals and unsafe prompts. Keep software updated, use a firewall and avoid installing unknown apps or profiles.

Is a mobile hotspot safer than café Wi-Fi?

A personal hotspot gives you more control over access and avoids joining a shared venue network. It still depends on cellular coverage, device security and a strong hotspot password, but it is often a sensible alternative for sensitive tasks.

What is Wi-Fi Enhanced Open?

Enhanced Open uses Opportunistic Wireless Encryption to encrypt traffic on open Wi-Fi without requiring a shared password. It improves link privacy but does not authenticate the hotspot or prevent an evil twin, so verify the network name.
