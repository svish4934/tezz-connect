What Is Tethering? Hotspot vs USB vs Bluetooth

Tethering lets one device share its internet connection with another. Learn what it means and how Wi-Fi hotspot, USB tethering and Bluetooth tethering differ.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Tethering is the process of sharing one device's internet connection with another. A phone can share its mobile data with a laptop, tablet or another phone through a Wi-Fi hotspot, a USB cable or Bluetooth. The three methods do not deliver identical performance: Wi-Fi is convenient for several devices, USB provides a direct wired link to a compatible computer, and Bluetooth can be useful for modest tasks when the other options are unavailable.

Imagine your home broadband has stopped working just before an online meeting. Your phone still has a mobile signal, so you connect the laptop to the phone and continue working. That connection is tethering. The laptop is not receiving a new broadband service; it is using the phone's existing internet connection, and the data usually comes from the phone's mobile plan.

This guide explains the technology in practical terms, compares Wi-Fi hotspot, USB and Bluetooth, covers Android, iPhone and Windows setup, and shows how to prevent unexpected data use, battery drain and security problems. Menu names differ by device and software version, so use the official instructions for your exact model when a setting is not where this guide describes.

Tethering, hotspot and personal hotspot: what is the difference?

Tethering is the broad term for sharing an internet connection from one device to another. The device that already has internet access is the host or sharing device. The laptop, tablet or phone that uses the shared connection is the client.

A Wi-Fi hotspot is a common way to make that shared connection available. The host creates a small wireless network, and other devices join it using a network name and password. On Android, the feature is often called Wi-Fi hotspot or Hotspot and tethering. Apple calls its feature Personal Hotspot.

USB tethering uses a cable instead of a local Wi-Fi network. Bluetooth tethering uses a paired Bluetooth connection. These are three different ways to carry traffic between the host and client; they do not change the underlying mobile network or guarantee a particular internet speed.

The word hotspot is also used for public Wi-Fi access points in cafés, airports and hotels. A public Wi-Fi hotspot is not necessarily tethering from a phone. In this article, “phone hotspot” means the personal Wi-Fi network created by a phone to share its own connection.

How phone tethering works from end to end

When a phone shares its cellular connection, it performs two jobs. First, it communicates with the mobile network using its SIM or eSIM. Second, it forwards internet traffic from connected devices through that mobile connection. The phone manages the local link as well as the upstream cellular link.

For Wi-Fi tethering, the phone creates a local wireless access point. A laptop joins that network just as it would join a home router. The phone assigns local network settings and routes the laptop's traffic out through the cellular connection. For USB tethering, the phone and computer establish a network link over the cable. For Bluetooth tethering, they establish a network connection over Bluetooth after pairing and enabling the relevant sharing option.

In all three cases, the internet journey still depends on the mobile signal, the carrier's network, congestion, radio conditions, plan restrictions and the destination service. A strong Wi-Fi signal between your laptop and phone does not mean the phone has a strong 4G or 5G signal to the tower. There are two separate links to consider.

Some phones and operating systems can share an existing Wi-Fi connection instead of cellular data, but this capability is device-specific. Google's Android guidance notes that some phones can share a Wi-Fi connection by tethering. Do not assume this works on every model or that a phone can always use Wi-Fi for its own connection and repeat it for other devices.

When a Wi-Fi hotspot is the best choice

Choose Wi-Fi hotspot when you want to connect a laptop without a cable, share the connection with more than one nearby device, or move around while staying connected. It is familiar because the client device connects through its normal Wi-Fi settings. A phone hotspot can be useful during travel, a temporary broadband outage, a short work session or when a tablet has no cellular connection of its own.

Wi-Fi also gives connected devices flexibility. A laptop, tablet and second phone can use the same hotspot if the phone and carrier permit the number of clients. However, each active device can add traffic. A laptop downloading a large update while another device streams video can use the mobile allowance quickly and compete for the available cellular throughput.

The local Wi-Fi connection has its own range and interference limitations. Keep the phone reasonably close to the laptop, avoid placing it behind thick walls or inside a bag, and use the band that gives the best combination of compatibility and stability. Some phones allow selection of a hotspot band or a compatibility mode. A 2.4 GHz network can reach farther and support older devices, while 5 GHz may offer more capacity over shorter distances in a suitable environment. The best option depends on the phone and client capabilities and the surrounding radio environment.

Wi-Fi hotspot can also increase heat and battery use because the phone is running its cellular radio and its local Wi-Fi access point while forwarding traffic. If you are sharing for a long session, place the phone on a hard surface with airflow rather than covering it with bedding or leaving it in direct sun. If the device becomes unusually hot, disconnect and let it cool.

When USB tethering makes more sense

USB tethering is useful when one computer needs a connection and a cable is available. It creates a direct link between the phone and computer, so it avoids the local Wi-Fi radio hop and nearby Wi-Fi interference. It can be convenient for a desk-based work session, particularly if the computer's Wi-Fi adapter is unreliable or the wireless environment is crowded.

A cable connection does not bypass the mobile network. If the phone has weak cellular reception or the carrier is congested, the internet connection can still be slow. USB only changes how the computer communicates with the phone; it cannot create extra signal or capacity from the mobile tower.

Use a data-capable USB cable. Some inexpensive cables are designed only for charging or have damaged data conductors. If the phone charges but the computer does not show a network connection, try a known data cable, another USB port and the phone's tethering settings. Unlock the phone and approve any trust or connection prompt that appears.

Compatibility varies. Google's Android Help says Mac computers cannot use USB tethering from Android phones. Apple's Personal Hotspot instructions describe USB connections to Mac and Windows computers; on Windows, Apple says the Apple Devices app or iTunes for Windows may be required. Check the current instructions for your operating system.

USB may also charge the phone while it shares data, but this depends on the port, cable, power delivery and device. A laptop port may supply limited power, and a phone under heavy cellular use can still lose charge. Do not assume that being plugged in guarantees the battery percentage will rise.

When Bluetooth tethering is useful

Bluetooth tethering is an alternative for a paired device when Wi-Fi and USB are inconvenient or unavailable. It can suit messaging, light browsing or a small amount of background traffic. It is generally not the first choice for large downloads, high-resolution streaming or other bandwidth-heavy work because Bluetooth networking is usually lower throughput than Wi-Fi or a wired USB connection.

Pairing alone does not always enable internet sharing. You normally need to pair the phone and computer, turn on Bluetooth tethering in the phone's hotspot settings, and select the phone as a network connection on the computer if that option is supported. If the computer is paired for audio or file transfer, that does not prove it has established a Bluetooth network connection.

Bluetooth is designed for short-range, low-power communication, but the real battery impact depends on the devices, radio conditions and traffic. It is not accurate to promise that Bluetooth tethering always uses less battery than Wi-Fi. If the task needs more bandwidth, the longer time spent transferring data can also affect total energy use.

How to turn on a hotspot on Android

Android settings vary between Google Pixel, Samsung Galaxy, OnePlus, OPPO, vivo, Xiaomi, Motorola and other manufacturers. The feature is commonly under Settings, then Network and Internet or Connections, then Hotspot and tethering or Mobile Hotspot. Some phones also provide a Hotspot tile in Quick Settings.

  1. Confirm that mobile data is on and the phone itself can open a website.
  2. Open Settings and find Hotspot and tethering, Mobile Hotspot or the equivalent menu.
  3. Open Wi-Fi hotspot and review the network name, password and security option.
  4. Use a strong password that is not shared with your home Wi-Fi or other accounts.
  5. Turn on the hotspot and wait for the phone to show that it is active.
  6. On the laptop or other client, open Wi-Fi settings, choose the phone's network name and enter the password.
  7. Open a website on the client to confirm that internet access works.

Google's official Android hotspot and tethering guide explains Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and USB methods. It notes that some steps apply only to newer Android versions, and that carriers may restrict or charge extra for tethering. Follow your manufacturer's menu instructions if the labels differ.

Some Android devices can automatically turn off a hotspot when no devices are connected. This can reduce the chance of leaving sharing enabled, but do not rely on it as your only safeguard. Check the hotspot status when you finish.

How to turn on Personal Hotspot on iPhone

On an iPhone with a supported cellular plan, open Settings and choose Personal Hotspot. Depending on the iOS version and device configuration, the option may also appear under Settings, then Mobile or Cellular. Turn on Allow Others to Join and check the Wi-Fi password displayed on the screen.

On the laptop or tablet, open Wi-Fi settings, select the iPhone's name and enter the password. Keep the Personal Hotspot settings screen available if the client does not discover the network immediately. Apple also supports USB and Bluetooth connection methods. For USB with Windows, Apple instructs users to install the Apple Devices app or iTunes for Windows, connect the cable, allow the accessory if prompted and trust the computer on the iPhone.

Apple's official Connect to the Personal Hotspot of an iPhone or iPad page provides current Wi-Fi, USB and Bluetooth steps. Apple notes that Personal Hotspot availability and the number of connected devices can depend on the carrier and iPhone model. Some mobile operators may apply additional charges or restrictions.

If an older or less compatible client cannot connect, Apple's Maximise Compatibility setting may help in some situations, but Apple warns that enabling it can reduce performance for connected devices. Try it as a compatibility troubleshooting step rather than assuming it will make every connection faster.

How to use a Windows laptop as a hotspot

Tethering can work in the opposite direction too. A Windows computer can share an internet connection from Wi-Fi, Ethernet or cellular data with nearby devices. This is useful when a laptop is connected to a wired network and needs to share that connection temporarily, or when the computer itself has cellular connectivity.

In Windows 11, open Settings, choose Network and internet, then Mobile hotspot. Select the connection to share and choose how to share it. Microsoft documents Wi-Fi and Bluetooth sharing options, and allows you to edit the hotspot network name, password and band in the Properties section. Turn on Mobile hotspot, then connect the other device through its Wi-Fi or Bluetooth settings.

Microsoft's Use your Windows device as a mobile hotspot guide includes current setup steps and notes that sharing cellular data uses the data plan associated with that connection. The available options depend on the computer's wireless hardware and Windows version.

Do not confuse Windows mobile hotspot with a phone's personal hotspot. The computer is acting as the sharing device in this case. The source connection might be Ethernet, Wi-Fi or cellular, and the devices that join use the laptop's shared connection.

Does tethering use mobile data—and how much?

When a phone shares its cellular connection, traffic from the connected laptop or tablet normally counts against the phone's mobile data allowance. Tethering does not create a separate free internet connection. It simply allows another device to use the connection the phone already has.

The amount consumed depends on what the connected device does. Sending messages and loading simple web pages may use relatively little data. Video streaming, video meetings, large software updates, cloud backups, game downloads and high-resolution media can use much more. A laptop may also perform background tasks that are easy to overlook because they are not visible in the browser window.

Do not estimate tethering usage from the number of connected devices alone. One laptop performing a large system update can transfer more data than several phones used briefly for messaging. Review the data-usage controls on the phone and the carrier's usage app or website. If the plan has a daily or monthly allowance, check the billing cycle and the provider's fair usage policy.

Some operators limit tethering, require a particular plan or apply different rules to hotspot usage. Google's Android Help explicitly advises users to check with their mobile carrier because some operators limit or charge extra for tethering. Apple's Personal Hotspot guidance also notes that availability can depend on the carrier. Verify the current terms for your SIM or eSIM rather than relying on a generic claim about all Indian mobile plans.

Stop a laptop from consuming your mobile data in the background

One of the most important tethering habits is to tell the laptop that the connection may be metered. A metered connection setting can reduce automatic data use by some Windows services and applications. It does not block every download, so keep an eye on updates and cloud sync.

  • Set the network as metered: in Windows Wi-Fi settings, open the connected hotspot network's properties and enable Metered connection where available.
  • Pause cloud synchronisation: temporarily pause OneDrive or other sync tools if large folders are uploading or downloading.
  • Schedule operating-system updates: avoid starting a large update over a limited mobile allowance unless it is urgent.
  • Check application updates: game launchers, creative applications and app stores may download large files automatically.
  • Reduce video quality: select a lower streaming quality when using a limited mobile plan.
  • Disable automatic backups temporarily: check photo and file backup settings on all connected devices.
  • Disconnect idle devices: a device may continue background traffic even when its screen is locked.

When you return to unlimited or fixed broadband, review any paused updates and backups so that important security updates are not forgotten. The goal is to control when large transfers happen, not to leave devices permanently out of date.

Why a hotspot can feel slower than the phone itself

A phone may load websites quickly while a laptop connected to its hotspot feels sluggish. Several factors can explain the difference. The phone may have a better antenna position, the laptop may be farther away, the hotspot band may be congested, the phone may be warm, or the laptop may be downloading data in the background. A VPN, proxy or security application on either device can also affect performance.

Remember that tethering has two links: the cellular connection from the phone to the mobile network and the local connection from the phone to the laptop. Test each link separately where possible. First open a website directly on the phone with Wi-Fi disabled to confirm the mobile connection. Then connect the laptop and test close to the phone. If the phone works well but the laptop does not, focus on the hotspot link, client settings or laptop background traffic.

Move the phone to a location with better cellular reception, such as near a window, but keep it cool and secure. Keep the laptop close enough for a stable local connection. If Wi-Fi is crowded, try another supported hotspot band. If a cable is available and both devices support it, compare USB tethering. Run more than one test and compare at similar times; a single speed test is not a complete diagnosis.

A speed test can also use a noticeable amount of data, especially if run repeatedly. If the mobile allowance is limited, use a small number of tests and avoid unnecessary repeated downloads.

Why your hotspot keeps disconnecting

Frequent disconnections may be caused by a power-saving setting, automatic hotspot timeout, weak local Wi-Fi, phone overheating, low battery, a carrier interruption or a client device that sleeps its wireless adapter. Some phones turn off the hotspot when no device is connected for a period of time. Others have compatibility options that change the local Wi-Fi behaviour.

Start with a simple isolation routine:

  1. Confirm that the phone itself still has mobile internet when the client disconnects.
  2. Keep the phone and client close together and temporarily remove obstacles.
  3. Check whether the hotspot automatically turns off when idle.
  4. Disable aggressive battery saver temporarily and observe whether the problem changes.
  5. Restart the hotspot and reconnect the client using the saved password.
  6. Try another client device to see whether the fault follows the phone or stays with the laptop.
  7. Test USB tethering if supported to distinguish a local Wi-Fi problem from a mobile-network problem.

Change one variable at a time and note the result. Resetting all network settings immediately can remove saved Wi-Fi networks and Bluetooth pairings, so reserve that step for later and understand what will be erased before proceeding.

Hotspot security: protect your data and devices

A personal hotspot is a small access point. Anyone who can join it may be able to use your mobile data, and an unknown connected device may create a privacy or security risk. Keep hotspot access limited to people and devices you trust.

  • Use a strong password that is difficult to guess and not reused from another network.
  • Use the strongest security mode supported by both host and client devices.
  • Do not disable hotspot security simply to make joining easier.
  • Check the connected-device list and remove devices you do not recognise.
  • Change the password if you shared it widely or suspect someone else has it.
  • Turn off the hotspot when it is no longer needed.
  • Keep the phone operating system updated and use a screen lock.
  • Avoid sharing the hotspot password publicly or in an open group chat.

Bluetooth tethering also requires care. Pair with a device you recognise, check the pairing code when prompted and remove old pairings that you no longer use. Pairing is not the same as granting internet access, but leaving unnecessary sharing features enabled increases the chance of accidental connections.

When travelling, do not assume that a phone hotspot is automatically safer than every public Wi-Fi network. It is a connection you control, but your connected devices still need current security updates, encrypted websites and sensible account protection.

Battery, heat and long tethering sessions

Tethering can use more battery than ordinary phone use because the device maintains its cellular connection and also manages the local Wi-Fi, USB or Bluetooth link. The workload increases when multiple clients transfer data or the phone operates in a weak-signal area. In poor reception, the cellular radio may work harder to maintain the connection.

For a long session, keep the phone on a stable surface with ventilation. Avoid covering it, leaving it in direct sunlight or placing it next to a heat source. If it becomes unusually hot, stop sharing and allow it to cool. A USB connection may supply power, but the charging rate and heat depend on the computer port, cable and phone. Wireless charging during heavy tethering may add more heat and is not always the best choice.

Turn off tethering when you finish. If your phone supports an automatic hotspot timeout, it can help, but manually checking the hotspot status is still good practice. If the phone is your only way to receive calls or emergency alerts, consider whether a long tethering session will leave enough battery for other essential use.

Can you tether while roaming?

Technically, a phone may be able to share a roaming mobile connection, but whether it works and what it costs depend on the carrier, roaming plan, destination and device. Data used by a connected laptop can count toward the roaming data allowance just like data used directly on the phone. Some plans may have separate hotspot rules or restrictions.

Before travelling, check the roaming package's data allowance, fair usage terms, tethering rules, speed limits and charges outside the included quota. Turn off automatic updates, cloud backup and high-quality streaming before connecting a laptop. A device that silently downloads a large update can consume a travel allowance quickly.

Do not assume that a domestic “unlimited” plan includes unlimited roaming data or unrestricted hotspot use abroad. Read the plan terms for the country you are visiting and monitor usage while travelling.

Phone hotspot versus a dedicated 4G or 5G router

A phone hotspot is convenient because you already carry the device and can turn sharing on when needed. It is suitable for temporary access, travel and a small number of connected devices, provided the phone, plan and signal support the workload.

A dedicated mobile router may be a better option when several people need regular internet access, when the connection must stay on for long periods, or when the household wants to position the cellular modem in a location with better reception. Some routers support external antennas, Ethernet ports, more client devices or network-management features that a phone does not provide. These capabilities vary by model and should be checked before purchase.

A dedicated router is not automatically faster. It still depends on the same mobile operator coverage, network congestion, supported frequency bands, SIM plan and location. Before buying equipment, test the mobile network at the place where it will be used, check the operator's coverage information and compare the device's supported bands with the carrier's network.

If you only need internet for a short meeting or occasional laptop session, a phone hotspot may be enough. If the phone becomes hot, drains quickly, disconnects devices or is needed for other daily tasks, a dedicated router may offer a more convenient arrangement.

Troubleshooting: hotspot is on but the laptop has no internet

Work through the following checks in order instead of changing every setting at once:

  1. Check mobile data on the phone. Turn off Wi-Fi on the phone and load a website using cellular data. If the phone itself has no internet, troubleshoot mobile coverage, data balance, APN or carrier service first.
  2. Check the plan. Confirm that tethering is allowed and that the data quota has not been exhausted or reduced by a fair usage rule.
  3. Confirm the correct network. Make sure the laptop is connected to the phone's hotspot, not a saved public or old Wi-Fi network.
  4. Re-enter the password. If you recently changed the hotspot password, remove the saved network on the laptop and join again.
  5. Check connected-device limits. Disconnect clients you no longer need and check the phone's hotspot settings.
  6. Temporarily disable VPN or proxy settings. A VPN or proxy can make a connection appear broken even when the hotspot itself is working. Re-enable protection after the test.
  7. Restart both devices. Restarting can clear a temporary networking fault, but it will not fix a carrier outage or unsupported plan.
  8. Try another connection method. If Wi-Fi hotspot fails, compare USB or Bluetooth if both devices support them.

If the phone has internet but no client device can use the hotspot, capture the phone model, operating-system version, carrier, error message and whether the issue occurs over Wi-Fi, USB and Bluetooth. That information helps the device maker or carrier identify a compatibility or account restriction.

Troubleshooting: USB tethering is missing or does not work

USB tethering may be unavailable because the device does not support it, the carrier or operating system restricts it, or the phone is connected using a charge-only cable. On Android, connect the phone to the computer with a known data cable, unlock it and open Hotspot and tethering settings. The USB tethering control may only become available after a supported USB connection is detected.

Try another USB port and cable. Avoid a damaged hub or adapter while troubleshooting. Check whether the computer recognises the phone and whether a driver or Apple software component is required. On some systems, the phone may ask you to trust the computer or allow an accessory connection.

Google's Android Help specifically states that Mac computers cannot use USB tethering from Android phones. If you are using a Mac with an Android phone, use Wi-Fi or Bluetooth if supported, or check the manufacturer's current documentation for alternatives. For an iPhone connected to Windows, Apple's instructions say Apple Devices or iTunes for Windows may be required.

Troubleshooting: Bluetooth is paired but there is no internet

Bluetooth pairing creates a relationship between devices, but it does not necessarily turn on tethering. On the phone, open hotspot and tethering settings and enable Bluetooth tethering. On the computer, open its network settings and select the paired phone's internet-access option if available. The labels differ across operating systems.

If the network option is missing, check that both devices support Bluetooth networking, not merely Bluetooth audio or file transfer. Remove the pairing on both devices and pair again if the connection is stale. Keep the devices close together during setup. If Bluetooth networking is not supported by the computer or phone, use Wi-Fi hotspot or USB instead.

How to run a fair tethering speed test

If you want to compare Wi-Fi hotspot and USB tethering, keep the test conditions as similar as possible. Use the same phone, SIM, location, client computer, test service and approximate time. Do not compare a USB test performed beside a window in the morning with a Wi-Fi test in a different room during evening congestion.

  1. Check that the phone itself has mobile internet and note the signal conditions.
  2. Pause large downloads, backups and streaming on other connected devices.
  3. Connect the laptop by Wi-Fi hotspot and run one or two speed tests.
  4. Record download speed, upload speed, latency and the time of each test.
  5. Disconnect Wi-Fi tethering and connect by USB if supported.
  6. Repeat the same test without moving the phone or changing the mobile network conditions.
  7. Compare stability and practical performance, not only the highest single download number.

Speed-test results can vary because the mobile network, test server, routing, device load and radio conditions change. Repeated testing consumes data. If your plan is limited, do only the tests needed to answer the question.

Data-saving checklist before you connect a laptop

  • Check the remaining mobile data and the plan's hotspot terms.
  • Set the laptop's hotspot network as metered where supported.
  • Pause cloud synchronisation and automatic photo backups if they are not urgent.
  • Close streaming services and stop background video playback.
  • Pause large game and application downloads.
  • Choose lower video quality for meetings or entertainment when necessary.
  • Disconnect devices that do not need internet access.
  • Check the phone's data-usage screen after a longer session.
  • Turn tethering off when finished.

For a useful estimate, record the phone's data usage immediately before and after a known work session. Repeat the observation for a few different activities, such as email, a video call and a software update. This gives you a better picture of your own workload than a generic claim about how much data tethering consumes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between tethering and a hotspot?

Tethering is the general practice of sharing one device's internet connection with another. A hotspot is commonly the Wi-Fi network or feature used to make that sharing available to nearby devices.

Is USB tethering faster than a Wi-Fi hotspot?

USB can provide a stable direct connection to one compatible computer and avoids the local wireless hop, but actual speed depends on the phone, cable, USB port, cellular network and computer. It is not guaranteed to be faster in every setup.

Does tethering use my mobile data?

When a phone shares its cellular connection, internet traffic from connected devices normally uses the phone's mobile data allowance. Check your carrier's plan terms and usage meter.

Does USB tethering charge my phone?

A USB connection may charge the phone while tethering, depending on the computer port, cable and device power settings. Charging and data transfer are separate capabilities, and some cables support charging only.

Why is my hotspot connected but there is no internet?

Check that mobile data works on the phone itself, the plan permits tethering, the connected device has no conflicting VPN or proxy, and the hotspot has not reached a device limit. Restarting both devices and reconnecting can help isolate a temporary fault.

Is Bluetooth tethering better for battery?

Bluetooth is designed for low-power short-range communication and can be useful for light tasks, but battery impact depends on the phone, radio state, signal conditions and workload. Do not assume it will always use less energy than every Wi-Fi setup.

Can I share Wi-Fi from my phone instead of mobile data?

Some Android phones and models support sharing an existing Wi-Fi connection, but behaviour varies by manufacturer and software. Check the device's official documentation; do not assume every phone can repeat a Wi-Fi connection while acting as a hotspot.

How many devices can connect to a phone hotspot?

The limit depends on the phone model, operating system and mobile operator. Google documents support for up to 10 devices on many Android phones, but this is not universal; check your device settings and carrier.

Is a phone hotspot safe to use?

Use a strong unique password, modern hotspot security when available, keep the phone updated, share access only with trusted people and turn the hotspot off when finished. Avoid leaving an open hotspot available to nearby strangers.

Why does my laptop use so much data when tethered?

Laptops may download operating-system updates, synchronise cloud files, load high-resolution media, update applications or back up data automatically. Mark the connection as metered where supported and pause non-essential background transfers.
