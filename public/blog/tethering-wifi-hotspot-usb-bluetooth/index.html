Tethering Explained: Hotspot vs USB vs Bluetooth

What does tethering mean? Compare phone hotspot, USB and Bluetooth sharing, including speed, battery use and when each option makes sense.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Your laptop is ready for a video call, but the Wi-Fi has gone down. If your phone still has mobile data, you may be able to get the laptop online by sharing the phone’s connection. That is tethering. You can do it over Wi-Fi, USB or Bluetooth, and each method has a different trade-off.

First, what do “tethering” and “hotspot” mean?

Tethering is the broad term for letting one device use another device’s internet connection. A mobile hotspot is the familiar Wi-Fi version: your phone creates a small wireless network, and other devices join it with a password. People often use the two terms as if they mean exactly the same thing, but hotspot is just one way to tether.

Wi-Fi hotspot: easiest when more than one device needs access

Turn on the hotspot, find its network name on your laptop or tablet, and enter the password. There is no cable to find and several devices can usually connect. The downside is that the phone has to keep its Wi-Fi radio working while sharing mobile data. Video calls, laptop updates and cloud backups can drain the phone’s battery and use more data than you expected.

Before starting, check the hotspot password and look at your mobile plan. Some operators limit tethering or charge for it, even when ordinary phone data is included.

USB: useful for a one-computer connection

Connect the phone to a computer with a cable that supports data, then enable USB tethering in the phone’s settings. This avoids creating a Wi-Fi hotspot and can be handy if the computer’s wireless adapter is unavailable. A connected phone may charge, but do not count on it: power delivery depends on the cable, port and devices.

There is an important compatibility catch: Google says Android phones cannot provide USB tethering to Mac computers. Check the instructions for your own phone and computer before relying on this method.

Bluetooth: for lighter use, not a big download

Bluetooth tethering starts with pairing the two devices. It can be convenient when Wi-Fi is not an option, but it is generally a poor choice for moving large files or streaming high-quality video. Compatibility and setup can vary, and some computer combinations do not support it.

Which one should you use?

  • Wi-Fi hotspot: a good default for a laptop and other nearby devices.
  • USB: worth trying for one compatible computer, especially when you want a direct cable connection.
  • Bluetooth: a fallback for modest tasks when both devices support it.

Whichever method you choose, tethering does not create extra data. It simply moves your phone’s connection to another device. A laptop can quietly use a lot of it, so pause automatic updates and cloud syncing if your allowance is limited. Turn sharing off when you finish, and use a password rather than leaving a hotspot open.
