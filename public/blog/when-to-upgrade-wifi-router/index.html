Broadband & ISP Insights

When Should You Upgrade Your Wi-Fi Router? A Practical Decision Guide

Learn when to replace a Wi-Fi router, when to fix placement or settings, how to check security support, and what Indian homes should compare before upgrading.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

A Wi-Fi router does not become obsolete on a fixed birthday. Some routers remain suitable for years because they receive security updates and meet the needs of the household. Others deserve replacement sooner because the manufacturer has stopped supporting them, the hardware is failing, or a specific limitation is disrupting everyday use. The useful question is not simply “How old is my router?” It is “What problem am I trying to solve, and is this router actually the cause?”

This guide uses a practical decision method: check security support first, separate internet-service problems from Wi-Fi problems, identify whether the issue is coverage or capacity, and compare the capabilities of the router with the devices and broadband plan in your home. It is written for home users in India, where a household may use an ISP-provided optical network terminal (ONT), a combined modem-router, a separate router, or a mesh system.

The short answer: when is it time to replace a Wi-Fi router?

Consider replacing your router when its manufacturer no longer provides security updates, when it cannot be secured with modern encryption, when hardware faults cause repeated outages, or when a verified hardware limitation prevents it from meeting your needs. A router upgrade may also make sense when you have added many devices, moved to a faster broadband plan, or need more reliable coverage across several rooms and floors.

Do not replace it just because a shop advertises Wi-Fi 6, Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7. A newer wireless standard can bring useful improvements, but the benefit depends on compatible client devices, the router’s actual design, local radio conditions, the wired connection and the internet plan. A new router cannot increase the capacity of an ISP connection that is already the bottleneck.

Use this order of priority:

  1. Security: Is the exact model and hardware revision still receiving firmware and security updates?
  2. Reliability: Does the router itself repeatedly freeze, reboot or lose its WAN connection?
  3. Measured limitation: Can you demonstrate a speed, port, capacity or compatibility limit?
  4. Coverage: Is the problem confined to distant rooms or blocked areas that might be fixed by placement, an access point or mesh?
  5. Value: Will a replacement solve the identified issue at a reasonable total cost?

1. Check whether the router is still supported

Security support is the strongest reason to replace an otherwise functional router. A router sits between your home devices and the wider internet. Its firmware controls network services, wireless security, remote administration and other functions. If a manufacturer stops publishing fixes for vulnerabilities, the device can remain connected while becoming progressively harder to maintain safely.

Find the exact model number and hardware revision. The model may appear on a label underneath the router, on its original packaging, or in the manufacturer’s mobile app or administration page. Do not rely only on a family name printed on the front. One product family can have several hardware revisions, regional variants and firmware branches.

Visit the manufacturer’s official support site and search for that exact model. Look for an end-of-life, end-of-service or end-of-support notice. Check the latest firmware release date, the supported hardware revision, the security advisory history and whether the device is eligible for automatic updates. A router may receive occasional firmware updates without having a clearly stated end date, so ask the manufacturer or ISP for written clarification if the status is uncertain.

There is no universal age at which every router becomes unsafe. Support periods vary by manufacturer, model, region and product category. A device bought recently may already be an older model, while a well-supported model may continue to receive fixes. The absence of a recent feature update does not automatically prove that security support has ended; equally, the fact that the Wi-Fi still works does not prove that the device is supported.

When checking update status, distinguish these situations:

  • Supported with current updates: Keep it updated and review its security settings.
  • Supported, but updates are manual: Set a reminder to check the official support page and install verified updates.
  • Support status unclear: Ask the manufacturer or ISP to confirm the exact hardware revision and security-update policy.
  • Officially end of service: Plan a replacement, particularly if the router is exposed directly to the internet and cannot receive security fixes.
  • Known serious vulnerability with no available patch: Follow the manufacturer’s mitigation advice and prioritise replacement if no safe fix exists.

Never install firmware from an unofficial download site simply because it appears newer. Firmware is privileged software. A file for the wrong model or revision can disable the device or introduce security risks. Use the manufacturer’s official instructions and do not interrupt power during an update.

2. Check Wi-Fi security before judging speed

Wireless security is not a speed feature. It protects the network from unauthorised access and helps protect traffic transmitted over the air. Review the router’s security mode and use the strongest mode supported by the router and all devices that need to connect.

WPA3-Personal is a modern security option. Some homes need a transitional WPA2/WPA3 mode to support older devices. If WPA3 is unavailable, WPA2-Personal with AES is preferable to obsolete protocols. Avoid WEP, original WPA, TKIP and open networks for normal private use. Apple’s published router recommendations describe WPA3 and WPA2/WPA3 transitional settings and identify older modes that should be avoided.

Also change the router’s default administrator password. This is different from the Wi-Fi password: the administrator password controls configuration. Use a unique, strong password for each. Keep remote administration disabled unless you have a specific need and understand how to secure it. Review guest-network settings and disable features you do not use, following the manufacturer’s guidance rather than changing unfamiliar options at random.

If your existing router supports current firmware and an appropriate security mode, a newer Wi-Fi generation is not automatically a security upgrade. Conversely, a router that cannot be updated or configured securely may need replacement even if its download speeds seem acceptable. Security support and wireless performance are separate questions and should be evaluated separately.

3. Find out whether the router or the broadband connection is slow

Before buying equipment, establish where the slowdown occurs. A household often describes every connectivity problem as “slow Wi-Fi”, even when the issue is the ISP connection, a damaged fibre patch cord, a congested network, a faulty ONT, a weak phone signal or one application’s server.

Start with a wired comparison if your equipment permits it. Connect a laptop or desktop to a LAN port using a suitable Ethernet cable, temporarily disconnect other heavy network activity, and run more than one speed test. Compare the result with the subscribed plan and the ISP’s stated conditions. If wired performance is also poor, a new wireless router may not solve the problem. Contact the ISP and ask it to check the line, ONT, provisioning, signal levels where applicable, and any service fault.

If wired performance is healthy but Wi-Fi is slow, investigate the wireless side. Test in the same room as the router, then repeat in the room where the problem occurs. Keep the same device, test service and approximate time where possible. Note the connection band if the device exposes it. A phone connected over 2.4 GHz should not be compared casually with a laptop connected over 5 GHz and treated as a controlled test.

Run tests at different times. A single result is a snapshot, not a diagnosis. If performance falls only during evening hours and wired tests also fall, ISP-side congestion or shared service capacity may be involved. If only one room is affected, coverage or interference is more likely. If only one phone is affected, check that phone before replacing network equipment.

Record download, upload, latency and, where available, jitter and packet loss. Download speed alone does not describe the full experience. Video calls and interactive games can feel poor when latency varies or packets are lost, even when a speed test reports a high download number.

4. Separate coverage problems from router performance problems

Coverage describes where a usable wireless signal reaches. Performance describes how well the network works once a device is connected. A router may deliver strong throughput beside it but fail to reach a bedroom behind several concrete walls. That is a coverage problem; buying a faster router may or may not fix it.

Radio signals weaken as they pass through walls, floors, doors, furniture and other materials. Dense masonry, reinforced concrete, metal surfaces and water-containing objects can be particularly challenging. The layout of an Indian apartment, independent house or multi-storey home can therefore matter more than the router’s headline speed rating.

Try a placement test before replacing hardware. Put the router in an open, elevated position as centrally as the broadband entry point and cabling allow. Avoid hiding it inside a closed cabinet, behind a television, on the floor or beside large metal objects. Keep it away from equipment that may cause interference. Google’s Wi-Fi placement guidance similarly recommends an open, raised and central position, while noting that the best arrangement depends on the home’s layout and construction.

Repeat the same tests after moving the router. Change one factor at a time and note the result. If coverage improves substantially, the original problem may have been placement rather than age or capability. If the broadband cable enters at one corner of the home, moving the main router may require a longer cable or a wired access point; do not stretch or sharply bend fibre cabling.

For a single dead zone, a properly positioned access point connected by Ethernet can be a strong solution. A mesh system may be useful for multiple rooms or floors when running Ethernet is impractical. A wireless mesh node must still receive a good signal from the main router or another node. Placing a node inside the dead zone gives it a weak connection to repeat, so position it partway between the main router and the area needing coverage.

A basic extender can help in a limited area, but performance and roaming behaviour vary. Some extenders use a shared wireless channel for both the connection back to the router and the client device, which can reduce available throughput. Do not assume that adding more repeaters always improves the network. More devices also mean more configuration and potential interference.

5. Understand 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz bands

Many modern home routers support more than one frequency band. The 2.4 GHz band generally reaches farther and passes through some obstacles more effectively, but it has fewer non-overlapping channel choices and is commonly shared with many household devices. The 5 GHz band can provide higher practical throughput and more channel options in many environments, but its coverage is often shorter through walls. The 6 GHz band, where permitted and supported, adds spectrum for compatible Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 devices, but it does not make an older phone capable of connecting to 6 GHz.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. Actual results depend on local regulations, channel availability, router configuration, client hardware, distance, interference and building materials. A device may select a band automatically, and the user may not see which one it chose. Check the router or device interface before drawing conclusions.

Do not buy a tri-band router simply because three bands sound faster. Ask what the additional band will do in your home. It may be useful for compatible devices or for a mesh system’s wireless backhaul, but it cannot improve an old phone’s radio capabilities. A dual-band router with good placement may be a better value for a small flat with a modest number of devices.

In India, confirm that the exact model is intended for the Indian market and supports the locally permitted frequency and channel settings. Imported variants may have different firmware, warranty coverage, power adapters, regulatory configuration or support arrangements. Do not assume every advertised 6 GHz feature is available in every country or on every regional model.

6. Decide whether the Wi-Fi generation matters to your devices

Wi-Fi 5, Wi-Fi 6, Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 describe generations of wireless networking capabilities. A newer generation can improve efficiency, capacity or peak link rates under suitable conditions. But the router and client device must support the relevant features, and the network environment must allow them to be used.

Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) introduced features designed to use wireless airtime more efficiently in busy environments. Wi-Fi 6E extends Wi-Fi 6 operation into the 6 GHz band where permitted. Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) adds further capabilities, including wider channels and multi-link operation on supported equipment. These labels do not promise a particular internet speed. The broadband plan, Ethernet ports, client radio, channel conditions and application server still matter.

Make a simple inventory of the devices that matter most: work laptop, primary phones, television, gaming console, security cameras and smart-home hubs. Check their supported Wi-Fi generation and band in the device specifications. If most devices are older and your current router is stable and supported, a premium Wi-Fi 7 router may provide little immediate benefit. If you are replacing an unsupported router anyway, selecting a current standard with a documented support commitment may be sensible, provided the price and features fit your requirements.

Compatibility is usually designed to allow newer routers to connect to older Wi-Fi clients, but the connection uses capabilities shared by both ends. An older client does not become Wi-Fi 7 merely because the router is Wi-Fi 7. Likewise, a newer phone may connect to an older router using an older standard. The weakest or least capable part of a particular link can limit that connection.

7. Check Ethernet ports and the wired side of the router

Wireless specifications get attention, but wired ports can be just as important. Check the speed and number of LAN ports, the WAN port capability, and whether the router supports the broadband connection type supplied by your ISP. A router with only 100 Mbps Ethernet ports can become a bottleneck on a faster broadband plan, even if its Wi-Fi marketing claims a much larger theoretical rate.

Gigabit Ethernet ports are sufficient for many home broadband plans. Faster multi-gigabit ports may matter if the ISP provides a service above 1 Gbps, if a local network transfers large files between compatible devices, or if a NAS or workstation needs higher wired throughput. The port speed is only one part of the chain: the ONT, cable, connected device and ISP plan must also support the higher rate.

Check whether the router has the correct WAN interface. Some ISP installations use a separate ONT with Ethernet output; others use an integrated gateway or a device that needs particular VLAN, PPPoE or ISP provisioning settings. Do not purchase a router based only on the number of antennas. Confirm that it can work with your provider’s installation and that the ISP will share the required configuration or support the device.

Ask whether the ISP-supplied unit can be placed in bridge mode or whether the new router can operate behind it without creating a double-NAT problem for applications that need inbound connectivity. These are configuration questions, not universal requirements. If you are unsure, ask the ISP to confirm the supported topology before disconnecting the existing device.

8. Recognise signs of failing hardware

Repeated disconnections can be a symptom of hardware failure, but they are not proof by themselves. A failing power adapter, overheating, loose Ethernet connector, damaged cable, unstable electrical supply or ISP line fault can produce similar symptoms. Diagnose the simplest possibilities before replacing the entire router.

Look for a pattern. Does the router restart without anyone touching it? Do all wireless and wired devices lose access at the same time? Does the power light cycle as if the device is rebooting? Does the problem occur after the unit becomes hot or after a heavy workload? Do the event logs show repeated restarts or WAN reconnections? These clues help separate a router problem from an internet service interruption.

Place the router where its ventilation openings are not blocked. Do not cover it with fabric or put it on top of a heat-producing device. Use the power adapter specified by the manufacturer; an adapter with the wrong voltage, polarity or current rating can cause instability or damage. If the adapter or router makes unusual sounds, smells burnt or shows physical damage, disconnect it safely and contact the manufacturer or ISP.

A factory reset is not a routine first step. It can erase ISP settings, Wi-Fi names, passwords, port rules and other configuration. Back up the settings where possible and make sure you have the information needed to reconnect. Follow the manufacturer’s reset procedure only when troubleshooting calls for it. If the router is ISP-managed, contact the provider before resetting it.

9. When should you add mesh instead of buying a more powerful router?

Choose the remedy that matches the shape of the problem. If the router works well in the same room but signal becomes weak across a large home, a mesh system or wired access point may be more appropriate than replacing one router with a more expensive single unit.

A single stronger router can still struggle with multiple concrete walls or a long multi-floor layout. A mesh system uses several nodes to distribute coverage. The nodes coordinate the network so compatible devices can move between coverage areas, although roaming decisions are influenced by client devices and system design. Mesh does not guarantee seamless handoff in every device or room.

A wired backhaul connects mesh nodes using Ethernet rather than relying on wireless communication between nodes. This can preserve more wireless capacity for client devices and may improve consistency, but it requires suitable cabling and compatible equipment. If the home already has Ethernet wiring, a wired access point or wired mesh node may be a practical alternative.

For a small home with one weak corner, first try router placement. For one distant room and no Ethernet, a carefully positioned extender may be enough. For several floors or separated rooms, mesh may be more convenient. For a home with Ethernet cabling, wired access points may offer better control. Compare the total cost, not just the price of the main router: mesh systems may require two or more units.

10. A practical router diagnosis you can do before shopping

Use the following sequence to avoid buying equipment without evidence. Keep notes so you can describe the issue clearly to your ISP or the router manufacturer.

  1. Identify the equipment. Record the router model, hardware revision, firmware version, ISP, broadband plan and whether the router is combined with an ONT or modem.
  2. Check support. Search the manufacturer’s official site for firmware, security advisories and end-of-service notices for the exact revision.
  3. Check the cables. Inspect power and Ethernet connections. Do not pull, bend sharply or disconnect fibre connectors unless the ISP instructs you.
  4. Update safely. If an official update is available, read the release notes and instructions. Back up settings and keep power connected throughout the update.
  5. Test near the router. Use the same device and test service. Record download, upload and latency.
  6. Test by Ethernet. If possible, compare a wired device with the same broadband service. This helps identify whether the bottleneck is upstream of Wi-Fi.
  7. Test the problem room. Repeat the test in the place where the issue occurs. Record walls, floors and distance between the device and router.
  8. Try a better position. Move the router to an open, raised and more central location where the cabling permits. Change one thing at a time.
  9. Compare another device. If one phone is slow while another device performs normally in the same place, investigate the phone’s Wi-Fi capability, software and settings.
  10. Ask the ISP. If wired tests are poor or the WAN connection repeatedly drops, request a line and ONT check before buying a router.

For a meaningful comparison, avoid running a large download on another device while testing. Note the time and repeat tests more than once. Do not compare results from different speed-test servers as if they were identical measurements. A test result can vary with server load, routing, device performance and background activity.

12. What to look for when buying a replacement in India

Start with compatibility, support and the actual problem you want to fix. Do not begin with the highest advertised wireless number. The following checklist can help compare products sold in India, including ISP-provided replacement units and retail routers.

  • Local model and warranty: Confirm that the exact model is sold for India, has a valid local warranty and can be serviced locally.
  • Security-update commitment: Look for a clear support policy, update mechanism and end-of-support information. Ask how long security updates are expected and where advisories are published.
  • Security features: Prefer WPA3 support, or a suitable WPA2/WPA3 transition option for older devices. Check that administrator credentials can be changed and updates are maintained.
  • Broadband compatibility: Confirm the WAN port, ISP authentication, VLAN or PPPoE requirements where applicable, and whether the provider supports third-party equipment.
  • Ethernet ports: Match port speed and count to the broadband plan and wired devices. Do not pay for multi-gigabit ports unless there is a use for them.
  • Wireless generation: Choose Wi-Fi 6 or newer when it fits the budget and device mix. Consider 6 GHz or Wi-Fi 7 only when compatible devices and local availability make the features useful.
  • Coverage design: Decide between one router, a wired access point or mesh based on the home layout and cabling options.
  • Configuration: Check whether the router provides guest Wi-Fi, automatic updates, parental controls or other functions you genuinely need. Some advanced features may require subscriptions; confirm before purchase.
  • Power and backup: Check power requirements and whether the router or ONT needs a compatible UPS during local power cuts. A router UPS does not keep the ISP’s network powered outside your home.
  • Setup and support: Check whether the ISP will configure the device and whether you can restore the old setup if the new unit fails to connect.

For many households, a reliable supported Wi-Fi 6 router with gigabit Ethernet is a sensible starting point, not a universal recommendation. A small flat with a moderate plan may not need a premium mesh kit. A larger multi-floor home may benefit more from two well-placed access points than from one expensive router. A home with an ISP-managed ONT should confirm compatibility before purchasing anything.

13. Worked examples: choosing the right fix

The following scenarios are illustrative examples, not customer case studies or measured TEZZ CONNECT tests. They show how the decision method changes depending on the evidence.

Example A: A three-year-old router still works well

A household uses a supported router, receives firmware updates, and gets stable wired and wireless performance in the rooms where it needs internet. The phones and laptop do not support the newest Wi-Fi standard. Replacing the router only to obtain a newer label is unlikely to solve a current problem. The sensible action is to keep the firmware current, review security settings and reassess when needs change or support ends.

Example B: Fast broadband but one bedroom has weak signal

The wired connection and the speed beside the router are satisfactory, but a bedroom behind several walls has unreliable Wi-Fi. This points towards coverage, not necessarily a slow router. The household can test a better central position, then compare a wired access point or mesh node placed between the router and bedroom. Replacing the main router with a more expensive single unit may not overcome the building materials.

Example C: A 100 Mbps port on a faster broadband plan

A user subscribes to a service above 100 Mbps, but the router’s WAN or LAN port is limited to 100 Mbps. Even if the wireless specification looks impressive, traffic passing through that port cannot deliver the full higher rate. Confirm the exact port specification and cable link speed. If the port is the bottleneck, a compatible gigabit router or ISP gateway is a justified upgrade.

Example D: Every device disconnects at the same time

Phones, television and laptop all lose internet access together. The router’s WAN indicator changes, while the Wi-Fi network name remains visible. This may indicate a broadband or ONT issue rather than a wireless radio problem. Record the time and indicator pattern and ask the ISP to investigate. Replacing the router before the line is checked risks spending money without resolving the cause.

Example E: The manufacturer has ended security support

The router still delivers the subscribed speed, but the manufacturer confirms that its hardware revision has reached end of service and no further security fixes will be issued. Performance is not the only measure of whether equipment remains suitable. The household should plan a supported replacement, configure it securely and update its firmware before connecting all devices.

14. Questions to ask your ISP before replacing an ISP-provided router

In many Indian broadband installations, the router or ONT is supplied, configured or remotely managed by the service provider. Before purchasing your own device, ask the provider:

  • Is the supplied device an ONT, router, or combined ONT-router gateway?
  • Can I use my own router with this connection?
  • Does the service require PPPoE credentials, VLAN tagging, a registered MAC address or another configuration?
  • Can the supplied gateway be placed in bridge mode, if needed?
  • Will the ISP continue to provide support if I connect a third-party router?
  • Is a replacement available if the current unit is faulty or no longer supported?
  • Are there rental, deposit, installation or return charges for changing equipment?
  • Will the ISP technician configure the new device, or must I do it myself?

Do not share passwords or account credentials in a public forum. Use the provider’s official support channel. If the ISP manages the router remotely, confirm how the change affects diagnostics and support before disconnecting it.

Frequently asked questions

How many years should a Wi-Fi router last?

There is no single lifespan that applies to every router. Physical condition, firmware support, security updates, usage, heat and manufacturer policy all matter. Check the support status of the exact model and hardware revision rather than using age alone.

Does an old router make internet speed slow?

It can, if its wireless capabilities, processing capacity or Ethernet ports limit the connection. But slow internet can also come from the ISP, the ONT, interference, router placement or the client device. Compare a wired connection with Wi-Fi before deciding.

Will a Wi-Fi 6 router make my internet faster?

It may improve wireless performance or efficiency for compatible devices in suitable conditions. It will not increase the speed of your broadband plan, and older devices will not gain Wi-Fi 6 capabilities just because the router supports them.

Should I upgrade to Wi-Fi 7 now?

Only if its capabilities match your devices, broadband service, home network needs and budget. Check local model availability and support commitments. For many homes, a supported Wi-Fi 6 router or a better coverage layout may provide better value.

Is a mesh system better than a new router?

Mesh is designed to extend coverage across a larger or more complex home. A single router may be enough for a small home. A wired access point can be a strong option where Ethernet is available. Choose based on the location of the weak areas, not on the number of antennas.

Can I use any router with my fibre broadband?

Not always. The ONT, WAN interface, ISP authentication and provider configuration can affect compatibility. Ask your ISP whether third-party routers are supported and what settings are required before purchasing.

Should I replace a router that no longer gets updates?

If the manufacturer has ended security support and there is no safe update path, replacement should be prioritised. A router can continue to provide Wi-Fi while missing important security fixes. Confirm the exact model and revision with the manufacturer.

Does moving a router improve speed?

It can improve the Wi-Fi connection in some rooms by reducing obstructions and improving signal distribution. It does not increase the speed supplied by the ISP. Compare the same device and test method before and after moving it.

Can too many devices overload a router?

Many connected devices do not automatically overload every router. The impact depends on how many are active, what they are doing, wireless airtime, router design and the broadband connection. Identify heavy traffic and test wired or wireless performance before replacing equipment.

Should I buy a router from my ISP or a retail store?

Either can be appropriate. An ISP-supplied device may be easier to configure and support. A retail router may offer features or flexibility that suit your needs. Compare support, security updates, compatibility, warranty and total cost rather than assuming one route is always better.

Conclusion: replace the cause, not just the age

A router upgrade is worthwhile when it fixes a real and identified limitation. Start with security support because an unsupported router can remain fast while becoming harder to protect. Then compare wired and wireless performance, test more than one room, check the devices involved, and consider whether the problem is coverage, capacity, hardware failure or the ISP connection.

If the existing router is supported, secure and reliable, there may be no reason to replace it today. Better placement, a firmware update, an Ethernet cable or a well-positioned access point may solve the issue for less money. If the router has reached end of service, cannot meet the broadband plan’s wired requirements or repeatedly fails after appropriate checks, choose a compatible replacement with a clear update policy and features that match the home.

The best router is not the one with the largest number on its box. It is the supported device or network arrangement that reliably serves the rooms, people and devices that actually use the connection.
