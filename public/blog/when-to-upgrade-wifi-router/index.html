When Should You Upgrade Your Wi-Fi Router?

A router upgrade can help, but age alone is not enough reason to replace equipment.

Internet performance is affected by several connected parts of your home network and the wider internet. This guide explains what to check and how to narrow down the cause without assuming that one test or device tells the whole story.

Start with support and security

The first question is whether the exact router model and hardware revision still receive security and firmware updates. Manufacturers publish end-of-life or end-of-support notices, and support can differ between revisions sold under similar names. If security updates have ended, replacing the router is worth considering even if basic connectivity still works. Check the manufacturer’s official support page rather than relying only on the router’s purchase date.

Look for a limitation you can identify

Frequent reboots, unstable wireless coverage, limited Ethernet ports, or a router that cannot handle the speed tier you subscribe to may indicate a genuine limitation. But slow internet can also come from the ISP, a damaged cable, interference, poor placement or a single device. Test close to the router and, where possible, compare a wired connection before buying new equipment.

Match features to your home

Consider the number of active devices, floor plan, wall materials and the activities taking place. A newer Wi-Fi generation may improve efficiency and capacity when both router and client devices support it, but it does not guarantee a faster connection on every device. Dual-band or tri-band capability, channel width, Ethernet port speed and mesh compatibility may matter depending on your setup.

Router speed ratings are not real-world guarantees

Theoretical wireless link rates are not the same as internet throughput. Actual results depend on client capabilities, distance, interference, channel use, router processing and the broadband connection. A high advertised Wi-Fi number should not be treated as a guaranteed speed for one phone or laptop.

Try these checks before replacing it

Install available firmware updates, place the router in an open central location, check connected devices and remove obsolete settings. Restart it if the manufacturer recommends that step, and inspect power and network cables. If only one device has trouble, investigate that device first. If every device loses connection, record whether the router’s internet or Wi-Fi indicators change.

Make the upgrade decision

Replace the router when security support has ended, hardware is failing, or documented limitations prevent your home from using the service reliably. Choose equipment with supported firmware, suitable wired ports and wireless features that match your devices and home. Keep the old router’s configuration and ISP credentials available until the new connection is confirmed.

Before you finish

Change one variable at a time, record what you observe and repeat tests under comparable conditions. This makes it easier to identify a genuine pattern and explain the issue to your equipment manufacturer or internet provider.
