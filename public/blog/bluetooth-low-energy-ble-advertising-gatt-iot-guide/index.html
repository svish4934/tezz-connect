Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) Explained: Advertising, GATT and IoT

A fitness band reporting a step count, a temperature sensor sending a reading and a phone discovering a nearby accessory can all use Bluetooth Low Energy. BLE is designed for efficient local wireless communication, especially when devices exchange small amounts of data and spend much of their time asleep. This guide explains how discovery, connections, GATT data structures, security and power management fit together.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

What is Bluetooth Low Energy?

Bluetooth Low Energy, commonly shortened to BLE or Bluetooth LE, is a Bluetooth radio technology built for efficient communication between nearby devices. It is widely used in wearables, health and fitness accessories, smart-home sensors, beacons, asset tags, keyboards, industrial monitors and many other connected products.

The name can be misleading. BLE is not simply a slower version of ordinary Bluetooth, and it is not a synonym for every wireless feature in a phone. It is a distinct mode in the Bluetooth family with its own link-layer behaviour, connection model, application profiles and power-management choices. Its strength is not high-bandwidth internet access. Its strength is exchanging useful data without keeping a radio continuously active.

A small sensor might wake periodically, measure temperature, transmit a short update and return to a low-power state. A phone can scan for the sensor, connect when needed, read its characteristics and disconnect. The exact design depends on whether the application needs occasional broadcasts, a sustained connection, acknowledgements, secure configuration or continuous updates.

BLE normally operates in the unlicensed 2.4 GHz ISM band. That band is also used by Wi-Fi and other devices, so interference and coexistence matter. BLE uses frequency-hopping techniques and defined radio channels to make communication more robust, but real-world performance still depends on the environment, antennas and device design.

BLE versus Bluetooth Classic

Bluetooth BR/EDR, commonly called Bluetooth Classic, and Bluetooth LE serve different design goals. Classic Bluetooth is used for many continuous data and audio applications. BLE is optimised for low-energy communication, often involving small bursts of data, low duty cycles and devices that may operate for long periods on small batteries.

Some products support both modes, but support for one does not automatically guarantee support for the other. A phone accessory's packaging may say Bluetooth without explaining which mode its features require. Check the supported profiles and operating-system compatibility rather than assuming that every Bluetooth device behaves the same way.

How BLE devices discover one another: advertising and scanning

BLE discovery often begins before two devices have a connection. An advertiser transmits packets that nearby devices can listen for. A scanner listens for those packets and may use the received information to identify a device, display it to a user or decide whether to connect.

Advertising is connectionless. A sensor can broadcast a small amount of information without first establishing a dedicated connection with every receiver. This makes it useful for beacons, presence detection and simple telemetry. A broadcast may be visible to several scanners at once, depending on the advertising mode and radio conditions.

Scanning can be passive or active. A passive scanner listens and receives advertising packets. An active scanner may request additional information from an advertiser when the advertising event and packet type allow it. The advertiser can return a scan response. The details depend on the advertising mode, packet format and Bluetooth version supported by the devices.

Advertising intervals are a design trade-off. Advertising more frequently can make a device easier to discover and reduce the time before a scanner notices it, but it increases radio activity and energy use. Longer intervals save energy but can make discovery slower. Random timing variation may be used by the protocol to reduce repeated collisions between devices advertising at similar intervals.

Legacy advertising has payload-size limits, while extended advertising allows more flexible data transfer using primary and secondary advertising channels. Periodic advertising lets a receiver synchronise with a repeating broadcast train. These features are useful for different products, but support varies across controllers, operating systems and application APIs.

Advertising data is not automatically private. Nearby scanners may observe unencrypted broadcast content. Avoid placing secrets, account credentials, personal details or sensitive location information in an advertisement. A device can advertise a service identifier or temporary token and require a secure connection before disclosing protected information.

Central, peripheral, observer and broadcaster roles

In a common connected BLE arrangement, one device acts as the central and another as the peripheral. The central scans for advertising devices and initiates a connection. The peripheral advertises and accepts an appropriate connection request. A smartphone often acts as central while a sensor or accessory acts as peripheral, although the roles are not fixed to device categories.

BLE also defines observer and broadcaster roles for connectionless communication. An observer scans for advertising packets but does not initiate a connection in that role. A broadcaster transmits advertising packets but does not accept a connection in that role. These roles are useful for one-to-many broadcast applications and passive monitoring.

Do not confuse central and peripheral with GATT client and GATT server. The first pair describes link-layer connection roles. The second pair describes how an application exchanges attribute data. A device may combine roles in ways supported by its stack and use case. For example, a phone can be a central for one accessory while also hosting a service for another device.

What happens when two BLE devices connect?

After discovering a connectable advertiser, an initiating device can request a connection. The devices agree on connection parameters and begin scheduled connection events. They exchange packets during those events and use the negotiated timing to balance responsiveness, throughput and energy consumption.

A connection interval determines how often the devices have an opportunity to communicate. Shorter intervals can support more responsive exchanges, but they may increase radio activity and power use. Longer intervals can reduce energy consumption for applications that tolerate slower updates. Peripheral latency can allow a device to skip some connection events when it has no data to send, subject to the negotiated parameters and supervision rules.

The supervision timeout determines how long a connection can go without receiving a valid packet before it is considered lost. If devices move out of range, batteries fail or interference becomes severe, the link may time out. Applications should handle disconnect events and reconnect safely rather than assuming a connection lasts forever.

BLE connection parameters should be chosen around the actual application. A heart-rate monitor, a smart lock and a firmware update tool have very different traffic and responsiveness requirements. Aggressively requesting the shortest possible interval is not automatically better: it may increase battery consumption and may be rejected or adjusted by the other device or operating system.

GATT: how BLE application data is organised

The Generic Attribute Profile, or GATT, defines a structured way for BLE applications to expose and exchange data. It organises information into services, characteristics and descriptors. A service groups related capabilities. A characteristic represents a specific value, such as a temperature reading, battery level or control setting. Descriptors provide additional information about a characteristic or its configuration.

For example, a thermometer might expose an Environmental Sensing service. Within that service, a temperature characteristic carries the measured value. A client can read the value when it wants a snapshot, or subscribe to notifications if the device supports updates. The actual assigned service and characteristic identifiers are defined by Bluetooth SIG specifications or by a product's custom profile.

Each attribute has a handle and a type identifier, commonly represented by a UUID. Bluetooth SIG-assigned UUIDs identify standard services and characteristics. Vendors can also define custom 128-bit UUIDs for product-specific functions. A custom UUID does not make a service interoperable by itself: the client and server still need to agree on the meaning, units, byte order, permissions and data format.

Characteristic properties describe available operations. Common properties include read, write, write without response, notify and indicate. A read lets a client request the current value. A write sends data to the server. A notification sends an update without an application-level acknowledgement. An indication requires confirmation at the ATT/GATT layer. The correct choice depends on whether the application needs reliability, latency and confirmation.

Notifications are efficient for frequent sensor updates, but an application should not treat every notification as a durable record or assume it can never be lost. If every measurement must be accounted for, design sequence numbers, buffering, acknowledgements or a higher-level recovery mechanism. The BLE link provides transport procedures; the application defines what the data means and how missing updates are handled.

GATT client and GATT server: the application roles

A GATT client discovers services and characteristics on a GATT server and performs operations such as reading, writing, subscribing to notifications or confirming indications. A GATT server hosts the attribute database and responds to requests. In many consumer examples, the phone is the client and the wearable is the server, but an application can use the roles differently.

Service discovery allows a client to learn which services and characteristics a server exposes. The client may read characteristic properties and descriptors to understand which operations are available. Robust applications should not assume every device exposes exactly the same optional features or that a characteristic is accessible without authentication.

Standard profiles help devices from different manufacturers interoperate. A standard profile defines how services and characteristics should be used for a particular purpose. Custom profiles can be appropriate for proprietary products, but they require clear documentation and version management. If a product changes its data structure, clients need a way to detect supported versions and avoid interpreting bytes using the wrong format.

ATT, MTU and data transfer limits

GATT is built on the Attribute Protocol (ATT), which defines requests, responses, commands, notifications and indications for exchanging attribute values. The negotiated ATT Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) affects how much attribute data can be carried in an individual protocol operation. The default ATT MTU is 23 octets, leaving less room for a characteristic value after protocol overhead. Devices can negotiate a larger MTU when supported.

A larger MTU can reduce the number of protocol exchanges needed to transfer a payload, but it does not guarantee a particular application throughput. Actual performance depends on connection interval, data length extension, PHY, controller buffers, operating-system scheduling, retransmissions, application processing and radio conditions. A BLE device should be tested with the target phones and gateways rather than relying on a theoretical maximum.

For larger transfers, applications may need to divide data into chunks and include sequence information. Firmware updates, for example, require integrity checks, resume behaviour and a safe recovery strategy if the connection drops. A device should not assume that a long transfer will remain uninterrupted simply because the first packets succeeded.

BLE PHY options, range and speed

The Bluetooth LE physical layer defines radio modes with different trade-offs. LE 1M is the baseline PHY. LE 2M can provide a higher over-the-air symbol rate where both devices support it and conditions permit. LE Coded uses additional coding to improve robustness and range at a lower data rate. These are radio-level options, not guaranteed application speeds.

Range is not a fixed number. It depends on transmit power, receiver sensitivity, antenna efficiency, enclosure materials, device orientation, interference, obstacles and the selected PHY. A small sensor inside a metal cabinet may behave very differently from an open-air development board. Walls, floors, people and nearby 2.4 GHz equipment can attenuate or disrupt the signal.

LE Coded can help in challenging radio environments, but the lower data rate means it is not a universal performance upgrade. LE 2M can move data faster over a suitable link, but it may not provide the same range margin as a coded mode. Product designers should choose the PHY that matches the actual data and coverage requirements.

Bluetooth radio range claims in advertisements should be treated as scenario-specific. Ask whether the distance was measured indoors or outdoors, with line of sight, at what transmit power, with which antenna and at what packet success rate. A useful product test measures reliability and latency in the real installation rather than only recording the farthest distance at which one packet was received.

Why BLE can save battery—and what drains it

BLE's energy advantage comes from system design, not a magic property that makes every Bluetooth feature consume almost no power. Devices can spend long periods in low-power states, wake for scheduled advertising or connection events, transmit short packets and return to sleep. The application determines how often this happens.

Advertising interval, transmit power, scan duration, connection interval, peripheral latency, payload size, retransmissions and the number of active connections all affect energy use. A device that advertises constantly at a short interval may drain its battery much faster than one that sends an occasional update. A phone scanning continuously can also consume more power than an application that uses filtered, time-limited scans.

Sensor design matters too. A radio may be efficient while a continuously powered display, processor, GPS receiver or measurement circuit dominates the product's energy budget. Battery-life estimates should account for the entire device and the expected usage pattern.

To improve battery life, transmit only the information needed, choose an appropriate advertising interval, use connection parameters that match the application, avoid unnecessary scanning, batch data where practical and measure current consumption on real hardware. Test worst-case conditions such as weak signal strength, repeated reconnects and firmware update sessions.

Pairing, bonding and BLE security

BLE security includes procedures for pairing, authentication, encryption and bonding. Pairing establishes security keys and may authenticate the devices, depending on the selected association model and available input/output capabilities. Bonding stores keys so devices can reconnect securely later without repeating the full pairing process.

Not every BLE connection is encrypted, and not every pairing method provides the same protection against an active attacker. A product that uses a weak or unauthenticated pairing method may be vulnerable to interception or impersonation in situations where stronger protection is required. The correct security level depends on the sensitivity of the data and the consequences of unauthorised control.

Designers should require authenticated access for sensitive operations, protect keys, validate inputs, restrict write permissions and avoid exposing administrative controls to unauthorised nearby devices. A smart lock should not treat the fact that a phone can see its advertisement as proof that the phone's owner is authorised to unlock it.

Privacy is another concern. Stable device identifiers can allow observers to track a device over time. BLE supports privacy features such as resolvable private addresses, but effective privacy depends on correct implementation and how applications handle identifiers. Avoid broadcasting persistent personal identifiers and do not use radio visibility as a substitute for user consent.

Applications should request only the operating-system permissions they need and explain why scanning or connecting is necessary. On mobile platforms, permission rules and background scanning behaviour change across operating-system versions. Follow current platform documentation and test the app on supported versions.

Where BLE is used in everyday life and industry

Wearables and fitness devices: Watches, bands, cadence sensors and heart-rate accessories can exchange readings with phones and fitness equipment. The phone may retrieve measurements on demand or subscribe to updates during an activity.

Smart homes: Locks, switches, temperature sensors and commissioning tools may use BLE for local setup and control. Some devices use BLE to help provision credentials for a separate Wi-Fi or Thread connection. BLE does not mean the product itself has internet access.

Asset tracking and beacons: Tags can advertise identifiers or telemetry that nearby gateways detect. Location estimates may be based on received signal strength, but RSSI is affected by walls, bodies, reflections and antenna orientation. It is not a precise distance measurement on its own.

Healthcare and wellness: Consumer devices can transfer measurements to phones or dedicated receivers. Medical products require appropriate validation, risk management, privacy controls and regulatory compliance; using BLE does not make a measurement medically accurate.

Industrial monitoring: Battery-powered sensors can report status, temperature, vibration or maintenance information to nearby gateways. Industrial deployments need careful radio planning, device identity management, security updates and resilience when gateways or backhaul links are unavailable.

Human interface devices: Keyboards, presentation controls and other accessories can use BLE for local input. The user experience depends on latency, reconnection behaviour, battery reporting and compatibility with the host platform.

BLE, Wi-Fi, NFC and Thread: choosing the right connection

BLE is not the answer to every wireless problem. Wi-Fi is often more appropriate when a device needs higher throughput or direct access to a local IP network. NFC is designed for very short-range interactions such as tap-based pairing or contactless use. Thread is a low-power IPv6 mesh networking technology designed for connected devices. Products may combine these technologies because they solve different parts of the system.

Common BLE problems and how to troubleshoot them

Device does not appear during scanning: Confirm that the peripheral is powered, advertising and within range. Check whether the app applies a service UUID or manufacturer-data filter that excludes the device. Confirm mobile permissions and Bluetooth settings, and test with a known BLE scanner where permitted.

Device appears but connection fails: It may stop advertising after a connection, allow only one connection, reject an unauthorised central or be busy with another client. Check connection limits, pairing state, firmware logs and whether the app is trying to connect to a stale device address.

Connection drops repeatedly: Investigate distance, interference, antenna placement, battery voltage, connection supervision timeout and device firmware. Test with the phone close to the peripheral and remove likely sources of 2.4 GHz interference to narrow the cause.

Service or characteristic is missing: Discover services after connecting and check the device's firmware version. The peripheral may expose a different profile, require authentication before revealing attributes or use a custom UUID that the application does not recognise.

Notifications never arrive: Confirm that the characteristic supports notifications or indications, that the client enabled the appropriate configuration descriptor and that the peripheral actually updates the value. Check whether the application unsubscribes during background transitions or reconnects.

Data looks incorrect: Verify byte order, signed versus unsigned values, scaling factors, units, field lengths and protocol version. A characteristic value is a sequence of bytes; the application must decode it according to the profile definition.

Battery life is shorter than expected: Measure current draw across advertising, scanning, connected idle and data-transfer states. Review advertising frequency, connection parameters, retries, transmit power and background activity. Use measurements rather than assuming that the BLE radio is the only power consumer.

Design checklist for a reliable BLE product

  • Define whether the device needs connectionless advertising, a connected link or both.
  • Choose central, peripheral, observer and broadcaster roles around the intended topology.
  • Use standard GATT services where they fit; document custom UUIDs and data formats clearly.
  • Specify units, scaling, byte order, permissions and protocol versioning for every characteristic.
  • Choose connection and advertising intervals based on latency and battery measurements.
  • Plan reconnection, buffering and recovery for radio loss and mobile app backgrounding.
  • Use authenticated pairing and encryption for sensitive data and control operations.
  • Minimise identifiers and sensitive information in unencrypted advertisements.
  • Test on actual phones, gateways, operating-system versions and installation environments.
  • Provide a secure firmware-update and vulnerability-response process for deployed devices.

Frequently asked questions

1. What does BLE stand for?

BLE stands for Bluetooth Low Energy, a Bluetooth technology designed for devices that exchange data efficiently and often send small amounts of information while spending much of their time in low-power states.

2. Is Bluetooth Low Energy the same as Bluetooth?

Bluetooth Low Energy and Bluetooth BR/EDR, often called Classic Bluetooth, are distinct Bluetooth radio modes with different design goals. BLE is optimised for low-energy communication, while Classic Bluetooth supports other use cases such as many continuous audio connections.

3. What is BLE advertising?

Advertising is a connectionless way for a BLE device to broadcast packets that nearby scanners can discover. Advertising can announce a device, expose limited data or invite another device to connect.

4. What are GATT services and characteristics?

GATT organises application data into services and characteristics. A service groups related functions, while a characteristic represents a value with properties that describe whether it can be read, written, notified or indicated.

5. What is the difference between a BLE central and peripheral?

A central scans for advertising devices and can initiate a connection. A peripheral advertises and can accept a connection. These are link-layer roles and should not be confused with GATT client and server roles.

6. Does BLE need internet access?

No. BLE communication works locally between nearby devices and does not require an internet connection. A phone or gateway can separately forward collected data to an online service.

7. Is BLE secure?

BLE includes security mechanisms such as pairing, bonding, authentication and encryption, but protection depends on the pairing method, device implementation, permissions and application design. Not every BLE advertisement is encrypted.

8. How far can Bluetooth Low Energy reach?

There is no single fixed BLE range. It depends on transmit power, receiver sensitivity, antenna design, PHY, obstacles, interference, device orientation and local radio conditions. A product's tested range is more useful than a universal distance claim.

9. Does BLE consume less battery than Wi-Fi?

BLE is often a good fit for small, intermittent exchanges because devices can sleep between events. Actual battery life depends on advertising frequency, connection interval, payload size, radio conditions, firmware and the device's other power demands.

10. Can a phone connect to multiple BLE devices?

Many modern phones can maintain connections with multiple BLE peripherals, but practical limits vary by operating system, chipset, application, connection parameters and device behaviour. Developers should test the intended combination on real hardware.
