AI-Native Networks: How Telecom Operators May Use AI to Run Networks

A mobile network is more than the towers and antennas people see. Behind every call, message and data session are radio links, transport routes, core systems, cloud platforms and operational teams working together. As these systems grow more complex, telecom operators are exploring ways to use artificial intelligence to understand network conditions, predict problems and make carefully controlled adjustments. The long-term idea is an AI-native network: one designed to use data and intelligence throughout its operation, rather than treating AI as a separate tool bolted onto a few dashboards. That future is developing in stages. It does not mean that a chatbot will run a national mobile network on its own.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

1. What does an AI-native network actually mean?

There is no single switch that turns a conventional telecom network into an AI-native one. The term describes a direction of architecture and operations: network systems are built to collect useful data, learn from patterns, support decisions and coordinate actions across different parts of the network.

Traditional network operations already use automation. A script can restart a service, a monitoring system can raise an alarm, and a planning tool can forecast traffic. AI adds capabilities such as pattern recognition, anomaly detection, forecasting and decision support. An AI-native approach tries to connect these capabilities to the way the network is designed, managed and improved over time.

The important distinction is between AI-assisted operations and autonomous operations. An assistant may summarise alarms and suggest a likely cause. An autonomous workflow may be permitted to make a bounded change, check whether it worked and reverse it if agreed conditions are not met. The second requires far more than a capable model: it needs trusted data, clear policies, safe interfaces, testing, audit trails and a way to involve people when risk is high.

2. Three ways AI and mobile networks can work together

The AI-RAN Alliance describes three complementary areas. They are useful because people sometimes use “AI network” to mean three very different things.

ApproachMeaningExample
AI for RANAI improves how the radio access network operates.Predicting cell congestion or helping optimise radio resources.
AI and RANAI workloads and radio network workloads share computing infrastructure where performance and isolation requirements allow.Using a common distributed computing platform for telecom functions and selected AI workloads.
AI on RANThe network infrastructure supports AI services closer to connected users and devices.Running an enterprise inference service at a network edge location.

These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. An operator can use AI to forecast faults without hosting customer-facing AI applications at cell sites. It can also explore edge AI services without giving an AI model permission to reconfigure the radio network.

3. What could AI help a telecom operator do?

Forecast traffic before a busy period

Networks experience changing demand. A stadium event, a commuter rush, a festival or a local outage can shift traffic between cells and routes. Forecasting models can combine historical patterns with current measurements to estimate where demand may rise. Engineers can use those estimates to plan capacity, adjust resources or prepare additional support. Forecasts are not guarantees: unusual events, poor input data and sudden behaviour changes can make predictions wrong.

Spot unusual behaviour earlier

Network equipment produces alarms, counters, logs and performance measurements. A model can look for combinations that are unusual even when no single metric has crossed a fixed threshold. For example, a gradual rise in retransmissions alongside a change in handover failures may point to a developing issue. This can help teams investigate sooner, but an anomaly is a clue—not proof of a fault or its cause.

Support radio optimisation

Radio conditions change with user movement, buildings, interference, weather and the number of active devices. AI/ML techniques can support tasks such as beam management, mobility optimisation, load balancing and coverage or capacity optimisation. 3GPP work has introduced AI/ML-related capabilities into the 5G evolution, including work on the NR air interface and network-side optimisation. The exact feature set depends on the release, implementation and operator deployment; it should not be described as one universal AI mode present on every 5G network.

Plan maintenance rather than wait for breakdowns

Models can compare equipment telemetry with past failures and maintenance records to identify components that deserve inspection. That can help prioritise field visits and spare parts. A model should not automatically declare a tower, radio or power system defective based on one weak signal. Good maintenance decisions combine model output with engineering checks and the actual operating environment.

Improve service assurance

A customer's slow connection may involve radio access, backhaul, core-network policy, DNS, an application server or a device. Cross-domain analysis can correlate events that separate monitoring tools might show as unrelated. The goal is to shorten the time needed to find the real cause, not simply to produce more alerts or close tickets faster.

Manage energy more intelligently

Network energy use changes with traffic and site conditions. AI-supported forecasting may help operators adjust selected resources during quieter periods while maintaining coverage, resilience and service quality. The GSMA's Mobile Net Zero 2026 report discusses opportunities such as network sleep modes, dynamic shutdown, load balancing and traffic forecasting. Energy saving must be measured against service impact: switching off capacity in the wrong place or at the wrong time can harm customers.

4. How a safe AI control loop works

A useful way to picture network automation is as a repeating loop. The system observes the network, evaluates what is happening, chooses an action within its authority, applies the change and checks the result.

  1. Observe: collect telemetry such as traffic load, signal quality, alarms, latency, packet loss, power use and service-level indicators.
  2. Understand: compare current conditions with baselines, forecasts, topology and known incidents.
  3. Recommend: identify a likely cause and propose one or more actions with expected benefits and risks.
  4. Check policy: confirm the action is permitted for this network area, time, customer group and risk level.
  5. Act: use a controlled management interface to make the approved change.
  6. Verify: measure whether the change improved the intended service without creating a new problem. Roll back or escalate if it did not.

This is often called a closed loop because the system does not stop after issuing a command. It checks the outcome and uses that evidence for the next decision. ETSI's Zero-touch network and Service Management work explores end-to-end automation, intent-based management, network digital twins and agent-based network assurance. A digital twin can help evaluate a proposed configuration in a model of the network before applying it to live infrastructure, although its usefulness depends on how accurately it represents the real system.

For a high-impact change—such as altering a large group of cells, changing routing policy or affecting emergency-service connectivity—a human approval step may remain essential. The safe level of autonomy depends on the consequence of being wrong.

5. From automation to autonomy: what do the levels mean?

TM Forum's Autonomous Networks framework describes six maturity levels, from Level 0 to Level 5. It is a way to assess autonomy for a particular network scenario or operational domain; it is not a certificate that an entire operator is fully autonomous.

  • Level 0 – Manual: people perform the operational work.
  • Level 1 – Assisted: tools provide basic support, but people make and carry out decisions.
  • Level 2 – Partial automation: specific routine tasks can be automated under defined conditions.
  • Level 3 – Conditional autonomy: the system can handle a wider set of tasks, with human oversight and intervention for exceptions or important decisions.
  • Level 4 – High autonomy: intent-driven, predictive decision-making and closed-loop management can operate across defined services or domains, with people setting policies and handling exceptions.
  • Level 5 – Full autonomy: the framework's highest maturity stage, where the network can manage the relevant scenario with minimal or no human intervention in normal operation.

TM Forum notes that Level 4 is a significant step beyond systems that merely assist people. The distinction matters: an operator may have advanced automation for energy saving in one group of sites while relying on manual processes for a different task. Claims about autonomy should state which process, geography and network domain have been assessed.

6. Where do O-RAN controllers and AI agents fit?

In an Open RAN architecture, the RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) provides a framework for applications that analyse and influence radio network behaviour. Non-real-time RIC functions support longer-horizon optimisation, policy and model management; near-real-time RIC functions support faster control within their defined operating range. Applications commonly called rApps and xApps use these environments to deliver different kinds of optimisation and control.

These components are not magic AI boxes. Their behaviour depends on the data they receive, the interfaces exposed by network equipment, the model or logic inside each application, and the operator's policies. Open interfaces can help multi-vendor integration, but interoperability still needs testing. A system can be technically open and still be difficult to operate if data formats, timing, model lifecycle or vendor implementations differ.

AI agents are another developing area. An agent may gather evidence from several systems, summarise an incident, call approved diagnostic tools or prepare a change request. That does not mean a general-purpose language model should be allowed to issue arbitrary commands to production network equipment. In a responsible design, the agent operates through restricted tools, validates its inputs, records what it did and escalates when it cannot prove that an action is safe.

7. What changes in 5G-Advanced and future 6G?

AI in telecom networks is not waiting for 6G. 3GPP has already worked on AI/ML capabilities for 5G radio systems and network operations. The standards process includes specific use cases such as radio-interface enhancement, mobility optimisation, network slicing and coverage or capacity optimisation. These features are introduced through defined specifications and releases; they are not evidence that every operator has deployed every feature.

For 6G, AI and machine learning are being studied as part of the wider system and radio design. The direction is to consider data collection, model lifecycle, inference, security and network behaviour earlier in the architecture. It is still an evolving standards and research programme. No one can responsibly describe the final 6G AI architecture or promise that a particular feature will be present in every commercial network.

Our earlier articles explain edge computing, submarine cable resilience and IPv6. These technologies solve different problems, but they illustrate why network intelligence must work across radio, transport, internet protocols and computing locations.

8. What could users notice?

Most AI-driven network operations will happen behind the scenes. If the systems work well, a user may experience fewer avoidable service interruptions, more consistent capacity during busy periods, quicker restoration after some faults or better energy management at network sites. Those are possible outcomes, not automatic results of adding AI.

AI does not create extra spectrum, repair a cut fibre cable, remove a building's radio shadow or guarantee that an application server is healthy. It cannot compensate for every shortage in physical infrastructure. A well-designed network with good engineering and reliable operations may outperform a poorly designed network with an expensive AI platform.

For people testing their connection, the same practical checks still matter: compare results at different times, test close to the router, use a wired connection when possible, and separate Wi-Fi problems from wider ISP issues. AI in the operator's network does not change what a speed test measures at your device.

9. The risks: why more autonomy needs stronger governance

Network automation can scale a good decision—but it can also scale a bad one. Important risks include:

  • Bad or incomplete data: missing telemetry, incorrect timestamps or inconsistent network inventories can mislead a model.
  • Model drift: traffic patterns, equipment and software change, so a model that once worked may become unreliable.
  • False confidence: a model can provide a plausible explanation that is wrong. Operators need evidence, confidence thresholds and independent checks.
  • Security threats: attackers may manipulate data, exploit management interfaces or target model pipelines. AI systems add assets that must be protected.
  • Unintended interactions: two individually sensible optimisation loops may conflict when they change shared resources.
  • Vendor lock-in: proprietary data models, orchestration tools or model formats can make it hard to move between suppliers.
  • Privacy and accountability: network data may reveal patterns about users and locations. Operators need appropriate access controls, retention policies and clear responsibility for automated actions.

Good safeguards include staged rollouts, simulation and digital-twin testing, bounded permissions, rate limits, human approval for high-impact changes, explainable decision records, continuous monitoring and tested rollback procedures. Operators should measure not only how often automation succeeds, but also how often it makes a harmful change, how quickly people can intervene and whether customers actually receive a better service.

10. What does this mean for India?

India's mobile networks serve dense cities, highways, villages, industrial sites and regions with very different power, transport and radio conditions. AI-supported traffic forecasting, energy management, fault detection and field-force planning could be useful in such varied environments. But local geography, fibre availability, power reliability, spectrum, site density and equipment support all influence whether a particular use case makes sense.

India is involved in the international standards conversation around future mobile networks. The Department of Telecommunications and TSDSI supported 3GPP RAN meetings on 6G standardisation in Bengaluru in August 2025. That is evidence of India's participation in the standards process—not proof that a nationwide AI-native 6G network has been deployed.

Public claims about an operator's AI maturity should be checked carefully. A demonstration, a pilot in selected cells, an AI-assisted customer-care tool and a validated closed-loop network operation are different achievements. Unless an operator publishes the scope and evidence, it is better not to assume that a feature is active across the country.

11. What is likely to happen next?

Over the next few years, expect more AI-assisted workflows for network planning, fault analysis, energy management and service assurance. Operators are likely to expand automation first in tasks that are repetitive, measurable and reversible. More complex cross-domain actions will require better data foundations, standard interfaces, careful testing and governance.

AI-RAN research will continue exploring how AI can improve radio performance, how AI and radio workloads can share computing resources, and how network infrastructure might support edge AI services. At the same time, standards bodies and industry groups will keep working on interoperability, model management, security and measurable autonomy.

The most realistic future is not a network that never needs engineers. It is a network that gives engineers better information, handles well-understood tasks safely and makes it easier to detect when a situation falls outside its design limits.

12. The network data an AI system needs

Network intelligence depends on observations from several layers. Radio measurements alone cannot explain every customer experience because a mobile session also depends on transport, core-network functions, DNS, external services, device software and the application itself. A useful operational model combines signals while preserving their meaning and ownership.

LayerUseful observationsOperational question
Radio accessCell load, signal quality, interference, handover outcomes, retransmissions and radio resource use.Is the radio link or local cell capacity contributing to the problem?
Transport/backhaulLink utilisation, queue depth, errors, route changes, latency and packet loss.Can traffic move reliably between the site and core?
Core networkSession setup success, control-plane load, user-plane throughput, policy outcomes and service-function health.Are sessions being created and handled correctly?
Application/serviceDNS resolution, service response time, API errors, content delivery and application availability.Is the network healthy while the destination service is failing?
Site infrastructurePower events, temperature, battery condition, cooling alarms and equipment status.Could a physical-site condition cause degradation?
Customer experienceCall setup failures, dropped sessions and carefully aggregated experience indicators.Does a network metric correspond to a real user-visible impact?

Putting every field into one large model is not the same as building a reliable data foundation. Each measurement needs a definition, timestamp, unit, collection interval, source, quality indicator and retention rule. A radio counter collected every 15 minutes cannot safely be treated as equivalent to a one-second transport alarm. Clock drift can make unrelated events appear correlated, while a missing feed can make a healthy region look unusually quiet.

Operators should minimise personal data. Many optimisation tasks can use aggregated or pseudonymised operational measurements rather than detailed histories tied to identifiable subscribers. Access should be limited by role, and retention should match the operational purpose and applicable legal requirements.

13. Worked example: diagnosing slow mobile data

Consider a hypothetical cluster of complaints around a busy commercial district. Users report that pages take a long time to open during the evening. A simplistic system might see high cell utilisation and recommend adding radio capacity. A careful workflow checks whether that is actually the bottleneck.

  1. Define the symptom: identify the affected area and time window. Distinguish slow page loading from low throughput, failed session setup or application-specific errors.
  2. Check radio conditions: compare cell load, signal quality, interference and handover outcomes. Saturated radio resources may contribute, but load alone does not prove causation.
  3. Check transport: inspect backhaul utilisation, queueing, packet loss and route changes. A cell can have available radio resources while the site-to-core link is congested.
  4. Check core and DNS: review session setup, user-plane health and resolver performance. Slow name resolution can make an otherwise fast connection feel unresponsive.
  5. Check the destination: compare performance to multiple services. One overloaded application server should not trigger a network-wide radio change.
  6. Recommend within bounds: present evidence, confidence, affected scope, expected benefit and possible side effects to the responsible engineer.
  7. Apply and verify: if an approved change is made, compare before-and-after metrics and customer impact. Reverse it if the target improves only by harming another cell or service.

This example illustrates why correlation is not causation. A busy cell and a slow application may occur together without the cell being the cause. AI can assemble evidence and narrow the investigation; it should not convert a statistical association into an unreviewed production command.

Establish a baseline using comparable weekdays, event conditions, device populations and traffic levels. If radio parameters and backhaul routing are changed simultaneously, it becomes difficult to know which change helped or caused a regression. Controlled experiments and one-change-at-a-time rollouts are safer where the network permits them.

14. Evaluate an AI use case before deployment

Operators should begin with a measurable operational problem, not a desire to deploy a fashionable model. A proposal should explain the existing process, the decision that needs improvement, the data available, the action the system may take and the consequences if the recommendation is wrong.

AreaQuestionsEvidence
Problem definitionWhat customer or operational problem is being addressed?Baseline incident rate, diagnosis time, service metric or energy use.
Data qualityAre measurements timely, complete, correctly labelled and representative?Missing-data rate, clock accuracy, schema ownership and lineage.
Model performanceDoes it work across sites, seasons, vendors and traffic patterns?Precision, recall, false-positive rate, calibration and drift.
Operational safetyWhat can it change, and what is outside its authority?Permissions, approval rules, rate limits and rollback tests.
InteroperabilityCan it work across the deployed vendor environment?Interface conformance and integration tests.
Business valueDoes improvement exceed implementation and lifecycle costs?Service outcomes, labour impact, energy use and support cost.
AccountabilityWho owns outcomes and handles exceptions?Named owner, audit trail and escalation route.

Model accuracy alone is not enough. In outage detection, missing a serious incident may be more costly than generating extra alerts. In radio optimisation, a false recommendation could affect many customers. The acceptable error profile depends on the action and its impact.

Test on data not used to train or tune the model. Include rare events, seasonal variation, new equipment versions and unusual traffic. Evaluate by geography and vendor as well as overall averages; a model that performs well nationally can still fail in a particular region. Record limitations and do not extrapolate a small pilot into a nationwide claim.

15. Shadow mode, pilot cells and controlled rollout

A safe introduction often begins in shadow mode. The model receives operational data and produces recommendations, but cannot change the network. Engineers compare its output with incidents and human decisions. This exposes false alarms, missing context and recommendations that are technically valid but operationally unsuitable.

A limited pilot can then allow a narrow class of reversible changes in a controlled set of sites. Define permitted parameter ranges, maximum affected cells, change frequency, blackout periods and immediate rollback conditions. Include a comparison group where practical, so improvements are not confused with ordinary changes in traffic or weather.

Expansion should depend on evidence. A successful pilot must show that the intended service metric improved, other important metrics did not deteriorate, engineers could understand and intervene, and the system behaved safely during exceptions.

Use staged deployment rings: laboratory or digital-twin evaluation, shadow mode, a small pilot, a broader region and wider production. Each stage needs entry and exit criteria. Treat a model update as a meaningful change: version it, test it, record dependencies and retain a route back to a known-good model and policy.

16. Digital twins: rehearsal, not a perfect copy

A network digital twin models some part of a real network and its behaviour. It may represent topology, equipment, configuration, traffic patterns or selected physical processes. Operators can explore proposed changes, test scenarios and train teams without immediately affecting customers.

A twin is only as reliable as its scope and calibration. If it does not represent a vendor software change, interference pattern, congested transport link or failure mode, its simulation may give false confidence. Teams should record which parts of the live network it represents, when it was updated and which behaviours it cannot reproduce.

Use the twin to reject unsafe or ineffective options, not to prove a change is guaranteed to work in production. Follow simulation with controlled live testing and monitoring. Document assumptions, input data, model version and limitations so engineers can interpret the result correctly.

17. Protect the AI operations pipeline

An AI-native network includes data collectors, message brokers, feature pipelines, model registries, training environments, inference services, orchestration tools, credentials and interfaces to network management systems. Every component can become a security or reliability dependency.

  • Separate observation from control: systems that read telemetry should not automatically receive broad write permissions.
  • Use least privilege: give each service and operator only the access required, and expire temporary permissions.
  • Authenticate management interfaces: protect APIs, certificates and secrets; log privileged actions.
  • Protect data integrity: validate source identity, timestamps, schema and expected ranges before measurements enter a decision pipeline.
  • Control model changes: approve, version and test models and configuration before production promotion.
  • Monitor unusual behaviour: detect unexpected command volume, policy failures, unusual access and model-output shifts.
  • Prepare manual recovery: engineers must be able to disable automation and operate the service safely.

Threat modelling should consider malicious data injection, compromised suppliers, stolen credentials, insecure model artefacts and abuse of legitimate permissions. It should also consider accidental failure: a bad model update or corrupted telemetry feed can be as disruptive as an external attack. Security teams, network engineers, data teams and suppliers need a shared incident process.

18. Prevent conflicting optimisation loops

Multiple automation systems may act on shared resources. A radio optimisation workflow may increase transmit power to improve coverage, while an energy-saving workflow tries to place a neighbouring resource into a low-power state. A mobility workflow may change handover parameters at the same time. Each decision can look reasonable in isolation, yet their combined effect may be unstable.

Operators need coordination across domains. Define which system owns a parameter, which actions take precedence, how changes are rate-limited and how one workflow learns about another's pending actions. Where systems share a resource, a central policy or orchestration layer should resolve competing intents before commands reach the network.

Look for oscillation: a system repeatedly changes a setting, observes a temporary improvement and then reverses itself after another controller acts. Track configuration churn, rollback frequency and repeated actions as reliability indicators. A network constantly being optimised may be less stable than one making fewer, well-justified changes.

19. Measure whether AI actually improves the network

Success should be measured in operational and customer terms, not by the number of models deployed or commands executed. The right metrics depend on the use case, but a balanced scorecard can include:

OutcomeExample measuresGuardrail
ReliabilityAvailability, dropped-session rate, incident duration and recovery time.Measure by geography and customer segment, not only network-wide averages.
Customer experienceCall setup success, application response, throughput consistency and complaints.Separate network causes from device and application causes.
Operational efficiencyDiagnosis time, repeat incidents, field visits and manual steps.Do not reward ticket closure if the issue remains.
EnergyEnergy per unit of traffic, site consumption and time in energy-saving states.Ensure savings do not reduce coverage, capacity or resilience.
Automation safetyFalse actions, rollback rate, policy violations and human overrides.Report near misses and harmful changes, not just successes.

Compare outcomes with a credible baseline and account for external factors such as new spectrum, site upgrades, fibre expansion, software releases and seasonal demand. If several investments happen together, do not attribute the entire improvement to AI. Public claims should state scope: pilot or production, number of sites, duration, network generation, metric definition and independent assessment where applicable.

20. Rules, machine learning and generative AI are not interchangeable

Not every network task needs a large AI model. A fixed threshold or deterministic rule may be easier to explain and test for a well-understood condition. Statistical methods may suit trend detection or forecasting. Machine-learning models can help where relationships are complex and sufficient representative data exists. Generative AI can summarise incident timelines, search technical documentation or help engineers navigate approved diagnostic tools.

Choose methods according to the task. A generative model that produces fluent text is not automatically suitable for real-time radio control. It may misunderstand a vendor-specific command, invent a parameter or omit a safety condition. If used in operations, it should retrieve from approved sources, show supporting evidence, use narrowly scoped tools and require confirmation before high-impact changes.

A sensible architecture combines methods: deterministic policy checks constrain what is allowed; forecasting models estimate demand; anomaly detectors highlight unusual behaviour; an operations assistant assembles evidence; and a controlled orchestration system executes an approved change. This is safer than giving one general-purpose model unrestricted authority.

21. Procurement questions for operators and enterprise buyers

Ask vendors which use cases are supported today and which are only on a roadmap. What data does the product collect, how is it normalised and who owns it? Which interfaces and standards are implemented? Can the operator export telemetry, policies, models and audit records if it changes supplier?

Ask how the system behaves when telemetry is delayed, a management interface is unavailable or a model produces a low-confidence recommendation. Can it run in read-only or shadow mode? What actions can it perform without approval? How are model versions tested and rolled back? How are vulnerabilities, supplier access and incident notifications handled?

Request evidence from a deployment with a comparable topology and operational environment. A laboratory demonstration does not prove that a product interoperates with a multi-vendor production network. Define acceptance criteria in contracts: measurable outcomes, data portability, security controls, support responsibilities, upgrade windows and exit arrangements.

23. What AI-native networks will not solve by themselves

AI cannot manufacture spectrum, build fibre where none exists, replace a damaged power supply, eliminate physical obstructions or guarantee that every handset supports a network feature. It cannot make a poorly maintained asset reliable simply by predicting its failure. It also cannot remove the need for competent engineering, field operations, cybersecurity, standards and public accountability.

In India, the same AI system may face very different conditions across dense urban sites, rural coverage areas, highways and industrial campuses. A model trained on one environment may not generalise to another without careful validation. Power availability, backhaul, weather exposure, local traffic, device mix and vendor configuration all matter.

The realistic promise is more disciplined operations: better visibility, earlier warning, more consistent decisions and automation for tasks whose risks are understood. That can be valuable, but it should be demonstrated with measurable results rather than assumed from the word “AI”.

Frequently asked questions

What is an AI-native network?

A network designed to use data, AI and automation across its operation and lifecycle. Autonomy can differ by task and domain.

Will AI make mobile internet faster?

AI may help manage resources and congestion, but speed also depends on spectrum, coverage, backhaul, devices, servers and demand.

What is the difference between AI for RAN and AI on RAN?

AI for RAN improves radio functions. AI on RAN uses network infrastructure to support AI services closer to users or devices.

Are 5G networks already using AI?

AI/ML capabilities have been specified for selected 5G-related use cases, but deployed features vary by network, release, vendor and operator.

Can AI run an entire mobile network without people?

Many systems assist people or automate narrow tasks. High autonomy requires policy, supervision and exception handling.

What are O-RAN rApps and xApps?

They are application types used with RAN Intelligent Controller environments for optimisation and control at non-real-time and near-real-time layers.

Can AI predict every network outage?

No. Models can detect patterns associated with some faults, but unexpected events, missing data and new failure modes can defeat predictions.

Will AI-native networks arrive only with 6G?

No. AI is already used in network operations and specified for selected 5G-related capabilities. 6G AI architecture remains under development.

Can AI reduce telecom energy consumption?

It can help forecast demand and optimise selected resources, but savings must be balanced against coverage, resilience and service quality.

Does an AI chatbot control a telecom tower?

Not by default. An assistant may help engineers analyse information; production actions need restricted, audited interfaces and approval rules.

What is closed-loop network operation?

A loop observes conditions, evaluates them, takes an authorised action and verifies the outcome, with rollback or escalation when needed.

What is shadow mode in network AI?

The model produces recommendations from operational data but cannot change the live network. Engineers evaluate its output before control is enabled.

What is a network digital twin?

A model of part of a network used to explore scenarios and proposed changes. Its value depends on scope, accuracy and calibration.

How do operators prevent unsafe AI changes?

They use least-privilege access, policy checks, staged rollout, rate limits, human approval for high-impact actions, monitoring and tested rollback.

How can an operator prove AI improved service?

It compares defined customer and operational metrics with a credible baseline, accounts for other changes and discloses the evaluation scope.
