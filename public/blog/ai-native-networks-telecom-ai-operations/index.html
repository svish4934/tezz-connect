AI-Native Networks: How Telecom Operators May Use AI to Run Networks

A mobile network is more than the towers and antennas people see. Behind every call, message and data session are radio links, transport routes, core systems, cloud platforms and operational teams working together. As these systems grow more complex, telecom operators are exploring ways to use artificial intelligence to understand network conditions, predict problems and make carefully controlled adjustments. The long-term idea is an AI-native network: one designed to use data and intelligence throughout its operation, rather than treating AI as a separate tool bolted onto a few dashboards. That future is developing in stages. It does not mean that a chatbot will run a national mobile network on its own.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

1. What does an AI-native network actually mean?

There is no single switch that turns a conventional telecom network into an AI-native one. The term describes a direction of architecture and operations: network systems are built to collect useful data, learn from patterns, support decisions and coordinate actions across different parts of the network.

Traditional network operations already use automation. A script can restart a service, a monitoring system can raise an alarm, and a planning tool can forecast traffic. AI adds capabilities such as pattern recognition, anomaly detection, forecasting and decision support. An AI-native approach tries to connect these capabilities to the way the network is designed, managed and improved over time.

The important distinction is between AI-assisted operations and autonomous operations. An assistant may summarise alarms and suggest a likely cause. An autonomous workflow may be permitted to make a bounded change, check whether it worked and reverse it if agreed conditions are not met. The second requires far more than a capable model: it needs trusted data, clear policies, safe interfaces, testing, audit trails and a way to involve people when risk is high.

2. Three ways AI and mobile networks can work together

The AI-RAN Alliance describes three complementary areas. They are useful because people sometimes use “AI network” to mean three very different things.

ApproachMeaningExample
AI for RANAI improves how the radio access network operates.Predicting cell congestion or helping optimise radio resources.
AI and RANAI workloads and radio network workloads share computing infrastructure where performance and isolation requirements allow.Using a common distributed computing platform for telecom functions and selected AI workloads.
AI on RANThe network infrastructure supports AI services closer to connected users and devices.Running an enterprise inference service at a network edge location.

These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. An operator can use AI to forecast faults without hosting customer-facing AI applications at cell sites. It can also explore edge AI services without giving an AI model permission to reconfigure the radio network.

3. What could AI help a telecom operator do?

Forecast traffic before a busy period

Networks experience changing demand. A stadium event, a commuter rush, a festival or a local outage can shift traffic between cells and routes. Forecasting models can combine historical patterns with current measurements to estimate where demand may rise. Engineers can use those estimates to plan capacity, adjust resources or prepare additional support. Forecasts are not guarantees: unusual events, poor input data and sudden behaviour changes can make predictions wrong.

Spot unusual behaviour earlier

Network equipment produces alarms, counters, logs and performance measurements. A model can look for combinations that are unusual even when no single metric has crossed a fixed threshold. For example, a gradual rise in retransmissions alongside a change in handover failures may point to a developing issue. This can help teams investigate sooner, but an anomaly is a clue—not proof of a fault or its cause.

Support radio optimisation

Radio conditions change with user movement, buildings, interference, weather and the number of active devices. AI/ML techniques can support tasks such as beam management, mobility optimisation, load balancing and coverage or capacity optimisation. 3GPP work has introduced AI/ML-related capabilities into the 5G evolution, including work on the NR air interface and network-side optimisation. The exact feature set depends on the release, implementation and operator deployment; it should not be described as one universal AI mode present on every 5G network.

Plan maintenance rather than wait for breakdowns

Models can compare equipment telemetry with past failures and maintenance records to identify components that deserve inspection. That can help prioritise field visits and spare parts. A model should not automatically declare a tower, radio or power system defective based on one weak signal. Good maintenance decisions combine model output with engineering checks and the actual operating environment.

Improve service assurance

A customer's slow connection may involve radio access, backhaul, core-network policy, DNS, an application server or a device. Cross-domain analysis can correlate events that separate monitoring tools might show as unrelated. The goal is to shorten the time needed to find the real cause, not simply to produce more alerts or close tickets faster.

Manage energy more intelligently

Network energy use changes with traffic and site conditions. AI-supported forecasting may help operators adjust selected resources during quieter periods while maintaining coverage, resilience and service quality. The GSMA's Mobile Net Zero 2026 report discusses opportunities such as network sleep modes, dynamic shutdown, load balancing and traffic forecasting. Energy saving must be measured against service impact: switching off capacity in the wrong place or at the wrong time can harm customers.

4. How a safe AI control loop works

A useful way to picture network automation is as a repeating loop. The system observes the network, evaluates what is happening, chooses an action within its authority, applies the change and checks the result.

  1. Observe: collect telemetry such as traffic load, signal quality, alarms, latency, packet loss, power use and service-level indicators.
  2. Understand: compare current conditions with baselines, forecasts, topology and known incidents.
  3. Recommend: identify a likely cause and propose one or more actions with expected benefits and risks.
  4. Check policy: confirm the action is permitted for this network area, time, customer group and risk level.
  5. Act: use a controlled management interface to make the approved change.
  6. Verify: measure whether the change improved the intended service without creating a new problem. Roll back or escalate if it did not.

This is often called a closed loop because the system does not stop after issuing a command. It checks the outcome and uses that evidence for the next decision. ETSI's Zero-touch network and Service Management work explores end-to-end automation, intent-based management, network digital twins and agent-based network assurance. A digital twin can help evaluate a proposed configuration in a model of the network before applying it to live infrastructure, although its usefulness depends on how accurately it represents the real system.

For a high-impact change—such as altering a large group of cells, changing routing policy or affecting emergency-service connectivity—a human approval step may remain essential. The safe level of autonomy depends on the consequence of being wrong.

5. From automation to autonomy: what do the levels mean?

TM Forum's Autonomous Networks framework describes six maturity levels, from Level 0 to Level 5. It is a way to assess autonomy for a particular network scenario or operational domain; it is not a certificate that an entire operator is fully autonomous.

  • Level 0 – Manual: people perform the operational work.
  • Level 1 – Assisted: tools provide basic support, but people make and carry out decisions.
  • Level 2 – Partial automation: specific routine tasks can be automated under defined conditions.
  • Level 3 – Conditional autonomy: the system can handle a wider set of tasks, with human oversight and intervention for exceptions or important decisions.
  • Level 4 – High autonomy: intent-driven, predictive decision-making and closed-loop management can operate across defined services or domains, with people setting policies and handling exceptions.
  • Level 5 – Full autonomy: the framework's highest maturity stage, where the network can manage the relevant scenario with minimal or no human intervention in normal operation.

TM Forum notes that Level 4 is a significant step beyond systems that merely assist people. The distinction matters: an operator may have advanced automation for energy saving in one group of sites while relying on manual processes for a different task. Claims about autonomy should state which process, geography and network domain have been assessed.

6. Where do O-RAN controllers and AI agents fit?

In an Open RAN architecture, the RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) provides a framework for applications that analyse and influence radio network behaviour. Non-real-time RIC functions support longer-horizon optimisation, policy and model management; near-real-time RIC functions support faster control within their defined operating range. Applications commonly called rApps and xApps use these environments to deliver different kinds of optimisation and control.

These components are not magic AI boxes. Their behaviour depends on the data they receive, the interfaces exposed by network equipment, the model or logic inside each application, and the operator's policies. Open interfaces can help multi-vendor integration, but interoperability still needs testing. A system can be technically open and still be difficult to operate if data formats, timing, model lifecycle or vendor implementations differ.

AI agents are another developing area. An agent may gather evidence from several systems, summarise an incident, call approved diagnostic tools or prepare a change request. That does not mean a general-purpose language model should be allowed to issue arbitrary commands to production network equipment. In a responsible design, the agent operates through restricted tools, validates its inputs, records what it did and escalates when it cannot prove that an action is safe.

7. What changes in 5G-Advanced and future 6G?

AI in telecom networks is not waiting for 6G. 3GPP has already worked on AI/ML capabilities for 5G radio systems and network operations. The standards process includes specific use cases such as radio-interface enhancement, mobility optimisation, network slicing and coverage or capacity optimisation. These features are introduced through defined specifications and releases; they are not evidence that every operator has deployed every feature.

For 6G, AI and machine learning are being studied as part of the wider system and radio design. The direction is to consider data collection, model lifecycle, inference, security and network behaviour earlier in the architecture. It is still an evolving standards and research programme. No one can responsibly describe the final 6G AI architecture or promise that a particular feature will be present in every commercial network.

Our earlier articles explain edge computing, submarine cable resilience and IPv6. These technologies solve different problems, but they illustrate why network intelligence must work across radio, transport, internet protocols and computing locations.

8. What could users notice?

Most AI-driven network operations will happen behind the scenes. If the systems work well, a user may experience fewer avoidable service interruptions, more consistent capacity during busy periods, quicker restoration after some faults or better energy management at network sites. Those are possible outcomes, not automatic results of adding AI.

AI does not create extra spectrum, repair a cut fibre cable, remove a building's radio shadow or guarantee that an application server is healthy. It cannot compensate for every shortage in physical infrastructure. A well-designed network with good engineering and reliable operations may outperform a poorly designed network with an expensive AI platform.

For people testing their connection, the same practical checks still matter: compare results at different times, test close to the router, use a wired connection when possible, and separate Wi-Fi problems from wider ISP issues. AI in the operator's network does not change what a speed test measures at your device.

9. The risks: why more autonomy needs stronger governance

Network automation can scale a good decision—but it can also scale a bad one. Important risks include:

  • Bad or incomplete data: missing telemetry, incorrect timestamps or inconsistent network inventories can mislead a model.
  • Model drift: traffic patterns, equipment and software change, so a model that once worked may become unreliable.
  • False confidence: a model can provide a plausible explanation that is wrong. Operators need evidence, confidence thresholds and independent checks.
  • Security threats: attackers may manipulate data, exploit management interfaces or target model pipelines. AI systems add assets that must be protected.
  • Unintended interactions: two individually sensible optimisation loops may conflict when they change shared resources.
  • Vendor lock-in: proprietary data models, orchestration tools or model formats can make it hard to move between suppliers.
  • Privacy and accountability: network data may reveal patterns about users and locations. Operators need appropriate access controls, retention policies and clear responsibility for automated actions.

Good safeguards include staged rollouts, simulation and digital-twin testing, bounded permissions, rate limits, human approval for high-impact changes, explainable decision records, continuous monitoring and tested rollback procedures. Operators should measure not only how often automation succeeds, but also how often it makes a harmful change, how quickly people can intervene and whether customers actually receive a better service.

10. What does this mean for India?

India's mobile networks serve dense cities, highways, villages, industrial sites and regions with very different power, transport and radio conditions. AI-supported traffic forecasting, energy management, fault detection and field-force planning could be useful in such varied environments. But local geography, fibre availability, power reliability, spectrum, site density and equipment support all influence whether a particular use case makes sense.

India is involved in the international standards conversation around future mobile networks. The Department of Telecommunications and TSDSI supported 3GPP RAN meetings on 6G standardisation in Bengaluru in August 2025. That is evidence of India's participation in the standards process—not proof that a nationwide AI-native 6G network has been deployed.

Public claims about an operator's AI maturity should be checked carefully. A demonstration, a pilot in selected cells, an AI-assisted customer-care tool and a validated closed-loop network operation are different achievements. Unless an operator publishes the scope and evidence, it is better not to assume that a feature is active across the country.

11. What is likely to happen next?

Over the next few years, expect more AI-assisted workflows for network planning, fault analysis, energy management and service assurance. Operators are likely to expand automation first in tasks that are repetitive, measurable and reversible. More complex cross-domain actions will require better data foundations, standard interfaces, careful testing and governance.

AI-RAN research will continue exploring how AI can improve radio performance, how AI and radio workloads can share computing resources, and how network infrastructure might support edge AI services. At the same time, standards bodies and industry groups will keep working on interoperability, model management, security and measurable autonomy.

The most realistic future is not a network that never needs engineers. It is a network that gives engineers better information, handles well-understood tasks safely and makes it easier to detect when a situation falls outside its design limits.

Frequently asked questions

What is an AI-native network?

It is a network designed to use data, AI and automation across its operation and lifecycle, rather than adding isolated AI tools to existing systems. The degree of autonomy can differ by task and network domain.

Will AI make mobile internet faster?

It may help operators manage resources and respond to congestion, but it cannot guarantee faster speeds. Spectrum, coverage, backhaul, device capability, server performance and local demand still matter.

What is the difference between AI for RAN and AI on RAN?

AI for RAN uses AI to improve radio network functions. AI on RAN uses network infrastructure to support AI services closer to users or devices. AI and RAN refers to sharing infrastructure for both types of workloads.

Are 5G networks already using AI?

AI/ML capabilities have been specified for selected 5G-related use cases, and operators may deploy AI tools in operations. Actual features vary by network, software release, vendor and operator.

Does AI run the entire mobile network without people?

No. Many current systems assist people or automate narrow tasks. High autonomy is a staged goal, and human policy, supervision and exception handling remain important.

What are rApps and xApps?

They are application types used with O-RAN RAN Intelligent Controller environments. They support different optimisation and control functions at non-real-time and near-real-time layers.

Can AI predict every network outage?

No. Models can identify patterns associated with some faults, but unexpected events, missing data and new failure modes can defeat predictions.

Will AI-native networks arrive with 6G?

AI is being studied for future 6G design, but AI use in networks already exists. The final 6G standards and deployment choices are still evolving.

Can AI reduce telecom energy consumption?

It can help forecast demand and optimise selected resources, including sleep modes and load balancing. Savings must be balanced against coverage, resilience and service quality.

Does an AI chatbot control a telecom tower?

Not by default. A chatbot may help an engineer understand information or prepare a request. Any production action should use restricted, audited interfaces and operator-defined approval rules.
