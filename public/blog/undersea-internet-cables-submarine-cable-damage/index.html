Undersea Internet Cables: What Happens When a Submarine Cable Is Damaged?

A video call, a bank transfer, a cloud backup and a message sent across the world may all depend on infrastructure most people never see: fibre-optic cables laid across the seabed. These links connect continents and carry more than 99% of international data exchanges. When one is damaged, the internet does not simply switch off. Network operators try to move traffic onto other routes, while specialist marine crews locate and repair the fault. The outcome depends on where the cable is, how much spare capacity exists elsewhere and how quickly a repair vessel can reach it.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

When someone in Mumbai opens a website hosted in another country, the request may travel through a chain of local fibre, provider networks, internet exchanges, international gateways and a submarine cable system. The cable itself is out of sight, often thousands of metres below the sea. Yet these systems are among the most important pieces of infrastructure connecting the world's digital economy.

A damaged submarine cable does not automatically switch off the internet. Network operators can often move traffic onto other routes while specialist marine crews arrange a repair. Whether customers notice a disruption depends on the geography of the fault, the capacity and diversity of alternative paths, the networks involved and the services they are trying to reach. This guide explains the physical system, how faults are found and repaired, what users may experience, and how operators can build resilience.

1. Why the global internet still depends on the ocean floor

Submarine fibre-optic cables connect landing stations on coastlines and carry the overwhelming majority of international data traffic. They provide very high capacity and relatively low latency for intercontinental communication. Satellites, microwave links and terrestrial routes are important too, but they do not offer a like-for-like replacement for the capacity of the global submarine cable network.

Think of a cable as one segment in a much larger journey. A video call from India to Europe might travel over Wi-Fi to a home router, through an access network, across an Indian backbone, through an international gateway and then over one or more submarine systems before reaching the destination network. The return traffic may follow a different route. The path can change as networks respond to congestion, failures and commercial routing decisions.

Submarine systems also support services that do not look like traditional web browsing: cloud storage, financial transactions, software updates, enterprise networks, content delivery, voice and video communications, and data exchanged between data centres. A fault can therefore have different effects on different applications.

2. What a submarine cable actually contains

The data-carrying element is optical fibre: thin strands of glass that guide pulses of light. A system contains fibre pairs and terminal equipment at its landing stations. Optical transmitters encode information into light; receivers interpret it at the other end. Long routes use undersea optical amplifiers or repeaters to compensate for signal loss. These devices are engineered to operate for long periods in a difficult environment.

The cable is not constructed identically along its entire length. Near shore and in areas exposed to fishing and anchoring, it may have additional armour and may be buried beneath the seabed where conditions permit. Deep-ocean sections can use a lighter construction because they face different risks. The cable design must account for water depth, seabed type, route geometry, handling loads and the need to protect the optical fibres.

Many systems also carry a power conductor. The optical fibres carry data, while electrical power supplied from terminal stations supports repeaters and other undersea equipment. Branching units can connect a main trunk to additional landing points. A cable system is therefore a complete engineered installation, not simply a long glass strand.

At each end, a cable landing station houses equipment that connects the submarine system to terrestrial networks. The route from the landing station to a data centre or internet exchange matters too. Two submarine cables that arrive at the same coastal area may still share a vulnerable terrestrial corridor, power dependency or facility. True resilience has to be assessed end to end.

3. What causes a cable fault?

Industry guidance from the International Cable Protection Committee (ICPC) commonly attributes around 70–80% of submarine cable faults to human activities, especially commercial fishing and ships' anchors. The ICPC describes roughly 150–200 faults worldwide in a typical year. These are broad industry estimates; annual totals and definitions can differ between sources and reporting periods.

Fishing equipment can snag a cable, drag it or apply enough force to damage its protective layers. An anchor that is dropped or dragged across a cable route can cut or crush it. This is one reason cable routes are charted and why maritime users are asked to observe designated protection areas and safe practices.

Natural hazards also matter. Earthquakes can displace the seabed, underwater landslides can bury or strain cables, and seabed movement can create tension or abrasion. Other faults can arise from component degradation, manufacturing defects, installation damage or failures in terminal and power equipment. Storms and coastal processes can be particularly relevant in shallow water.

A confirmed service outage is not proof of sabotage. Determining the cause of a specific incident requires evidence, technical investigation and, where appropriate, an assessment by relevant authorities. Operators should distinguish what is known—such as a loss of signal on a named system—from what remains unconfirmed about the cause.

4. How operators detect and locate a fault

Submarine systems are monitored from their terminal stations. Engineers may see a sudden loss of optical signal, a change in received power, an alarm from a repeater or a power-feed anomaly. Network monitoring can also show traffic shifting away from a route or a loss of reachability to particular destinations.

For many fibre faults, optical time-domain reflectometry (OTDR) helps estimate the distance to a discontinuity by analysing reflected light. The measurement is interpreted alongside the cable route database, system design and terminal readings. It provides a distance along the cable, not necessarily an exact position on a map. Engineers convert the measurement into a likely repair area using route information and system-specific data.

Different fault types need different tests. A fibre break, a power conductor fault, a repeater problem and a terrestrial landing-station failure are not interchangeable. Early diagnosis is important because it informs the repair method, vessel requirements and the likely customer impact.

Network operators do not have to wait for the ship to finish its work before restoring traffic. Their routing teams can assess alternate paths, adjust routing policies and move traffic where capacity and commercial agreements permit. Physical repair and service restoration are related but separate workstreams.

5. How a specialist vessel repairs the cable

A repair normally involves a specialist cable ship, trained marine technicians, cable-handling equipment and coordination with the system owner and relevant authorities. The vessel must be suitable for the route and fault conditions. It may need to travel from another repair job or port, so the time before work begins can be significant.

Once at the estimated location, the crew uses navigation data, sonar and cable-recovery tools to find the cable. Depending on water depth and conditions, a grapnel may be towed along the seabed to catch and lift the cable. Remotely operated vehicles and other equipment may be used where appropriate. The method depends on the seabed, burial, cable design and safety constraints.

The recovered cable is brought aboard and the damaged portion is examined. Technicians cut away the faulty section, prepare the cable ends and join in a replacement length. The optical fibres are spliced with precision equipment; any relevant electrical conductor and protective layers also have to be restored. The repaired section is tested before the cable is returned to the seabed.

When the repair is complete, the crew lowers the cable carefully, managing slack and the route so that the repaired section rests safely. The system operator then performs further tests and coordinates the return to normal service. It is a marine engineering operation as well as a fibre-optic repair.

6. Why repair timelines vary so much

There is no universal repair duration. ITU technical guidance has cited a typical mean repair-time estimate of one to three weeks for a wet-plant fault, but an individual incident may take less or considerably longer. That estimate should not be interpreted as a service-restoration promise: traffic may be rerouted before the cable is physically repaired.

7. What happens to internet traffic while a cable is out?

Internet routing is designed to adapt to changes in network reachability. If a route becomes unavailable, routers can select another path based on routing information and network policy. Operators may also deliberately move traffic to a backup route before or during a repair. The actual outcome depends on how networks are interconnected and how much capacity remains available.

Alternative paths can be longer. A packet may travel through a different landing station or another country before reaching its destination. That can increase round-trip time, commonly called latency. If many networks shift traffic onto the same remaining links, congestion can reduce throughput or increase packet loss and jitter.

Not every application will be affected equally. A locally hosted service may continue to work normally while a service hosted overseas becomes slower. A content delivery network may serve cached material from a nearby location, while a live transaction still needs to reach a distant origin. Cloud providers and large networks may have their own route diversity that differs from a smaller ISP's arrangements.

Rerouting also depends on capacity, not just the existence of another cable. A backup route that is already busy may not absorb all displaced traffic without performance changes. Some networks may have multiple physical routes but lack the commercial transit, peering or configuration needed to use them effectively.

8. Can one damaged cable disconnect a country?

Usually, a single cable fault does not disconnect an entire country. Countries and providers commonly use multiple submarine systems, terrestrial links and upstream networks. However, redundancy is uneven. A region with fewer landing points or a network with limited upstream diversity may be more exposed than a large provider with several independent routes.

Several cables can also share a geographic chokepoint. A cluster of faults in one region, a major power or landing-station issue, or damage to a shared terrestrial backhaul can affect multiple systems at once. This is why counting cable names is not enough to measure resilience.

It is more accurate to ask which networks and destinations have lost capacity, what alternative routes are available, and whether the remaining infrastructure can carry the displaced traffic. A country may remain connected while some providers, regions or international services experience serious degradation.

9. What the cable network means for India

India's international connectivity depends on submarine systems that land at coastal locations and connect to domestic fibre networks. Mumbai and Chennai are important landing areas. A cable fault can affect access to overseas cloud platforms, business applications, game servers, websites and communication services, but the impact depends on the provider's routing and available alternatives.

A Ministry of Communications response in the Lok Sabha dated 18 March 2026 listed India Europe Xpress (IEX) in Mumbai, SEA-ME-WE-6 in Mumbai and Chennai, 2Africa in Mumbai and Raman Cable in Mumbai as systems under commissioning at that time. It also listed systems in planning with applications submitted. These are historical statuses reported on that date, not a live list of systems currently in service; commissioning status can change.

For users in Mumbai, Thane, Navi Mumbai and elsewhere, an international route issue may appear as slow access to selected overseas destinations while local websites continue to load. But a user's location alone cannot identify the cable involved. The ISP's upstream arrangements, destination network and current routing determine the path.

India's resilience depends on more than submarine capacity. Domestic backhaul from landing stations, diverse routes to data centres, internet exchange capacity, power resilience, provider interconnection and access to repair resources all contribute. Multiple cables landing in one broad coastal region do not automatically provide complete independence if they share inland dependencies.

10. How to tell whether a problem might be international

Home users cannot diagnose a seabed fault from a single speed test. A slow result may come from Wi-Fi interference, a busy home network, the ISP's access segment, a congested test server, a remote service or many other causes. The following checks can help you collect useful evidence without jumping to conclusions.

  1. Compare several services. Check more than one website or app, including services you know are hosted or delivered from different locations. A single failing website may simply have its own outage.
  2. Compare local and international destinations carefully. A difference can be a clue, but hosting location and network route are not always obvious.
  3. Test more than one access network. If available, compare your fixed broadband with mobile data or another provider. They may use different upstream routes.
  4. Record the time and symptoms. Note affected services, error messages, latency and whether the issue is continuous or intermittent.
  5. Check provider notices. Look for confirmed maintenance or incident updates from your ISP and the service provider.
  6. Contact your ISP with evidence. Ask whether the issue is local, regional or upstream and whether there is a known incident.

Traceroute can show some of the routers a packet encounters, but it is not a definitive cable detector. Routers may filter probes, use load balancing or hide internal topology. A route that appears to stop at one hop does not prove that a submarine cable is broken.

11. What network operators can do to improve resilience

Resilience is a combination of physical diversity, network engineering, operational readiness and cooperation. Important practices include:

  • Geographically diverse cable routes: avoid placing all international capacity through one corridor where practical.
  • Independent landing facilities: assess whether backup systems share power, buildings, ducts or inland fibre.
  • Multiple upstream providers: arrange usable transit and peering alternatives, not just theoretical physical routes.
  • Capacity planning: ensure backup paths can carry a meaningful share of traffic during an outage.
  • Protection and charting: use appropriate burial and armouring and communicate cable locations to maritime stakeholders.
  • Repair readiness: maintain maintenance agreements, spare cable, trained staff and access to suitable vessels.
  • Monitoring and exercises: rehearse traffic failover and test whether routing policies work under realistic failure conditions.
  • Clear incident communication: publish confirmed scope, affected services and realistic updates without speculating about causes.

Operators should test more than whether a backup route exists. They should measure how quickly traffic moves, whether routing converges correctly, whether capacity is sufficient and whether customer-facing services remain usable. A route-diversity diagram is only useful if the network can actually use the alternate path during a real incident.

12. Do satellites make submarine cables unnecessary?

Satellite networks can add resilience and reach remote locations where terrestrial infrastructure is limited. They can support emergency communication and provide alternative connectivity when some ground routes are unavailable. However, satellite systems have different capacity, latency, coverage, spectrum and cost characteristics from fibre.

Submarine cables remain essential for high-volume intercontinental data exchange. Satellite links are best understood as a complementary layer. A robust communications ecosystem uses several technologies because each has different strengths, limitations and failure modes.

13. Common myths about submarine cable damage

Myth: One broken cable turns off the internet.

Reality: Traffic can often be rerouted, although backup capacity may be limited and performance can degrade.

Myth: Every cable fault is sabotage.

Reality: Industry reporting identifies fishing and anchoring as major causes. A particular incident needs evidence before its cause can be stated.

Myth: A repair ship simply ties the cable back together.

Reality: Crews locate and recover the cable, remove the damaged section, splice replacement fibre, restore relevant conductors, test the system and carefully redeploy it.

Myth: A speed test identifies a broken undersea cable.

Reality: A speed test measures performance to a particular server. It cannot identify the physical cause of a slowdown.

Myth: More cables always mean complete redundancy.

Reality: Routes can share landing stations, terrestrial backhaul, power and geographic chokepoints. End-to-end diversity matters.

14. A practical checklist during a reported cable incident

For home and mobile users:

  • Do not reset your router repeatedly if local connectivity is otherwise stable.
  • Check whether the issue affects one app, several overseas services or all internet access.
  • Compare fixed broadband and mobile data if possible.
  • Save timestamps, error messages and test results.
  • Follow confirmed ISP and service-provider updates.

For small businesses:

  • Identify which cloud, payment, voice and business services depend on overseas endpoints.
  • Ask providers about alternate routes, failover and incident status.
  • Keep a documented continuity procedure for essential services.
  • Avoid changing DNS, firewall or routing settings without a clear diagnosis.

For network teams:

  • Check upstream reachability, BGP announcements, latency, loss and capacity.
  • Confirm whether alternate routes are genuinely independent and provisioned.
  • Monitor convergence and application performance after failover.
  • Separate confirmed technical facts from unverified incident theories.

15. The bigger lesson: resilience is built before the fault

When a submarine cable fails, network operators try to keep traffic moving while marine crews work to restore the physical system. The public may see slower international services, higher latency or no noticeable change. The difference is determined by route diversity, spare capacity, provider interconnection, landing-station resilience and the speed of repair logistics.

The global internet is not unbreakable, but it is not a single cable either. It is a network of networks with multiple physical paths and operational teams working across borders. Understanding that distinction helps users interpret outages accurately—and reminds operators that resilience must be designed, funded and tested before an incident occurs.

Frequently asked questions

How much international internet traffic uses submarine cables?

ITU describes submarine cables as carrying more than 99% of international data exchanges. This does not mean that every local internet connection or every byte within a country travels underwater.

How many submarine cable faults occur each year?

ICPC industry guidance commonly cites roughly 150–200 faults worldwide in a typical year. Counts vary with reporting periods and definitions.

What is the most common cause of submarine cable damage?

Commercial fishing activity and ships' anchors are major causes of accidental faults. Natural seabed movement and equipment failures also occur.

How are damaged submarine cables located?

Engineers use terminal measurements, optical testing and route records to estimate the fault distance and identify a repair area. The repair ship then uses navigation and seabed equipment to locate the cable.

How long does it take to repair a submarine cable?

ITU technical guidance cites a typical mean estimate of one to three weeks for a wet-plant fault, but the actual time depends on vessel availability, weather, depth, fault type and permissions.

Can one broken cable disconnect India?

Not automatically. Other cable systems and terrestrial routes may carry traffic, but the impact depends on the affected network's route diversity and remaining capacity.

Why might overseas websites be slow while Indian websites work?

Different services can use different hosting locations, content delivery networks and network paths. An international route issue is one possible explanation, but it is not the only one.

Can a traceroute prove a submarine cable is broken?

No. Traceroute can provide clues about network paths, but filtering, load balancing and hidden network topology limit what it can prove.

Do submarine cables carry electricity?

Many systems include a power conductor that supplies undersea repeaters and equipment. The optical fibres carry data as light; the power conductor serves the undersea electronics.

Will satellite internet replace undersea cables?

Satellite connectivity can provide useful backup and reach remote locations, but its capacity, latency and economics differ from fibre. It complements rather than simply replaces submarine cables.

Are all submarine cable faults caused by human activity?

No. Fishing and anchoring account for a large share in industry estimates, but natural hazards and equipment faults also cause damage.

What should I do if I suspect an international connectivity problem?

Compare multiple services and access networks, record symptoms and timestamps, check provider notices and contact your ISP. Do not assume a cable fault from one speed test.
