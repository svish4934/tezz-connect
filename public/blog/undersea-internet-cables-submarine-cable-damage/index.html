Undersea Internet Cables: What Happens When a Submarine Cable Is Damaged?

A video call, a bank transfer, a cloud backup and a message sent across the world may all depend on infrastructure most people never see: fibre-optic cables laid across the seabed. These links connect continents and carry more than 99% of international data exchanges. When one is damaged, the internet does not simply switch off. Network operators try to move traffic onto other routes, while specialist marine crews locate and repair the fault. The outcome depends on where the cable is, how much spare capacity exists elsewhere and how quickly a repair vessel can reach it.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

Imagine sending a photograph from Mumbai to a friend in Europe. Your phone sends it over a local wireless or fibre connection to your provider. From there, the data may cross several networks and eventually travel through a submarine cable system between continents. The cable is not a single exposed glass thread: it is a carefully engineered assembly of optical fibres, protective layers and power equipment designed for a demanding underwater environment.

Submarine cable faults are not rare, but they are also not evidence that the global internet is on the verge of collapse. The network is built from many systems, landing stations and terrestrial links. That diversity can keep traffic moving when one route fails. It cannot guarantee that every region has enough alternative capacity or that users will notice no change.

1. Why does the internet need cables under the sea?

Satellites are useful for reaching remote places, broadcasting and providing connectivity where terrestrial infrastructure is difficult to build. But the vast majority of international data exchanges travel through submarine fibre-optic cables. Fibre offers enormous capacity, low signal delay over long distances and an efficient way to connect the networks of different countries.

These cables link landing stations on coastlines. At a landing station, the undersea system connects to equipment and terrestrial networks on land. From there, traffic can reach internet service providers, mobile operators, data centres, cloud platforms, businesses and public services.

The cable route is only one part of the journey. A packet may also cross metro fibre, national backbones, internet exchanges, data-centre networks and the access network serving a home or phone. That is why a problem in an international cable can affect a service even when the local broadband line and Wi-Fi router are working normally.

2. What is inside a submarine internet cable?

The central element is optical fibre: very thin strands of glass that carry information as pulses of light. A modern system can contain multiple fibre pairs, with optical equipment at the ends transmitting and receiving signals. Repeaters may be installed along long routes to amplify or regenerate optical signals so that data can travel across ocean basins.

The cable's physical construction changes with the environment. In shallow water, where fishing gear and anchors are more likely to reach the seabed, cables may use additional armour and may be buried beneath the seabed where conditions allow. In deep ocean, the cable can be lighter because it is less exposed to many human activities, although it still has to withstand pressure, movement and geological hazards.

Some submarine systems also include branching units that allow a cable route to connect to more than one landing point. The exact design depends on the system. It is therefore inaccurate to imagine every cable as one identical armoured rope stretching from one beach to another.

There is another detail that surprises many people: repeaters and other undersea equipment can receive electrical power from the cable system. The optical fibres carry data, while a separate conductor and power-feeding equipment support the undersea electronics. A fault investigation must identify whether the issue is in the fibre path, power system, joint or another component.

3. How often are submarine cables damaged?

The International Cable Protection Committee (ICPC) describes roughly 150 to 200 submarine cable faults worldwide in a typical year. Its published guidance attributes around 70–80% of faults to human activities, especially commercial fishing and ships' anchors. ITU reported more than 170 repairs worldwide in 2025. The precise annual count varies by source, reporting period and whether the measure is faults, repairs or incidents.

Fishing gear can snag a cable, while an anchor that is dropped or dragged across the seabed can cut or damage it. Other causes include underwater landslides, earthquakes, seabed movement, abrasion, storms affecting shallow-water areas and failures in cable equipment. Cables are routed and protected to reduce these risks, but no engineering design can remove every hazard across thousands of kilometres of seabed.

It is important to separate a confirmed fault from a claim about its cause. A cable outage by itself does not prove sabotage. Determining whether an incident was accidental, natural or deliberate requires evidence and investigation by the relevant authorities and operators. The ICPC also notes that it does not investigate or independently confirm the cause of individual cable damage.

4. What happens when a cable breaks?

The first signs may be an alarm in the cable operator's monitoring system, a loss of optical signal, a power-feed anomaly or reports that traffic on a route has degraded. Engineers compare telemetry from the cable terminals and network equipment to narrow down the affected section. The initial diagnosis is not necessarily an exact GPS location on the seabed.

For a fibre fault, specialist test equipment can estimate the distance to the break by measuring the optical signal response. Engineers combine those measurements with cable route records, system design information and other evidence to identify a likely repair area. A fault in a power conductor or repeater system may require a different diagnostic approach.

At the same time, network operators assess what the disruption means for traffic. They may withdraw or de-preference an affected route, change routing announcements, or move traffic to another cable system or terrestrial path. This is a separate activity from physically repairing the cable: network engineers work to maintain service while marine teams prepare the repair.

Rerouting is not always invisible. Alternative paths may be longer, more congested or have less available capacity. Users may experience higher latency, slower downloads, unstable connections to particular services or no obvious change at all. The impact depends on the route choices and capacity available to each network and service.

5. How does a repair ship find and lift a damaged cable?

Submarine cable repairs are carried out by specialist cable ships and trained marine crews. The operator coordinates the repair with the relevant maintenance arrangements, vessel availability, port logistics, permits and weather conditions. The ship travels to the estimated fault area and uses navigation, sonar and cable-handling equipment to locate the cable.

In a typical repair in deep water, a grapnel or other recovery tool may be towed along the seabed to catch and lift the cable. In shallower water, remotely operated vehicles and other methods may be used where appropriate. The exact method depends on the water depth, seabed, cable construction, location of the fault and the risks of the operation.

Once the cable is recovered to the ship, the crew identifies and cuts out the damaged section. The cable ends are prepared and joined to a replacement length using specialist equipment. Optical fibres are carefully spliced, and the repaired section is tested. The crew may then lower the repaired cable back to the seabed, often leaving a carefully managed slack section to accommodate the route and seabed conditions.

It is not simply a matter of tying two ends together. The repair has to restore the optical path and, where relevant, the electrical power conductor and protective structure. The work is performed in a controlled environment on board the vessel, with extensive testing before the system is returned to service.

6. Why can a repair take days or weeks?

There is no universal repair clock. ITU's technical guidance gives a typical mean repair-time estimate of one to three weeks for a wet-plant fault, while stressing that the result depends on the fault location, water depth, ship availability, cause of damage and weather. Some repairs can be faster; difficult locations and complicated logistics can take longer.

  • Finding the fault: the operator must interpret system measurements and establish the likely location.
  • Getting a vessel there: a suitable repair ship may already be at sea, assigned to another job or positioned far from the fault.
  • Weather and sea state: safe cable handling requires suitable conditions. Rough seas can delay operations.
  • Water depth and seabed: deep-water recovery, rocky terrain, slopes and burial can make the work more complex.
  • Permissions and coordination: repairs may involve territorial waters, national authorities, port access and coordination among cable owners.
  • Testing and restoration: the repaired system must be checked before normal service is declared.

The one-to-three-week figure is a technical estimate, not a guarantee for every incident. It describes the repair process, not necessarily the duration of customer impact: traffic may be rerouted before the physical cable is fixed.

7. Does one broken cable disconnect a country?

Usually, one cable fault does not disconnect an entire country from the internet. International connectivity is typically supported by multiple cable systems, landing points, providers and terrestrial routes. When a system fails, networks can use other available paths if they have capacity and suitable interconnection arrangements.

But redundancy is not the same as unlimited backup. If several systems share a narrow geographic corridor, a regional hazard can affect more than one route. If the remaining paths are already busy, rerouted traffic may cause congestion. Some networks or destinations may have fewer alternatives than others. A country can remain online while certain services become slower or less reliable.

There can also be uneven effects within the same country. One provider may have diverse international routes and another may depend heavily on a smaller set of upstream connections. A cloud service may use a different network path from a video platform or a bank. This is why broad statements such as “the whole country has lost internet” should be checked against actual provider and service reports.

8. What does this mean for India?

India's international internet connectivity depends on submarine cable systems that land at coastal locations and connect to domestic networks. Mumbai and Chennai are important cable landing locations, and the Government of India continues to report new systems and expansions in its official updates.

In a Lok Sabha response dated 18 March 2026, the Ministry of Communications listed four submarine cable systems under commissioning at cable landing stations: India Europe Xpress (IEX) in Mumbai; SEA-ME-WE-6 in Mumbai and Chennai; 2Africa in Mumbai; and Raman Cable in Mumbai. The same response listed three additional systems in planning, with applications submitted: Wyasa in Chennai, Indian Ocean Cable (IOC) in Raigad, Maharashtra, and WABAN in Mumbai. These were the statuses reported at that date; commissioning and planning status can change, so this list should not be treated as a live operational inventory.

For users in Mumbai, Thane, Navi Mumbai or elsewhere in India, a fault on an international route could affect access to overseas websites, cloud platforms, game servers or business systems. It does not mean that every local connection will fail. Domestic services hosted within India may follow different routes, and providers may shift international traffic to other systems.

India's resilience depends not only on the number of cables but also on route diversity, landing-station diversity, domestic backhaul, peering, provider choices, repair capability and coordination between operators and authorities. Several cables landing in the same broad region may still share some geographic or terrestrial dependencies.

9. How can network operators make the system more resilient?

There is no single fix for cable resilience. Operators and policymakers can reduce risk and improve recovery through a combination of engineering, planning and cooperation:

  • Diverse routes: avoid relying on one cable corridor or one landing location where practical.
  • More than one upstream path: arrange interconnection and capacity so traffic can move when a route fails.
  • Seabed protection: use burial and suitable cable armouring in higher-risk areas where seabed conditions permit.
  • Maritime awareness: improve charting, communication and awareness for fishing, anchoring and offshore activities.
  • Repair readiness: maintain repair agreements, spare cable, trained crews and access to suitable vessels.
  • Cooperation: coordinate across cable owners, telecom operators, governments, ports and marine authorities.
  • Transparent incident communication: share confirmed service-impact information without speculating about causes.

Route diversity must be assessed end to end. A second submarine cable is less useful as a backup if both systems depend on the same vulnerable landing station, power supply, terrestrial fibre corridor or upstream provider. Resilience is a property of the whole connectivity chain, not simply a count of lines on a map.

10. What can an ordinary internet user do during a cable disruption?

Most home users cannot repair or reroute an international cable, and changing Wi-Fi settings will not fix a fault in an ocean route. But a few sensible checks can help distinguish a local problem from a wider service disruption:

  • Check whether multiple websites or apps are affected, rather than relying on one destination.
  • Compare a service hosted locally with one hosted overseas, while remembering that hosting location alone does not prove the route.
  • Check your provider's service notices and credible network-status updates.
  • If possible, compare access through another provider or mobile network. Different networks may use different upstream routes.
  • Record the time, affected services and any error messages before contacting your ISP.

A speed test can show how your connection performs to a particular test server at that moment. It cannot identify a damaged submarine cable by itself. Likewise, a traceroute can provide clues about a path, but filtering, load balancing and network configuration can make the output incomplete or misleading.

11. Will satellites replace submarine cables?

Satellite networks can add valuable resilience, especially for remote communities, emergency communications and locations without diverse terrestrial routes. They can provide an alternative path when some ground infrastructure is unavailable. But satellite systems have different capacity, latency, coverage, spectrum and cost characteristics from fibre systems.

Submarine cables continue to provide extremely high-capacity links between continents. Satellite connectivity is better understood as a complementary layer, not a simple replacement for the global fibre network. A resilient internet uses multiple technologies because each has different strengths and failure modes.

12. The real lesson: the internet is built to reroute, not to be unbreakable

When a submarine cable is damaged, two difficult jobs begin. Network operators work to keep traffic moving over the routes that remain, while marine engineers locate, recover and repair the physical cable. These activities can happen in parallel, and the customer impact may be smaller or larger than the repair itself suggests.

Multiple cables and alternative routes make the global internet more resilient, but capacity, geography, landing stations and domestic backhaul all matter. A fault can cause localised disruption, higher latency or congestion without disconnecting an entire country. And although many faults are linked to everyday maritime activity, the cause of a specific incident should never be guessed from an outage alone.

The next time an international service becomes slow, remember that the path between two screens may cross thousands of kilometres of fibre resting on the ocean floor. The internet feels wireless at the edge. Much of its global reach still depends on cables under the sea.

Frequently asked questions

How much internet traffic travels through submarine cables?

ITU describes submarine cables as carrying more than 99% of international data exchanges. The figure refers to international connectivity, not every byte of local internet traffic inside each country.

How many submarine cable faults happen each year?

The ICPC reports approximately 150–200 faults annually in recent industry guidance. The count varies by year and by whether a source measures faults, repairs or reported incidents.

What usually damages undersea internet cables?

Commercial fishing activity and ships' anchors are leading causes of accidental damage. Natural hazards, seabed movement, abrasion and equipment failures can also cause faults.

How do engineers repair a broken submarine cable?

A specialist vessel travels to the estimated fault area, locates and recovers the cable, removes the damaged section, splices in a replacement and tests the repaired system before lowering it back to the seabed.

How long does a submarine cable repair take?

ITU technical guidance gives a typical mean estimate of one to three weeks for a wet-plant repair. Actual time depends on location, depth, weather, vessel availability, damage and permissions.

Will a broken submarine cable shut down the internet in India?

Not automatically. Traffic may be rerouted over other systems, but the effect depends on route diversity, available capacity, provider arrangements and the location of the fault.

Can a cable fault make my internet slower but not disconnect it?

Yes. Rerouted traffic may travel farther or use more congested links. Some users may notice higher latency or slower access to particular services while other connections remain normal.

Can a speed test tell me if an undersea cable is broken?

No. A speed test measures performance to its chosen test server. It cannot identify the physical cause of a network problem or prove that a submarine cable has failed.

Are submarine cable outages usually caused by sabotage?

No such conclusion should be drawn from an outage alone. Industry guidance identifies accidental human activity, especially fishing and anchoring, as common causes. A specific incident requires evidence and an investigation.

Will satellite internet replace submarine cables?

Satellites can provide useful backup and reach remote locations, but their capacity, latency and economics differ from fibre. They are complementary technologies rather than a straightforward replacement.
