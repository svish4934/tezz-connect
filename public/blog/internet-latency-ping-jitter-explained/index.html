Internet Latency, Ping and Jitter Explained: What Makes a Connection Feel Slow?

Understand internet latency, ping, jitter and packet loss, how they affect gaming, calls and browsing, how to measure them, and what you can do to improve connection responsiveness.

By Suraj Vishwakarma · Published by TEZZ CONNECT

Latency, ping and jitter are related—but they are not identical

When a video call freezes, a game feels delayed or a website takes a moment to respond, it is tempting to blame download speed. But many frustrating internet problems are caused by delay and instability rather than a lack of raw bandwidth. A connection can download a large file quickly and still feel sluggish when you click a link, move through a game or speak during a call.

Three terms help describe this experience: latency, ping and jitter. Latency is the time data takes to travel through a network path. Ping is a common diagnostic measurement that sends a small packet to a destination and measures the round-trip response time. Jitter describes variation in packet delay over time. Packet loss is a separate but closely related problem: some packets never arrive.

These measurements answer different questions. Download speed tells you how much data can arrive per second under test conditions. Latency tells you how long a packet takes to make a journey. Jitter tells you whether packet timing is consistent. Packet loss tells you whether delivery is failing. Understanding all four gives a much clearer picture of connection quality than a single speed number.

This guide explains what each metric means, why it matters for games and calls, how to test it without drawing the wrong conclusion, and what steps can improve responsiveness. The goal is not to chase the lowest number on a dashboard. It is to identify where delay is introduced and whether it affects the applications you use.

What is internet latency?

Latency is the time delay between an action and the corresponding network response. When you open a page, your device sends requests and waits for responses. When you play an online game, your device exchanges state updates with a server. When you speak in a video meeting, audio packets travel to other participants and return audio follows a separate path. Every part of those exchanges takes time.

Latency is commonly expressed in milliseconds (ms), where one millisecond is one thousandth of a second. A small number can still matter because interactive applications exchange many messages. If a game waits for a server response before confirming an action, extra delay can make controls feel less immediate. In a conversation, delay can cause people to interrupt each other because each speaker hears the other later than expected.

Network latency has several contributors. Data must be transmitted over a physical medium, processed by network equipment, queued when links are busy and routed across networks. The destination may be hundreds or thousands of kilometres away. The route can include access equipment, aggregation routers, transit networks, internet exchanges, content delivery systems and the destination service itself.

Distance creates a physical lower bound. Signals cannot travel faster than the propagation speed of the medium. Fibre carries light, but light in glass travels slower than it does in a vacuum, and real routes are not perfectly straight. A packet travelling between continents may cover a much longer cable path than the map distance suggests. No router setting can remove that physical propagation time.

Latency also depends on the type of path. A nearby server reached through a well-provisioned fibre network may respond quickly. A distant server reached through congested interconnection may respond more slowly. The access technology matters, but it is only one part of the end-to-end route.

What does ping measure?

Ping is both the name of a diagnostic utility and a familiar shorthand for a round-trip time measurement. A typical ping test sends an Internet Control Message Protocol (ICMP) Echo Request to a destination. If the destination responds with an Echo Reply, the tool calculates how long the round trip took. Results are commonly displayed in milliseconds.

A ping result is not a direct measurement of every application's delay. It measures the path used by that particular probe, under the conditions at that moment. Some hosts do not answer ICMP requests, and some networks treat diagnostic packets differently from ordinary application traffic. A failed ping therefore does not automatically mean that a website or game is unreachable.

Ping is useful because it is simple and repeatable. You can compare a local router, a provider gateway, a nearby public server and a destination relevant to your work. If the router responds consistently but a remote destination does not, the problem may lie beyond the Wi-Fi link. If ping to the router itself is erratic, investigate the local network first.

Many tools report minimum, average and maximum ping. The average can hide occasional spikes. A sequence such as 18, 19, 20, 21, 140 and 19 ms may have a reasonable average but one very noticeable delay event. Look at the distribution and the pattern, not only one average.

What is jitter?

Jitter is variation in packet delay. Imagine sending packets at regular intervals. If they reach the receiver at roughly the same intervals, timing is predictable. If some arrive quickly and others arrive much later, the receiver experiences variable delay. Different standards and tools calculate jitter in different ways, so two dashboards may not display identical values.

Real-time applications are sensitive to timing. A voice call cannot wait indefinitely for a delayed audio packet; the application may use a jitter buffer to hold packets briefly and play them in order. A larger buffer can smooth variation but adds delay. If packets arrive too late, the application may discard them, conceal the missing audio or produce a brief glitch.

Video meetings can respond to jitter by changing quality, buffering or dropping frames. Games may interpolate between updates, but irregular delivery can make movement appear inconsistent. Jitter is especially noticeable when it changes suddenly, even if the average latency remains acceptable.

Jitter is not simply the highest ping minus the lowest ping in every measurement system. Some tools calculate the difference between consecutive packet delays; others report a statistical measure over a window. When comparing services, check how the metric is defined and measured.

Packet loss: when data does not arrive

Packet loss occurs when packets sent across a network do not reach their intended destination. Networks can discard packets because of congestion, transmission errors, faulty equipment, radio interference, routing changes or overloaded systems. Some loss can also occur because a device intentionally rate-limits diagnostic probes.

Applications respond differently to loss. Web and file-transfer protocols can retransmit missing data, but retransmissions take time. Real-time audio and games may not be able to wait for a late packet, so missing information can appear as broken audio, frozen frames, rubber-banding or sudden movement. A small percentage of loss can be highly visible in a sensitive application.

Do not assume every missing ping response is user-visible packet loss. ICMP may be deprioritised while normal traffic continues. Compare multiple destinations, test with application-specific diagnostics where available and repeat the measurement. Persistent loss across several targets is more concerning than occasional missing replies from one host.

Latency versus bandwidth: why a fast plan can still feel slow

Bandwidth describes the capacity to transfer data over time. Latency describes delay. Consider a large delivery truck and a small parcel: a high-capacity link can move a great deal of data once transfer is underway, but a request still needs time to travel to the server and for a response to come back.

For large downloads and high-resolution streaming, throughput is important. For remote desktop, gaming, voice calls and interactive browsing, responsiveness is also important. A household may need enough download and upload capacity to avoid congestion, but buying a faster plan does not necessarily shorten the physical route to a server.

Latency can rise when a link is saturated. Packets wait in queues at a modem, router, access node or provider network. This delay under load is often called bufferbloat. A connection may show low idle ping but a large increase when someone uploads a video or downloads a game. Test both idle and loaded latency to understand the difference.

More bandwidth can reduce queueing if the previous connection was frequently saturated, but it is not a universal fix. Queue management such as FQ-CoDel or CAKE, where supported and configured appropriately, can help control latency under load. A congested provider segment or distant server needs a different remedy.

How latency affects gaming, calls, streaming and browsing

Online gaming

Games exchange frequent updates about player position, actions and world state. Low and stable latency helps inputs feel responsive. Jitter can make updates arrive unevenly, while packet loss can cause missing state and visible corrections. The relevant server location matters: a low ping to a nearby speed-test server does not prove that the game server has the same route.

Voice and video calls

Conversation depends on timely delivery in both directions. High delay makes turn-taking awkward. Jitter can cause audio to stutter or force a larger playback buffer. Packet loss can produce gaps, robotic speech or frozen video. Upload performance matters because your device must send audio and video to the meeting service.

Remote desktop and cloud applications

Remote work tools often send small interactive commands and wait for screen updates. A high-latency route can make every click or keystroke feel delayed, even when the connection has plenty of download bandwidth. A nearby cloud region and stable route can matter more than a faster headline plan.

Web browsing

Modern pages may request resources from multiple domains. Each connection setup, DNS lookup, secure handshake and server response can add time. Browsers use caching, connection reuse, multiplexing and content delivery networks to reduce delays, but a high-latency route can still make a page feel slow before much data is transferred.

Streaming

Streaming services usually buffer media, so they can tolerate more latency than a live conversation or game. Throughput and sustained stability are often more important once playback begins. However, high latency can slow initial requests, and packet loss or congestion can reduce the available bitrate.

What causes high ping or unstable latency?

Physical distance and routing

A distant server usually requires more travel time. The route may cross multiple networks or take an indirect path because of provider policy, peering arrangements or outages. A route change can increase or decrease latency without any change to the home router.

Network congestion

When traffic demand approaches link capacity, packets queue. The queue can exist in the home router, modem, access network, mobile radio cell, provider aggregation network or a remote service. Congestion often appears as higher latency during busy hours or while another device is transferring data.

Wi-Fi contention and interference

Wi-Fi is a shared radio medium. Devices take turns transmitting, and nearby networks may compete for airtime. Interference, weak signal, retransmissions and crowded channels can introduce delay and variation. A strong signal does not guarantee an uncongested channel.

Router, modem or device limitations

Old firmware, overloaded hardware, thermal problems, low memory, poor queue management or a faulty power supply can affect forwarding performance. A device running backups, updates or security scans may also delay network traffic.

Access technology and radio conditions

Fibre, cable, DSL, fixed wireless and satellite have different access characteristics. Copper line length can affect DSL; local segment capacity can affect cable; radio conditions and cell load affect fixed wireless; satellite orbit and gateway routing affect satellite latency. The specific provider implementation matters.

Server-side problems

The service you are contacting may be overloaded, geographically distant or connected through a congested network. If only one game, website or application is slow while other destinations respond normally, investigate that service before changing your broadband plan.

How to measure ping and jitter correctly

  1. Establish a baseline. Pause large downloads, cloud backups and streaming. Use a modern device and record the time, connection type and test destination.
  2. Test your local router. Ping the router's local address. On many home networks this is shown as the default gateway in device network settings. Consistent local results help rule out some Wi-Fi or LAN problems.
  3. Test a nearby external destination. Use a reliable public endpoint or a network measurement service. Remember that the destination may prioritise or limit diagnostic traffic.
  4. Test the service you care about. Use the game's network display, meeting diagnostics or application telemetry if available. A generic ping is only a clue.
  5. Repeat over Ethernet. If possible, connect a computer directly to the router with a suitable cable. Compare with Wi-Fi in the same location.
  6. Measure under load. Run a download and an upload separately while observing latency. A large increase only during upload suggests upstream queueing; an increase during both may indicate another bottleneck.
  7. Repeat at different times. Compare morning, evening and the time the problem usually occurs. Record several samples instead of relying on a single test.
  8. Compare more than one destination. This helps distinguish a local access problem from a route or server-specific issue.

Command-line tools can provide additional detail. On many systems, ping sends repeated echo requests. traceroute or tracert shows the sequence of routing hops, although routers may not answer every probe. Tools such as MTR combine repeated probes and route information. Interpret these results carefully: intermediate routers may deprioritise diagnostic traffic while forwarding ordinary packets normally.

For a useful record, write down median ping, the range or variation, packet loss, whether the test was wired or wireless, whether the connection was idle or loaded, and which destination was tested. Avoid comparing two results that use different servers, devices or test methods as if they were identical.

A practical troubleshooting sequence

Step 1: Separate Wi-Fi from the broadband connection

Test close to the router and, if possible, over Ethernet. If the wired connection is stable but Wi-Fi is not, focus on wireless placement, channel conditions, mesh backhaul and device capability. If both are poor, continue toward the modem, ONT, access line or provider network.

Step 2: Check whether the problem happens only under load

Compare idle latency with latency while uploading and downloading. If delay rises sharply when the connection is busy, identify which device or application is saturating the link. Schedule backups and large uploads outside important calls, or configure sensible queue management if the router supports it.

Step 3: Reduce avoidable local traffic

Pause large downloads, cloud synchronisation and software updates during a call or competitive game. Check whether a device is repeatedly uploading camera footage or backing up photos. This is a diagnostic step, not a permanent requirement to keep the network idle.

Step 4: Update and inspect network equipment

Check for router and modem firmware updates from the manufacturer or provider. Inspect cables and connectors, ensure equipment has ventilation and confirm that router ports support the service speed. Avoid repeated factory resets unless the provider or manufacturer recommends them.

Step 5: Improve Wi-Fi conditions if tests point to wireless

Place the router in an open, central location; avoid enclosing it in a cabinet or placing it beside large metal objects. Use a less congested channel where the router allows it. For distant rooms, consider Ethernet to an access point rather than assuming a wireless repeater will solve every problem.

Step 6: Escalate with evidence

Give the ISP dates, times, wired test results, destinations, latency variation and packet-loss observations. Explain whether the issue occurs when idle, under load or only to one service. Ask the provider to check line errors, signal levels, local congestion, routing and modem event logs as appropriate.

Can changing DNS, a VPN or a broadband plan fix latency?

DNS affects how a domain name is resolved to an IP address. A faster resolver can make the initial lookup quicker, especially when the previous resolver was slow or unreliable. Once a connection is established, DNS usually does not determine the ongoing packet latency between your device and the service. Changing DNS is therefore not a general fix for high in-game ping.

A VPN changes the route your traffic takes. It can add encryption and an additional network hop, often increasing latency. In some cases, a VPN provider has a better route to a destination than the ISP's default path, so the result can improve. Test the same destination with and without the VPN, and do not assume a lower ping to the VPN gateway means a lower ping to the final application.

Changing broadband technology may help if the current access link is the bottleneck. Moving from a long DSL line to fibre can improve capacity and access latency. Replacing congested fixed wireless with a stable wired connection may also help. But if the delay comes from a distant server or poor interconnection, switching the home access technology may make little difference.

Before paying for an upgrade, identify the source of delay. A new router cannot fix a distant server. A faster download tier cannot remove the propagation delay to another continent. A new DNS resolver cannot resolve a congested upload queue. Match the remedy to the measured problem.

Understanding loaded latency and bufferbloat

Idle latency is measured when little else is using the connection. Loaded latency is measured while data is being transferred. The difference can be important because network queues build when traffic arrives faster than a link can send it. If a queue grows excessively, interactive packets wait behind bulk traffic.

This behaviour is often discussed as bufferbloat. It can make a fast connection feel unresponsive during large uploads or downloads. A video call may stutter while a device backs up files, or a game may feel delayed while another user downloads a large update.

Some routers use active queue management (AQM) to control queue delay. Algorithms such as FQ-CoDel and CAKE are designed to manage queues and share capacity more fairly among traffic flows. They must be supported by the router and configured with realistic rates. Setting a shaper above the true bottleneck rate may not control the queue where congestion actually occurs.

For more detail, read the TEZZ CONNECT guide to bufferbloat and latency under load. If loaded latency is the main problem, that guide can help distinguish local queueing from other causes.

What good latency looks like—and why context matters

People often ask for a single good ping number. A nearby server returning a round-trip time below 20 milliseconds is generally responsive for many everyday uses, while 20–50 ms can also be comfortable for browsing, calls and many games. Higher values may remain perfectly usable for downloads and streaming. These are broad practical guides, not service guarantees or universal pass/fail boundaries.

For a competitive game, the server region and game mechanics influence what feels acceptable. For a video call, stable timing and low loss may matter more than a few milliseconds of average delay. For satellite access, a higher baseline may be expected because of the physical link. A fair assessment compares a service with the alternatives actually available at the same location.

Consistency matters. A steady 45 ms route can feel better than a route that alternates between 18 ms and 180 ms. A low average with repeated packet loss can be worse than a slightly higher but stable result. Look at the whole pattern and the experience of the application.

The bottom line

Latency is delay, ping is a common way to measure round-trip response time, jitter is variation in packet delay, and packet loss is missing data. They are related but not interchangeable. Download speed measures throughput, not responsiveness.

To diagnose a slow-feeling connection, compare wired and wireless results, measure idle and loaded latency, test multiple destinations and repeat at different times. If delay rises only when the connection is busy, investigate queueing and upload saturation. If only one service is affected, examine its route and server. If the local router test is unstable, begin with Wi-Fi and home equipment.

Use measurements to locate the problem before buying a faster plan or new hardware. The most useful connection is not simply the one with the highest advertised download speed. It is the one that delivers enough capacity, low and predictable delay, minimal loss and reliable performance for the applications that matter.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good ping for internet gaming?

There is no single threshold for every game or route. A consistently low round-trip time to the game server is generally preferable; under about 30 ms is often considered very responsive, while 30–60 ms can still feel good for many games. Server distance, jitter, packet loss and game design matter too.

Is ping the same as latency?

People often use the words interchangeably. Ping is a diagnostic tool and also the round-trip time it reports. Latency is the broader delay experienced as data travels through a network path; it can be measured one-way or round-trip.

What is jitter in simple terms?

Jitter is variation in packet delay. If packets arrive at uneven intervals, a voice or video application may have to buffer, conceal missing audio or show freezes even when the average ping looks acceptable.

Can high download speed still have high latency?

Yes. Throughput and latency measure different things. A connection can transfer large files quickly but take a long time to begin responding, particularly when queues are congested or the destination is far away.

Why does ping increase when someone uploads a file?

The upload can fill a queue in the modem, router or provider network. New interactive packets wait behind queued data, increasing round-trip time. This is one form of bufferbloat; queue management can reduce it.

Does Wi-Fi increase ping?

Wi-Fi can add delay and variation due to contention, interference, retransmissions, weak signal and power-saving behaviour. A wired Ethernet comparison helps determine whether the local wireless link contributes to the problem.

Can a VPN reduce latency?

Usually a VPN adds an extra routing or processing step, but it can occasionally improve a poor route or avoid a congested path. Compare the same destination with and without the VPN; results depend on server location, peering and provider quality.

Why is ping to a website different from ping in a game?

The destinations, routes, protocols and measurement methods differ. Some servers deprioritise or block ICMP echo requests, while games use their own server endpoints and timing methods. A website ping is not a reliable substitute for in-game latency.

What causes jitter and packet loss?

Common causes include wireless interference, congestion, overloaded queues, faulty cables, failing network equipment, weak radio links, routing problems and server-side load. A pattern of tests across wired and wireless connections helps isolate the source.

How can I test latency properly?

Measure idle latency and latency while downloading and uploading, repeat at different times, compare Ethernet with Wi-Fi, and test more than one destination. Record median and variation rather than relying on one reading.
